Tensor Networks Meet Neural Networks:
A Survey and Future Perspectives
Maolin Wang, Yu Pan, Zenglin Xu*, Senior Member, IEEE, Guangxi Li, Xiangli Yang,
Danilo Mandic, Fellow, IEEE, and Andrzej Cichocki, Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—Tensor networks (TNs) and neural networks (NNs) provide complementary approaches to high-dimensional learning. TNs
represent high-order arrays as contractions of smaller cores and can reduce otherwise prohibitive storage and computation when suitable
low-rank structure is present. NNs, by contrast, learn flexible nonlinear representations through architectural inductive biases and
data-driven optimization. Their connection does not arise from multilinearity alone; rather, TNs provide a principled framework for
analyzing and restructuring the multilinear components that occur within neural architectures. We refer to neural models that incorporate
such tensor-network structure as tensorial neural networks (TNNs). This survey examines TNNs from both data-processing and
model-architecture perspectives. The data perspective covers multi-source fusion, multimodal pooling, tensor compression, multi-task
learning, and quantum-state data representation. The model perspective considers convolutional and recurrent networks, Transformers,
graph neural networks, large language and mixture-of-experts models, and quantum neural networks. We further review training and
rank-selection strategies, contraction and hardware considerations, interpretability, and software support. The central contribution is a
cross-architectural synthesis of how tensorization, rank, topology, contraction cost, optimization, inductive bias, and deployment
constraints interact across model families. This synthesis identifies when tensorization functions as compression, structured parameter
sharing, or a task-aligned inductive bias, and it motivates concrete directions for reproducible evaluation and future research. We provide
a curated list of TNNs at https://github.com/tnbar/awesome-tensorial-neural-networks.
Index Terms—Tensor Networks, Deep Neural Networks, Network Compression, Information Fusion, Quantum Circuit Simulation

Tensor Networks (TNs). TNs [8], [11], [12] represent a
high-order tensor through contractions of smaller component
tensors, also called blocks, factors, or cores. When the target
admits an appropriate low-rank or low-entanglement structure, this
representation can replace exponential storage in the tensor order
with a number of parameters that scales polynomially in the mode
sizes and bond dimensions. The benefit is therefore conditional on
the tensorization, topology, and ranks rather than an unconditional
resolution of the curse of dimensionality. TNs also distribute
computation across their cores, enabling structured storage and
processing of tensors that would otherwise be prohibitively large.

Neural Networks (NNs). NNs are powerful learning struc-
tures that enable machines to acquire knowledge from observed
data [98], [99]. Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) [100], [101] have
revolutionized artificial intelligence by capturing complex patterns
from hierarchical data. Representative architectural paradigms
include convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural
networks (RNNs), and Transformers [100], [102], [103]. Their
impact spans computer vision, language, and scientific discovery,
including large-scale vision models and AlphaFold2 [104], [105].
The field of natural language processing has witnessed a paradigm
shift with the emergence of large language models (LLMs) built
upon Transformer architectures [102]. Their scale and deployment
constraints have made parameter efficiency, compression, and
hardware-aware inference central design concerns. The impact
of deep learning continues to expand across numerous scientific
and practical domains, underscoring why parameter efficiency and
structured model design have become increasingly important.
Tensor Networks Meet Neural Networks. Tensor Networks
(TNs) and Neural Networks (NNs), while stemming from distinct
scientific foundations, have each demonstrated unique capabilities
across diverse domains. Cichocki et al. [11] and Mondelli and
Montanari [106] establish connections between multilinear tensor
structure and neural computation. These connections motivate
tensorial neural networks (TNNs): neural architectures whose
parameters, activations, or data-processing modules are explicitly
structured through tensor networks. We argue that integrating TNs
with NNs can enhance model performance and sustainability from
both data and model perspectives:
(1) Effective Data Representation: Accurate modeling of
higher-order interactions from multi-source data is critical in
advancing performance and promoting responsible AI practices [8].
Integrating TNs within NN frameworks offers a principled way
to capture such interactions without materializing the full high-
order tensor, which is particularly useful in multimodal, multiview,
and multitask settings [27], [29], [33]. Tensorized representations
are likewise useful for compression-oriented data models such as
TensorCodec [36].
(2) Compact Model Structures: TNs also offer a sustainable
route to model compression by replacing dense weight tensors with
structured low-rank factors [49], [54], [55]. This can dramatically
reduce parameters and memory traffic while preserving useful
architectural priors. Representative examples include tensorized
convolutional and recurrent layers as well as TR-LSTM-style
recurrent compression [52], [54], [107].
We refer to this family of approaches that connect TNs with
NNs as Tensorial Neural Networks (TNNs). Although this com-
bination holds significant promise for sustainable AI by offering
efficient parameter compression and structured representations,
TNNs also present new training challenges that require careful
consideration. These challenges include numerical stability issues
during optimization, particularly for high-order tensor operations
and decompositions, complex hyperparameter selection especially
for determining optimal tensor ranks and network architectures,

and hardware acceleration requirements to efficiently handle tensor
contractions and parallel computations. Therefore, it is necessary to
redesign traditional neural network training techniques to address

Memmel et al. [108] position tensor networks as a method-
ological asset for Green AI and argue that tensorized models
should be assessed not only by accuracy but also by efficiency,
inclusivity, and deployment-aware metrics. While several excellent
surveys and book chapters have explored tensor decompositions in
deep learning or quantum tensor networks separately, our survey
focuses on their intersection. We investigate how tensor structures
are adapted into neural architectures, what trade-offs recur across
architectures, and which lessons follow from the literature on model
design and deployment.
More specifically, Biamonte and Bergholm [8] emphasize
quantum-TN foundations, graphical notation, contraction, and
entanglement. Panagakis et al. [76] survey tensor methods for rep-
resentation learning, computer vision, and deep learning, whereas
their later book chapter [109] provides a broader methodological
account that also includes quantum-network material. Our survey
asks a different organizing question: how do tensor-network
structures become trainable neural components across CNNs,
RNNs, Transformers, GNNs, LLMs, and QNNs, and which
design principles recur across these families? Accordingly, our
contribution is not a claim that the individual decompositions
or applications have not been reviewed, but a cross-architectural
synthesis that connects tensorization and topology choices with
contraction cost, optimization, software support, inductive bias,
and deployment constraints.
What do we learn from this review? Beyond cataloguing
applications, the surveyed literature supports four recurring mes-
sages. First, decomposition design is not only about choosing
ranks: mode permutations, tensorization shapes, and topology
choices can materially change the compression–accuracy fron-
tier [110], [111]. Second, the central architectural trade-off is often
expressiveness versus contractibility: richer loopy structures can
represent more complex correlations, but they require substantially
better approximate contraction algorithms to remain practical [112].
Third, modern TNN progress is increasingly algorithmic rather than
merely applicational, spanning structure search, sampling-based
decomposition, and uncertainty-aware tensor modeling [113], [114].
Fourth, tensorization should be viewed not only as compression,
but also as an inductive bias that can improve robustness and gener-
alization when aligned with the underlying problem structure [115].
An overview of both their data processing capabilities and
model architectures of TNNs is shown in Table 1. From the
data processing perspective, TNNs demonstrate versatility across
multiple domains including: multi-source fusion for integrating
heterogeneous data sources, multimodal pooling for efficient feature
combination, data compression for reducing storage requirements
while preserving information fidelity, multi-task training for
simultaneous learning of related objectives, and quantum data
processing for handling quantum state representations. From the
model architecture perspective, TNNs have been successfully
integrated into various deep learning frameworks including CNNs,
RNNs, Transformers, GNNs, Large Language Models (LLMs),
and Quantum Neural Networks (QNNs), each offering unique
advantages in their respective application domains.

TENSOR BASIS
2.1
Tensor Notations
A tensor [116], [117], also known as a multiway array, can be
viewed as a higher-order extension of a vector (i.e., a first-order
tensor) or a matrix (i.e., a second-order tensor). Like the rows
and columns in a matrix, an Nth-order tensor X ∈RI1×I2···×IN
has N modes (i.e., ways, orders, or indices) whose lengths (i.e.,
dimensions) are represented by I1 to IN, respectively. As shown
in Table 3, lowercase letters denote scalars, e.g., a, boldface
lowercase letters denote vectors, e.g., a, boldface capital letters
denote matrices, e.g., A and boldface Euler script letters denote
higher-order tensors, e.g., A. In this paper, we define a “tensor” in
a broad sense that includes scalars, vectors, and matrices.
2.2
Tensor Diagrams
In this subsection, we introduce TN diagrams and their correspond-
ing mathematical operations. TN diagrams were first developed by
Roger Penrose [118] in the early 1970s and are now commonly used
to describe quantum algorithms [8], [119] and machine learning
algorithms [12], [43], [107]. Within these diagrams, tensors are
denoted graphically by nodes with edges [12], which enables
intuitive presentation and convenient representation of complex
tensors. As both the data and weights in the deep learning field
are tensors, tensor diagrams are also promising for use as general
network analysis tools in this area. An overview of the basic
symbols of tensors is shown in Fig. 1.

2.2.6
Tensor Unfolding
Tensor unfolding is an operation that virtually flattens a tensor
into a high-dimensional but low-order tensor. Matricization is
a special case of tensor unfolding. To be more specific, given
an Nth-order tensor A ∈RI1×I2···×IN , its mode-n unfolding
process yields a matrix A(n) ∈RIn×I1I2···In−1In+1...IN . Such an
operation can also be regarded as performing tensor contraction
with a specifically designed tensor. A fourth-order tensor unfolding
diagram is illustrated in Fig. 1.
2.3
Tensor Decomposition Formats
The commonly used terminology “tensor decomposition” (TD)
is equivalent to “tensor network” to some extent. While TD was
employed primarily in signal processing fields [121], [122], TNs
were originally utilized largely in the physics and quantum circuit
fields [8], [118]. Traditional TD models, such as CP [13], [123],
[124] and Tucker decomposition [14], [125], can be viewed as
basic kinds of TNs. In the realm of signal processing, several
powerful TNs architectures for quantum analysis have also been
introduced. For instance, the MPS decomposition [126] was defined
as a TT decomposition [16] and had tremendous success in several
applications [12]. After years of collaboration and progress across
different research fields, there is no significant distinction between
these two terminologies. Therefore, TD and TNs are treated in a
unified way in this paper. We briefly introduce some basic TDs by
employing TN diagrams. Hong et al. [127], Ghadiri et al. [110],
and Li et al. [111] show that the design space of a decomposition is
richer than its format label: the loss function, core shape, and mode
permutation can all substantially affect practical performance.

When calculating a CP format, the first issue that arises is how
to determine the number of rank-1 tensor components, i.e., the
CP rank R. Actually, this is an NP-hard problem [128]. Hence,
in practice, a numerical value is usually assumed in advance (i.e.,
as a hyperparameter), to fit various CP-based models [121]. After
that, the diagonal core tensor G and the factor matrices A(n)
can be directly solved by employing algorithmic iteration, which
usually involves the alternating least-squares (ALS) method that
was originally proposed in [13], [123]. Hong et al. [127] extend CP
factorization beyond the standard least-squares setting to broader
loss functions and data types, which is especially relevant for
non-Gaussian observations and task-specific objectives.

2.3.3
Block-Term Decompositions
Block-term formats interpolate between CP and Tucker by rep-
resenting a tensor as a sum of low-multilinear-rank Tucker
blocks [15], [129]. In the BTT construction shown in Fig. 2 (c), RC
controls the number of blocks and RT controls their shared Tucker

a single block recovers Tucker. General BTD allows each block
to have its own multilinear-rank profile. Supplementary Note S1
gives the detailed BTT and BTD formulations.

whose cost grows linearly with tensor order for fixed mode sizes
and ranks [119]. Bharadwaj et al. [113] use leverage-score-based
sampling to reduce the cost of constructing TT approximations
while preserving high-quality subspace information. For tensorized
linear operators, MPO pairs local input and output modes in fourth-
order cores G(n) ∈RRn−1×In×Jn×Rn [53], [131], [132], [133].
Its full expression is moved to Supplementary Note S2.
2.3.5
Tensor Ring Decomposition
TR closes the two boundary bonds of a TT to form the cycle shown
in Fig. 2 (e) [18], [134], [135]. Its cores retain the shape G(n) ∈
RRn−1×In×Rn, but the cyclic condition R0 = RN removes the
distinguished rank-one endpoints. This symmetry provides flexible
mode ordering while making rank selection, gauge handling, and
optimization more involved than in an open chain. Supplementary
Note S3 records the cyclic contraction formula. This additional
flexibility makes rank and latent-space design especially important
in TR models. Yuan et al. [136] minimize rank in the latent space
to obtain compact and stable TR representations for completion
and learning problems.
2.3.6
Hierarchical Tucker Decomposition
HT organizes tensor modes with a dimension tree rather than a
chain [19]. Leaf frames represent individual modes and internal
transfer tensors combine child subsets recursively, producing the
structure in Fig. 2 (f). For a balanced tree with uniform mode
dimension d and rank bounded by R, its storage is commonly
written as O(NdR + NR3), compared with O(NdR2) for TT.
HT can therefore exploit meaningful hierarchical mode groupings,
but its performance and cost depend on the selected dimension tree.
The recursive formulation is provided in Supplementary Note S4.
Falco et al. [137] study tensors of fixed or bounded tree-based
rank as manifold-structured subsets of ambient tensor spaces. This
geometric view connects Tucker, HT, and TT representations and
clarifies why rank-constrained optimization has exploitable local
structure. Because the cited work remains a preprint, we use it as
mathematical background rather than as empirical evidence.
2.3.7
PEPS Decomposition
PEPS arranges local cores on a two-dimensional grid [8], [20],
[40], [138]. Each core contains one physical mode and up to
four bond modes connecting its left, right, upper, and lower
neighbors, as illustrated in Fig. 2 (g). The loopy lattice can
encode multidimensional dependencies beyond a chain or tree,
but exact contraction is #P-hard in general and practical use relies
on controlled approximations. Supplementary Note S5 gives the
grid-indexed reconstruction formula. Most current TNNs employ
low-treewidth structures, including TT/MPS chains, TR cycles,
and HT trees, because their contractions remain tractable. PEPS
provides additional correlation pathways through a loopy lattice,
but topology and contraction algorithm must be designed together.
Alkabetz and Arad [139] analyze belief propagation as a tensor-
network contraction procedure, while Tindall et al. [140] demon-
strate message-passing-based simulation on loopy lattice structures.
These results motivate approximate-contraction methods whose
numerical error and task-level effect are reported explicitly [141].
To reduce preliminary material in the main article, the detailed
formulations of BTT/BTD, MPO, TR, HT, and PEPS are moved to
the supplementary material. The tensor-network diagrams in Fig. 2
and the concise format descriptions above retain the structural
information needed for the subsequent TNN sections without
introducing another comparison table. The supplementary material
records the omitted formulas, while the remainder of the survey
uses the common topological, rank, and contraction properties
summarized in the text.
3
SUSTAINABLE AI THROUGH TNN IN DATA
ASPECT: EFFECTIVE DATA REPRESENTATION
In real-world data analysis, information often comes from multiple
sources, such as vision, sound, and text in video data [142], [143].
A prime example is the Visual Question Answering (VQA) task,
where the key challenge lies in effectively modeling interactions
between textual and visual information. Processing such diverse
data sources uniformly is impractical, necessitating specialized
architectures with multiple input channels to handle multimodal
sources - an approach known as information fusion. While tradi-
tional feature-level and decision-level fusion pipelines were popular
in early stages [144], these linear approaches failed to effectively
model intramodality dynamics. Tensor Neural Networks (TNNs)
have emerged as a solution, leveraging their natural multilinear
properties to model intramodality dynamics and process higher-
order data. TNNs provide effective frameworks for tensor opera-
tions, making them naturally suited for expressing and generalizing
information fusion modules commonly found in deep learning,
such as attention mechanisms and vector concatenation [145]. As
a result, numerous studies have adopted TNNs to capture higher-
order interactions among data or parameters. In the following
sections, we will explore various TNN-based approaches for data
representation and processing. First, we examine advanced data
compression techniques that leverage tensor network architectures
to achieve significant parameter reduction while preserving critical
information structures. We then investigate novel tensor fusion
layers (Section 3.1) designed to facilitate deep feature interactions
and transformations across modalities, followed by sophisticated
multimodal data pooling mechanisms (Section 3.2) that effectively
integrate information across different data types.

of multiplicative interactions in deep learning, see Jayakumar et

and acoustic modality [27]. Addressing multimodal sentiment
analysis, Zadeh et al. [27] proposed novel TNNs with deep
information fusion layers named tensor fusion layers (TFLs),

3.3
Multi-way Data Compression
TNNs present a powerful framework for addressing the unique
challenges in multi-dimensional data compression. Unlike tradi-
tional compression methods that treat data as vectors or matrices, or
conventional tensor methods that rely on fixed decomposition struc-
tures, TNNs leverage learnable neural architectures to adaptively
preserve and exploit the natural multi-dimensional relationships
in the data, leading to more efficient and accurate representations
with theoretical guarantees.
The BNTD [35] first introduces TNNs and advances multi-way
data compression through a principled probabilistic framework,
effectively modelling complex entity-relation interactions and
incorporating prior information via neural tensor architectures.
Wang et al. [154] propose federated latent embedding sharing and
tensor factorization (FLEST), which combines tensor factoriza-
tion with embedding-matrix decomposition for communication-
efficient federated knowledge-graph completion without directly
sharing local graph data. The TTHRESH method [38] leverages
HOSVD decomposition combined with bit-plane, run-length and
arithmetic coding to efficiently compress high-dimensional grid-
ded data for visualization, achieving smooth quality degradation
and enabling low-cost compressed-domain manipulations while
providing competitive compression ratios at low-to-medium bit
rates. Fan et al. [155] proposed a multi-mode deep matrix and
tensor factorization approach (M2DMTF) that employs TKD with
factor matrices generated using multilayer perceptrons, effectively
handling complex tensor data with missing values and noise. Lee
and Shin [156] developed a robust factorization method specifically
designed for real-world tensor streams containing patterns, missing
values, and outliers. Lamba et al. [157] introduced a method for
incorporating side information into tensor factorization, improving
the quality of compression and representation learning.
NeuKron [37] extends tensor neural networks by introducing
auto-regressive neural networks to generalize Kronecker products,
enabling constant-size lossy compression of sparse reorderable
matrices and tensors. TensorCodec [36], [158] extends tensor
neural networks for efficient data compression by introducing
neural tensor-train decomposition, tensor folding, and mode-index
reordering techniques, enabling accurate compression without
strong data assumptions. Light-IT and Light-IT++ [158] extend
tensor neural networks for efficient data compression by introducing
PROCEEDINGS OF THE IEEE (ACCEPTED VERSION)
9
vocabulary-based compression and core tensor operations, enabling
compact and accurate representation of irregular tensors. Loeschcke
et al. [159] construct coarse-to-fine tensor trains that can be
progressively refined for image fitting, denoising, inpainting, and
novel-view synthesis. Their results show that TT parameterizations
support flexible continuous visual modeling and storage reduction.
3.4
Multi-task Data Training
For multitask learning applications, WISDOM [160] pioneered
an incremental learning algorithm that performs supervised tensor
decomposition on spatio-temporal data encoded as third-order
tensors, simultaneously training spatial and temporal prediction
models from extracted latent factors while incorporating domain
knowledge, demonstrating superior performance over baseline
algorithms in global-scale climate data prediction across multiple
locations. Yang et al. [39] then proposed the Tensor Train multitask
(TTMT) and Tucker multitask (TMT) models using TT and Tucker
formats, respectively, to alleviate the negative transfer problem in
a hard sharing architecture and reduce the parameter volume in
a soft structure. The M2TD method [161] stitches patterns from
partitioned parameter subspaces of large simulation ensembles
to efficiently discover underlying dynamics and interrelationships
while maximizing accuracy under limited simulation budgets.
Zhang et al. [162] proposed a tensor network-based multi-task
model that decomposes person Re-ID into camera-specific classifi-
cation tasks and leverages low-rank tensor decomposition to capture
cross-camera correlations while aligning feature distributions
across different views. The SMART method [163] decomposes
spatio-temporal data into interpretable latent factors and trains
an ensemble of spatial-temporal predictors while incorporating
domain constraints to handle large-scale spatio-temporal prediction
tasks efficiently. A PEPS-like concatenated TN layer [40] for
multitask missions was also proposed, which, unlike the TTMT
and TMT models that suffer from the negative transfer problem
due to their hard sharing architectures, only contains a soft sharing
layer, thereby achieving better performance.
The MTCN method [42] achieves superior face multi-attribute
prediction by sharing all features in lower layers while differen-
tiating attribute features in higher layers, by incorporating tensor
canonical correlation analysis to exploit inter-attribute relationships.
The CTNN method [164] combines depthwise separable CNN
and low-rank tensor networks to efficiently extract both local
and global features from multi-task brainprint data, achieving
high recognition accuracy with limited training samples while
providing interpretable channel-specific biomarkers. The GTTN
method [41] combines matrix trace norms from all possible
tensor flattenings to automatically discover comprehensive low-
rank structures in deep multi-task learning models, eliminating the
need for manual specification of component importance. Zhang et
al. [165] propose a tensor-based multi-task learning framework that
leverages spatio-temporal similarities between brain biomarkers
to predict Alzheimer’s disease progression by encoding MRI
morphological changes into a third-order tensor and extracting
shared latent factors through tensor decomposition.
Ren et al. [166] extend PARAFAC2 with multi-task supervision
for EHR mining, improving phenotype extraction and prediction
through jointly modeled static and dynamic tasks. Wang et al. [167]
combine Tucker-based fusion with self-supervised multi-task
learning and a dual multimodal–unimodal training mechanism. Liu
et al. [168] map speech-quality features into a higher-dimensional
tensor-network representation and jointly optimize regression,
classification, and correlation objectives.

and fully connected layers through a variety of TD formats [50],

Pham et al. [176] exploit shared low-rank structure across
related network parameter tensors through coupled tensor decom-
position, rather than compressing each weight tensor independently.
Yang et al. [177] treat connectivity topology as an algebraic design
object and recursively generate architectures that interpolate be-
tween sparse and dense aggregation regimes. For scientific machine
learning, Zhou et al. [178] apply Tucker decomposition to a high-
dimensional Fourier neural operator, yielding a sequence of lower-
dimensional operators while retaining a universal-approximation
guarantee. Together, these studies broaden the compact-model
perspective from factorizing conventional weights to designing
structured connectivity and efficient operator models within a
common architectural framework.
In this section, we examine how TNNs enable more sustainable
AI through compact model structures. Modern DNNs, while
powerful, often require extensive computational resources and
memory due to their deep architectures and large-scale matrix
operations. Additionally, studies have shown significant weight
redundancy in DNNs, suggesting opportunities for compression
without sacrificing performance. Here, we explore five key TNN
architectures that address these challenges: TCNNs (Section 4.1),
TRNNs (Section 4.2), tensorial Transformers (Section 4.3), TGNNs
(Section 4.4), and tensorial quantum neural networks (Section 4.5).
By leveraging tensor decomposition techniques and efficient
parameter sharing, these approaches achieve significant model
compression while maintaining or even enhancing performance
compared to their conventional counterparts. We also examine
applications of tensor networks in large language models (Sec-
tion 4.6), where they enable parameter-efficient fine-tuning.
4.1
TCNNs
CNNs have achieved strong performance across a broad range of
tasks. However, their large parameter counts cause weight redun-
dancy and superfluous computations, affecting both performance
and efficiency. TD methods provide one approach to this problem,
and CNNs represented with tensor formats are called TCNNs. Prior
to introducing TCNNs, we first present a formulation of a vanilla
CNN, shown in Fig. 7 (a), in a standard tensorial form as
Y = X ⊛C + b,
(22)
where C ∈RK×K×I×O denotes a convolutional weight, X ∈
RI×H×W denotes an input, Y ∈RO×H′×W ′ denotes an output,
b ∈RO represents a bias, and ⊛denotes a convolutional
operator. K represents the kernel window size, I is an input
channel, H and W denote the height and width of X, O is an
output channel, and H′ and W ′ denote the height and width
of Y, respectively. TCNNs mainly focus on decomposing the
channels I and O. In detail, the weight C is first reshaped to
˜C ∈RK×K×I1×I2×...IM×O1×O2×...ON , where QM

To accelerate the CNN training and inference process, CP-
CNN [48], [107], [179], [180], [181] is constructed by decomposing
the convolutional weight into the CP format, as shown in Fig. 7 (c).
CP-CNN contains vector factors as its principal subcomponents and
can therefore achieve a high compression ratio when a sufficiently
small CP rank is adequate. As with CP-CNN, additional TCNNs
can be constructed by applying the tensor formats in Fig. 2 to
convolutional weights. Tucker decomposition is widely used for
Tucker-CNNs [49], [182]. A BTT-CNN instead uses a hyperedge
indexed by Rc to represent a sum of Tucker blocks [50]. This block-
term structure provides greater flexibility than a single Tucker
block, but introduces additional rank choices and optimization
cost. Highly compact TT formats have also been introduced to
CNNs to implement TT-CNNs [51], [183]. Relative to TT, the
cyclic topology of TR removes the fixed rank-one endpoints and
can provide a more flexible parameterization at comparable rank
settings [52]; whether this improves compression or accuracy
depends on the selected tensorization and ranks. To address
degeneracy in tensorial layers, CPD-EPC [180] uses a minimal-
sensitivity design for CP convolutional layers and hybrid Tucker2-
CP convolutional layers. The TR-Compress method [184] combines
tensor-ring factorization with execution scheduling to reduce
parameters while controlling inference overhead. There are also
some tensorial convolutional neural networks that decompose
more than just the convolution cores. The tensorized network
(T-Net) [185] treats the whole network as a one-layer architecture
and then decomposes it. As a result, the T-Net achieves better
results with a lighter structure. The CP-higher-order convolution
(CP-HOConv) [186] utilizes the CP format to efficiently model
higher-order data, such as spatial, temporal, and channel-wise
correlations, for tasks like spatiotemporal emotion estimation.
Obukhov et al. [187] model a set of neural tensors with shared
tensor-ring-style basis cores, showing that parameter sharing across
multiple tensors can itself provide effective compression under
resource constraints. Hamreras et al. [188] provide a comprehensive
analysis of tensorization as a powerful but underexplored tool for
compression and interpretability of neural networks, highlighting its
potential for advancing mechanistic interpretability and proposing
the vision of fully tensorized neural networks for future efficient
and transparent model design.
To provide a quantitative example that does not depend on a par-

Tensor Recurrent Neural Networks
RNNs, such as the vanilla RNN and LSTM, have achieved
promising performance on sequential data. However, when dealing
with high-dimensional input data (e.g., video and text data), the
input-to-hidden and hidden-to-hidden transformations in RNNs
will result in high memory usage rates and computational costs. To
solve this problem, low-rank TD is efficient for compressing the
transformation process in practice. First, we formulate an RNN as

b ∈RO is a bias, while ϕ(·) indicates a series of operations that
form RNN variants, including the vanilla RNN and LSTM [189].

widely used in TD, given by

The most direct compression strategy is to decompose the input-

Tucker parameterizations, respectively. At a sufficiently small rank,
CP-RNN can be among the most compact alternatives, although
its stronger rank-one structure may also reduce approximation
accuracy. The TT-RNN [53] implements the TT format on an RNN
to obtain a high parameter compression ratio. However, the TT-
RNN suffers from a linear structure with two smaller endpoints,
which hinders the representation ability and flexibility of TT-based
models. To release the power of a linear architecture, TRs were
proposed to link the endpoints to create a ring format [18]. The
TR-RNN [54] uses this ring structure to obtain a more compact
recurrent model. The BTT-RNN [50], [55] was constructed on
the generalized TD approach, the BTT decomposition [190]. The
BTT-RNN can automatically learn interparameter correlations to
implicitly prune redundant dense connections and simultaneously
achieve better performance. Lizaire et al. [191] interpret second-
order RNNs through tensors, clarifying when multiplicative recur-
rent interactions are useful and how they relate to classical TN
parameterizations rather than treating decomposition only as a
post-hoc compression tool.
Moreover, studies are utilizing TD to compress an RNN’s
two transformation layers, and some have even developed de-
composition methods that are suitable for both RNNs and CNNs.
The TT-GRU [192] and the HT-RNN [56] methods decompose
[W, U] to attain a higher compression ratio. Specifically, TT-
GRU [192] applies a TT for decomposition, and the HT-RNN [56]
adopts HT decomposition. Unlike prior works that decompose
hidden matrices, Conv-TT-LSTM [57] utilizes the idea of a TT to
represent convolutional operations. As shown in Fig. 8, through
a TT-like convolution, Conv-TT-LSTM can replace convolutional
LSTM with fewer parameters while achieving good results on
action benchmarks. For the adaptation of both CNNs and RNNs,
a hybrid TD (termed HT-TT) method that combines HT and TT
decomposition [193] was adopted to compress both the CNN
and RNN [W, U] matrices. The MPS-NLP [194] proposes tensor
recurrent neural networks (TRNNs) through matrix product states
and entanglement entropy, enabling explainable natural language
processing while maintaining model performance. In addition, the
tensor contraction layer (TC-Layer) [195] was designed to replace
the fully connected layer and therefore can be utilized as the last
layer of a CNN and the hidden layers in RNNs. Interestingly, TC-
Layer can be viewed as a special case of a TT-based layer obtained

parameters of these matrices. For instance, the MPO structure
was proposed to decompose each matrix in a Transformer [58],
generating central tensors (containing the core information) and
small auxiliary tensors. A tuning strategy was further adopted to
continue training the auxiliary tensors to achieve a performance
improvement while freezing the weight of the central tensor to
retain the main information of the original matrix. Moreover,
observing that a low-rank MPO structure can cause a severe
performance drop, Hypoformer [133] was proposed based on
hybrid TT decomposition; this approach concatenates a dense
matrix part with a low-rank MPO part. Hypoformer retains the full-
rank property while reducing the required numbers of operations
and parameters to compress and accelerate the base Transformer.
In addition, by concatenating all matrices into one larger tensor,
Tucker-Bert [59] decomposes the concatenated tensor with Tucker
decomposition to greatly reduce the number of parameters, leading
to extreme compression and maintaining comparably good results.
Xie et al. [198] extend tensorized compression to parameter
transfer across model scales by learning a shared self-supervised
weight template and size-specific lightweight scalers under Tucker
factorization. Their formulation initializes vision models with differ-
ent depths and widths across classification, detection, segmentation,
and generation tasks, indicating that tensorization can serve as a
reusable cross-scale parameter prior rather than only as a post-hoc
compression operator. Compared to compressing the original at-
tention operation, multiway multimodal transformer (MMT) [199]
explores a novel generalized tensorial attention operation to model
modality-aware multiway correlations for multimodal datasets.
Interestingly, Tuformer [60] generalizes MHSA into the Tucker
form, thus containing more expressive power and achieving better
results, as shown in Fig. 9. Advances in tensorial causal learning
have emerged through the implementation of causal capsules and
tucker-format tensor transformers for latent variable interaction
control. Klein et al. [61] interpret MHA, GQA, and MLA within a
unified Tucker-style low-rank view of approximate attention. Their
analysis connects classical tensor decomposition with attention
design, KV-cache reduction, and parameter-efficient Transformer
deployment. Zhang et al. [62] use tensor-product parameterization
to compress the KV cache for long-context language modeling
while retaining performance against MHA, MQA, GQA, and MLA.

4.6
Tensor Networks in Large Language Models
While LLMs share the same core tensor operations as the ten-
sorized Transformers discussed in Section 4.3, we keep a separate
subsection for three reasons: (i) scale, because LLMs operate at
parameter counts that make memory-aware tensorization a first-
order concern; (ii) deployment, because inference-time issues such
as KV-cache growth and multi-token prediction are especially
prominent; and (iii) adaptation, because tensorized parameter-
efficient fine-tuning has become a distinct sub-literature. Because
a substantial fraction of this literature remains in preprint form,
we summarize representative directions rather than attempting an
exhaustive catalogue.
Xu et al. [208] tensorize token embeddings for model com-
pression, while Yang et al. [209] use tensor-train adaptation
for parameter-efficient fine-tuning. At inference time, KV-cache
reduction and dynamic tensorization are especially relevant because
they target one of the dominant memory bottlenecks in LLM
deployment. More broadly, tensorized PEFT methods suggest that
structured adapters can preserve more multilinear structure than
matrix-only LoRA variants. Structured matrix parameterizations
such as Monarch Mixer [210] are also relevant here because
they reveal a broader continuum between tensor decompositions,
Kronecker/butterfly factorizations, and hardware-efficient sequence
models. Qiu et al. [211] reinforce this connection by showing that
BTT-style structured matrices can improve the compute–accuracy
trade-off in vision transformers and small GPT-style models.
Ye et al. [71] share or freeze selected TT cores across layers
and use lightweight controllers to preserve layer-specific flexibility.
Zheng et al. [75] avoid repeated reconstruction of tensorized
weights during adaptation to improve the accuracy–memory trade-
off, whereas Li et al. [73] combine a shared orthogonal basis with
an importance-pruned sparse core to alleviate the storage bottleneck
of dense Tucker-style cores. These studies show that tensorized
adaptation depends not only on the decomposition format, but also
on cross-layer sharing, reconstruction cost, and core sparsity.
Mixture-of-experts models provide another important setting.
Xu et al. [72] jointly tensorize all experts within a layer so that
a shared three-dimensional factorization captures cross-expert
redundancy. In a complementary formulation, Xu et al. [74]
represent lifelong multimodal knowledge edits as compact CP-
decomposed experts and use the input factors for intrinsic routing.
These results connect tensor decomposition with expert sharing,
routing, continual adaptation, and memory-efficient model editing.
On the Relationship Between Tensor Representations
and Architectures. A natural question arises regarding how
different tensor representations interact with different neural
architectures. We offer the following observations: (1) For the
same architecture, different tensor representations offer different
trade-offs. For instance, in CNNs, TR decomposition generally
outperforms TT due to its ring structure providing more flexibility
at endpoints, while CP offers the highest compression ratio but
may sacrifice accuracy. Tucker decomposition provides a balanced
middle ground. (2) The same tensor representation manifests dif-
ferently across architectures. TT decomposition in CNNs primarily
targets convolutional kernel channels; in RNNs, it decomposes
input-to-hidden and hidden-to-hidden transformation matrices; in
Transformers, it compresses attention weight matrices and FFN
layers. Despite sharing the same mathematical framework, the
specific design choices (e.g., reshaping strategies, rank allocation)
differ substantially across architectures. (3) Architectural affinity
of tensor decompositions. Certain decompositions show natural
affinity with specific architectures: BTT is particularly well-suited
for CNNs, as its hyperedge structure naturally captures channel-
wise summation of Tucker blocks, enabling implicit pruning
of redundant connections [50]; MPO is especially effective for
Transformers, as its structure aligns with the multi-head attention
paradigm, allowing compression of attention weights while preserv-
ing head-specific information [58]; and MPS/TT formats naturally
fit sequential data processing in RNNs due to their linear chain
topology. However, whether certain decompositions are exclusively
suitable for certain architectures remains an open question that
warrants further investigation.
5
TRAINING STRATEGIES FOR TNNS
While the aforementioned TNNs can perform well on various tasks
and machines, it is also worth exploring training strategies with
more stability, better performance and higher efficiency. In this
section, we introduce such strategies in three groups: (1) strategies
for stabilizing the training processes of TNNs are presented in
Section 5.1, (2) strategies for selecting and searching the ranks of
TNNs are provided in Section 5.2, and (3) strategies for applying
hardware speedup are shown in Section 5.3.
5.1
Stable Training Approaches
Despite their success, TNNs face significant training challenges
stemming from their inherent multilinear characteristics. While tra-
ditional neural networks primarily rely on simple linear operations
like matrix multiplication, TNNs involve tensor contractions that
result in exponentially scaling data flows as the number of modes
increases linearly [77]. This exponential scaling affects both the
forward propagation of features and the backward propagation of
gradients, creating substantial computational and numerical stability
challenges. Several approaches have been proposed to address these
issues. One straightforward solution involves using full-precision
float64 format to represent large weights, which helps mitigate
numerical instability problems. However, this approach comes with
significant drawbacks - the higher precision format requires more
computational resources and increases processing time compared
to lower-precision alternatives like float16. Conversely, while lower
precision formats offer computational efficiency, they can introduce
numerical stability issues that compromise training effectiveness.
To balance these competing concerns, Panagakis et al. [76]
introduced an innovative mixed-precision strategy. This dynamic
precision approach adaptively adjusts numerical precision during

Another feasible way to solve the training problem lies in
developing a suitable initialization method for tensorial neural
networks (TNNs). Two widely adopted adaptive initialization
methods in deep learning are Xavier [215] initialization and
Kaiming [216] initialization. Xavier initialization, proposed by
Glorot and Bengio in 2010, regulates the variances of data flows
between layers to prevent the vanishing gradient problem in deep
networks. Similarly, Kaiming initialization, introduced by He et
al. in 2015, was specifically designed for networks using ReLU
activation functions. However, these conventional initialization
methods face two major challenges when applied to TNNs. First,
they cannot accurately calculate the appropriate scales for TNNs
due to their inability to account for the complex interactions
occurring in tensor contractions.
Second, the diversity of tensor formats (e.g., CP decomposition,
Tucker decomposition, Tensor Train) makes it challenging to
develop a universally applicable initialization method that fits all
tensorial layers. To address these limitations, Yu initialization [77]
was proposed as a unified initialization paradigm. This method
extends the principles of Xavier initialization while introducing
adaptive mechanisms specifically designed for arbitrary Tensor-
based Convolutional Neural Networks (TCNNs). The key in-
novation of Yu initialization lies in its systematic approach to
handling tensor operations. Specifically, Pan et al. developed a
two-step process: First, they extract a backbone graph (BG) from a

structure of tensor operations. Second, they encode an arbitrary
TCNN into an adjacency matrix using this BG. Through this
adjacency matrix representation, the method can directly calculate
a suitable initial variance for any TCNN, taking into account
its specific tensor structure and operations. We illustrate three
representative cases of applying these unified initializations in

initialization was initially developed specifically for TCNNs, its
applicability extends far beyond this original scope. The method
has shown remarkable versatility and robustness across different
settings, and it can be effectively applied to a wide range of various
neural network architectures, including both convolutional and
Transformer-based models.
5.2
Rank Selection and Search
Prior studies [50], [54], [175] focused on finding efficient TN
formats (e.g., TTs and TRs) for compressing NNs and have
achieved significant efficiency for their natural compact structures.
However, despite these remarkable successes, efficient algorithms
for adjusting or selecting suitable ranks for a TN are lacking
since rank selection is an NP-hard problem [128]. As a result,
many approaches [49], [50], [52], [53] can only set values for
all ranks manually, which severely affects the resulting models’
training procedures. Fortunately, the rank selection problem can
still be optimized through heuristic strategies, such as Bayesian
optimization [81], [217], reinforcement learning (RL) [80] and
evolutionary algorithms (EAs) [79]. Here, we introduce some rank
selection methods for TNNs.
DNNs utilize neural architecture search (NAS) [218] to search
for the optimal network hyperparameters, achieving significant
success. As ranks can be treated as architecture hyperparameters,
NAS is applicable to searching for optimal tensorial layers with
better rank settings. Following this idea, the progressive searching
TR network (PSTRN) [79] employs NAS with an EA to select
suitable ranks for a TR network (TRN). In detail, the PSTRN
employs a heuristic hypothesis for searching: “when a shape-fixed
TRN performs well, part or all of its rank elements are sensitive, and
each of them tends to aggregate in a narrow region, which is called
an interest region”. Instructed by the interest region hypothesis, the
PSTRN can reach the optimal point with a higher probability than
a plain EA method. The PSTRN consists of an evolutionary phase
and a progressive phase. During the evolutionary phase, this method
validates the ranks in the search space on benchmarks and picks
the rank that yields the best performance. Then, in the progressive
phase, the PSTRN samples new ranks around the previously picked
rank and inserts them into a new search space. After several
rounds, the heuristic EA can find a high-performance solution.
With such an efficient design, the PSTRN successfully achieves
better performance than manual setting, which demonstrates that
its hypothesis is practical. Li et al. [111], [213], [219] formu-
late structure search through evolutionary topology optimization,
local-sampling-based permutation search, and alternating local
enumeration with fewer evaluations, respectively. Zeng et al. [220]
further study language-model-assisted discovery of tensor-network
structure-search algorithms. Together with the core-shape results
in Section 2, these studies show that practical TNN design depends
not only on rank values, but also on tensorization shape, mode
order, and topology.
In addition to NAS, some other efficient methods are also
available for rank selection. Zhao et al. [221] inferred a CP
rank by implementing a reduction process on a large rank value
via a variational Bayesian optimization procedure. Hawkins and
Zhang [81] extended this CP procedure [221] to TT-based TNNs
and adopted the Stein variational gradient descent method, which
combines the flexibility of the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
approach with the speed of variational Bayesian inference to
construct a Bayesian optimization method. In pretrained networks,
Kim et al. [222] and Gusak et al. [223] derived approximate
ranks by employing Bayesian matrix factorization (BMF) [224] to
unfolding weight tensors. Konstantin et al. [217] utilize a proxy-
based Bayesian optimization approach to find the best combination
of ranks for NN compression. Unlike Bayesian methods, Cheng et
al. [80] treated the rank searching task as a game process whose
search space was irregular, thus applying RL to find comparably
suitable ranks for a trained CNN. However, this algorithm is TD-
dependent, which indicates that its performance may be influenced
by the selected TD method.
Yin et al. [225] leveraged the alternating direction method of
multipliers (ADMM) to gradually transfer the original weight to a
low-rank representation (i.e., a TT). Farnaz et al. [226] proposed an
adaptive rank search framework for TR format in which TR ranks
gradually increase in each iteration rather than being predetermined
in advance. Wang et al. [82] apply renormalization-group flows to
automate tensor-network structure search, obtaining more compact
decompositions with substantially lower search cost. For TR models
in particular, latent-space rank minimization has also been shown
to be effective for controlling model complexity while preserving
representation quality [136]. Taken together, these results reinforce
a broader conclusion of this survey: rank, tensorization shape, and
mode order should be treated as a coupled design problem rather
than independent knobs.
We briefly discuss the strengths and limitations of the above
rank-selection approaches. Manual/Grid Search is the simplest
baseline. It is easy to implement but computationally expensive and
prone to suboptimal results. Heuristic methods (e.g., PSTRN [79])
leverage domain-specific assumptions (e.g., interest region hypoth-
esis) to guide the search, offering better efficiency than brute-force
methods, but may miss global optima when the assumptions do
not hold. Bayesian methods (e.g., CP-Bayes [221], TT-Bayes [81],
proxy-based Bayesian [217]) provide principled uncertainty quan-
tification over rank choices, enabling informed decisions with
limited evaluations; however, they are computationally demanding
and require careful prior specification. RL-based methods (e.g., TR-
RL [80]) offer flexible exploration of irregular search spaces but are
tensor-decomposition-dependent, meaning their performance may
vary across different TD formats. ADMM-based methods [225]
enable gradual transfer from full-rank to low-rank representations,
providing smooth optimization trajectories, but require careful
tuning of penalty parameters. Adaptive methods [226] automatically
grow ranks during training, avoiding the need for pre-specification,
but risk over-parameterization if growth criteria are not properly
calibrated. In practice, the choice of rank-selection strategy should
be guided by the specific requirements: Bayesian methods are
recommended when evaluation budgets are limited and uncertainty
quantification is important; heuristic methods are suitable for rapid
prototyping; and adaptive methods are preferred when the optimal
rank is unknown a priori.

5.3
Hardware Speedup
Accelerating the training and inference procedures of TNNs
can benefit resource consumption and experimental adjustment,
thereby achieving economic gains and green research. A direct and
effective approach is to optimize the speed of tensor operations in
TNNs to realize hardware acceleration. As inferring TT-format
TNNs inevitably results in enormous quantities of redundant
calculations, the TIE scheme [84] was proposed to accelerate
TT layers by splitting the working SRAM into numerous groups
with a well-designed data selection mechanism. Huang et al. [227]
designed a parallel computation scheme with higher I/O bandwidth,
improving the speed of tensor contractions. Later, they proposed an
LTNN [227] to map TT-format TNNs into a 3D accelerator based
on CMOS-RRAM, leading to significantly increased bandwidth via
vertical I/O connections. As a result, they simultaneously attained
high throughput and low power consumption for TNNs. Qu et
al. [85] proposed a spatial 2D processing element (PE) array
architecture and built a hardware TT engine consisting of off-chip
DRAM. Kao et al. [228] proposed an energy-efficient hardware
accelerator for CP convolution with a mixing method that combines
the Walsh-Hadamard transform and the discrete cosine transform.
ETTE [86] proposes a novel algorithm-hardware co-optimization
framework for TT based TNN acceleration, featuring new ten-
sor core construction, computation ordering mechanisms, and
lookahead-style processing schemes, thereby achieving significant
improvements in computational efficiency, memory consumption,
and data movement compared to existing acceleration solutions
across various DNN architectures and deployment settings.
Many more fascinating methods have been developed for the
acceleration of generic tensor operations, which are correlated
with TNNs. For instance, Huang et al. [229] observed that the
tensor matricization operation is usually resource-consuming since
its DRAM access is built on a random reading address; thus,
they proposed a tensor storage scheme with a sequential address
design for better DRAM accessibility. Both T2s-tensor [230] and
Tensaurus [231] mainly focus on designing general computation
kernels for dense and sparse tensor data. Xie et al. [120] and
Liang et al. [232] accelerated search procedures for obtaining an
optimal sequence of tensor contractions. Xie et al. [120] solved
the massive computational complexity problem of double-layer
TN contraction in quantum analysis and mapped such a double-
layer TN onto an intersected single-layer TN. Liang et al. [232]
implemented multithread optimization to improve the parallelism
of contractions. Meirom et al. [233] optimize tensor-network
contraction paths using reinforcement learning, complementing
the broader RL-based tensor-operation design perspective of Fawzi
et al. [234]. Xu et al. [87] develop a hierarchical optimizer that
combines top-down decomposition with bottom-up fusion to search
GPU kernel implementations while preserving local correctness
and global fusion opportunities. Their results suggest that TNN
acceleration can combine tensor-aware compiler representations
with learned, structure-aware kernel synthesis rather than relying
only on hand-designed contraction schedules. Although many
TNNs have low calculation complexity levels in theory, realistic
hardware deployments usually fall short of this objective due to
their numerous permutation operations [49], [235] and the absence
of sufficient parallelism [227]. Current hardware architectures are
primarily designed for matrix operations, making them suboptimal
for tensor operations that involve complex permutations and
contractions. Frequent data movement between memory hierarchies
creates substantial bottlenecks, especially for tensor transposition
and reshaping, which require extensive reorganization but con-
tribute little arithmetic computation. Promising directions include
specialized tensor processing units [236], tensor-aware memory
hierarchies, and efficient contraction and permutation primitives
that jointly optimize computation and data movement. In the future,
it is expected that more general hardware acceleration schemes
based on tensor operations will be developed to implement TNNs
with smaller storage and time consumption levels.
Remark. The training strategies discussed in this section
address three complementary aspects of TNN development: (1)
training stability through mixed-precision approaches and unified
initialization; (2) rank selection via heuristic, Bayesian, RL-based,
and adaptive methods; and (3) hardware acceleration through
specialized tensor operation engines. An important observation
is that these three aspects are interrelated—for instance, the choice
of rank directly affects both training stability and computational
efficiency, and hardware-aware rank allocation strategies could
potentially unify all three directions.
6
TNN TOOLBOXES
In this section, we present a systematic taxonomy and critical
analysis of the existing toolbox ecosystem for TNNs rather
than introducing new toolboxes. Specifically, our contributions
are twofold: (1) we propose a unified categorization of existing
toolboxes into three levels, namely basic tensor operations, deep
model implementations, and quantum simulations, which helps
readers quickly identify the tools most suitable for their needs;
and (2) we analyze the limitations and gaps of current toolboxes,
highlighting key directions for future improvement.
In 1973, Pereyra and Scherer [237], as pioneers in this field,
developed a programming technique for basic tensor operations.
Modern computing frameworks support a broad range of basic
tensor-operation toolboxes and higher-level TNN libraries for
network compression and quantum-circuit simulation. In this
section, toolboxes for TNNs are presented in three categories
according to their design purposes: (1) toolboxes for basic tensor
operations, which contain important and fundamental operations
(e.g., tensor contraction and permutation) in TNNs (Section 6.1); (2)
toolboxes for network compression are high-level TNN architecture
toolboxes based on other basic operation tools (Section 6.2); and
(3) toolboxes for quantum circuit simulation are software packages
for the quantum circuit simulation or quantum machine learning
processes that use TNs from a quantum perspective (Section 6.3).
6.1
Toolboxes for Basic Tensor Operations
Toolboxes for basic tensor operations aim to implement some
specific TD algorithms. Many basic tensor toolboxes based on
different programming languages and backends have been designed
for this purpose. For example, the online stochastic framework for
TD (OSTD) [238] and Tensor Toolbox [89] were constructed for
low-rank decomposition and implemented with MATLAB. Regard-
ing Python-based toolboxes, TensorTools based on NumPy [239]
implements CP only, while T3F [240] was explicitly designed
for TT decomposition on TensorFlow [241]. Similarly, based on
TensorFlow, TensorD [242] supports CP and Tucker decomposition.
Tntorch [90] is a PyTorch-based library for tensor modeling in the
CP, Tucker and TT formats. TorchMPS [67], TT-Toolbox [243]
and Scikit-TT [244] are all powerful Python-based specific TT
solvers that efficiently implement the DMRG algorithm. Tensorly
PROCEEDINGS OF THE IEEE (ACCEPTED VERSION)
19
is a powerful general TD library that supports many decomposition
formats and various Python backends including CuPy, Pytorch,
TensorFlow and MXNet. TensorNetwork [92] is a powerful general-
purpose TN library that supports a variety of Python backends,
including JAX, TensorFlow, PyTorch and NumPy. Complemen-
tarily, CoLA [245] automates structure-aware numerical linear
algebra by dispatching efficient routines for compositional matrix
structures, such as Kronecker products, block diagonals, and
sums, enabling accelerated solves, eigendecompositions, and trace
estimation with automatic differentiation support in both JAX
and PyTorch. In addition, some toolboxes based on C++ are also
available. TenDeC++ [246] leverages a unique pointer technology
called PointerDeformer in C++ to support the efficient computation
of TD functions. ITensor [91] is an efficient and flexible C++ library
for general TN calculations. Tensor4ML [247] provides a compre-
hensive overview of tensor decomposition models, algorithms, and
optimization techniques, along with Python implementations and
datasets, serving as a bridge between theoretical foundations and
practical applications in machine learning and data science.
6.2
Toolboxes for Network Compression
Specific TNN toolboxes are used to assist with the development of
tensorial layers. Although some general tensor toolboxes such as
Tensorly [88] are powerful for TD processing and can use their TD
operations to help initialize TNN modules to a certain extent, they
still lack support for application programming interfaces (APIs) for
building TNNs directly. Therefore, a TNN library (Tensorly-Torch)
based on Tensorly was developed to build some tensor layers
within any PyTorch network. Pan et al. also developed a powerful
TNN library called TedNet [49]. TedNet can quickly set up TNN
layers by directly calling the API. In addition, TedNet supports the
construction of TCNNs and TRNNs in single lines of code.
6.3
Toolboxes for Quantum Simulation
A number of quantum circuit simulation toolboxes have been
designed. For example, some TT toolboxes such as Scikit-TT and
TorchMPS can partially simulate quantum circuits to some extent,
although they were not specifically designed for quantum circuits
simulation. In contrast, general TN toolboxes, e.g., TensorNetwork
and ITensor, can simulate any quantum circuit. In addition, with
optimized tensor contraction, TeD-Q [93], a TN-enhanced open-
source software framework for quantum machine learning, enables
the simulation of large quantum circuits. Furthermore, Yao [248],
an extensible and efficient library for designing quantum algorithms,
can provide support for dumping a quantum circuit into a TN.
In addition, TensorNetworkQuantumSimulator.jl [94], a Julia
toolbox for quantum circuit simulation with PEPS-style tensor-
network backends, further broadens the software landscape beyond
the Python/C++ ecosystems and is directly relevant to TN-based
quantum simulation workflows. Although no practical hardware
implementations of quantum TNNs are currently available, these
classical quantum circuit simulations are still potentially useful and
valuable for simulations.
Remark. Despite the success of current toolboxes, some
areas for improvement remain. (1) Existing basic tensor operation
toolboxes are mostly implemented on top of high-level runtime
stacks, which makes it difficult to fuse reshaping, permutation, and
contraction into layout-aware kernels that fully exploit modern
accelerators. (2) Existing deep model implementation toolboxes for
TNNs can only contain a limited number of predefined TNN
structures and cannot allow users to design structures freely.
(3) Existing quantum simulation toolboxes focus more on the
simulation of quantum circuits using TNs and do not facilitate the
processing of embedded quantum data via TNNs.
6.4
Benefits of Tensor Networks for Interpretability
Tensor networks offer significant potential for interpretability, a
dimension largely underexplored in the TNN literature. Their
structured, low-rank form enables the extraction of quantities
such as mutual information and entanglement entropy between
input features. Puljak et al. [95] use MPS representations for
transparent classification and anomaly detection. Hamreras et
al. [188] analyze low-rank factorizations of neural weights and
activations as tools for both compression and interpretability. Pareja
Monturiol et al. [96] construct MPS representations of trained black-
box networks through sketching and cross interpolation, enabling
structural analysis while obfuscating parameter-level information
associated with the training distribution. Hamdi and Angryk [249]
further show that tensor factorization can expose meaningful latent
structure in graph data. Heidari and Rabusseau [97] construct a
graph-aligned tensor-network surrogate for a black-box predictor
and deterministically recover first- and higher-order Shapley
interactions without repeated model queries. Their formulation
uses TN topology directly as an interpretable surrogate structure
rather than only as a compressed parameterization.

DISCUSSION AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES
The value of a survey lies not only in organizing existing
methods, but also in identifying the design principles that recur
across otherwise different applications. The literature reviewed
in this article indicates that the central questions in TNN re-
search concern how tensors are formed, how network topology
affects expressiveness and computation, how learning algorithms
exploit multilinear structure, and when tensorization provides an
appropriate inductive bias. We therefore organize the discussion
around five cross-cutting themes, followed by a perspective on the
exchange of ideas between tensor-network learning and quantum
physics. These themes should be interpreted together with several
unresolved limitations. Tensorized models remain nonconvex and
can be sensitive to initialization, rank choice, mode ordering, and
numerical conditioning. Theoretical parameter reductions do not
necessarily translate into wall-clock or energy savings because
permutation, contraction scheduling, and memory traffic can
dominate execution. Robustness to perturbations, calibration, cross-
domain transfer, and reproducibility across common benchmarks
also remain less established than compression results. Accordingly,
the following perspectives emphasize testable design criteria and
matched-budget evaluation rather than treating tensorization as an
automatic advantage.
7.1
Tensorization Design Beyond Rank
Tensorization design is a multi-dimensional model-selection prob-
lem. Rank is an important capacity parameter, but it does not
uniquely determine either approximation quality or computational
cost. The mapping from a dense tensor to a collection of modes
determines which variables interact locally, the dimensions of
the cores determine where representational capacity is allocated,
and the network topology determines which correlations can be
expressed through short contraction paths. Ghadiri et al. [110]
PROCEEDINGS OF THE IEEE (ACCEPTED VERSION)
20
show that core shape alone can materially change decomposition
efficiency, while Li et al. [111] demonstrate that mode permutation
can alter the quality of a fixed tensor-network structure. These
results imply that reporting only the decomposition family and rank
is insufficient for reproducible comparison.
Topology selection further couples statistical and computational
considerations. Li et al. [213], [219] search over tensor-network
structures using evolutionary and local-enumeration strategies,
whereas Wang et al. [82] use renormalization-group flows to
guide structure search. Such methods move beyond the assumption
that TT, TR, Tucker, or PEPS should be selected in advance.
However, the resulting search spaces are highly nonuniform:
two structures with the same number of parameters may have
very different contraction widths, memory requirements, and
optimization landscapes. Structure search should therefore optimize
a vector of objectives rather than parameter count alone.
A useful evaluation protocol should report the tensorization
map, mode ordering, core dimensions, rank profile, topology,
and contraction strategy together with accuracy, peak memory,
and measured latency. Comparisons should control at least one
of parameter count, arithmetic cost, or memory traffic, because
matching rank does not imply matching computational complexity.
An important research direction is to develop differentiable or
bilevel procedures that jointly select tensorization, topology, ranks,
and contraction order. Such procedures would turn architectural
choices that are currently treated as implementation details into
explicit, testable components of the learning problem.
7.2
Expressiveness Versus Contractibility
The expressive power of a tensor network is inseparable from the
cost of contracting it. Chain and tree structures, including TT/MPS
and HT, remain common because they admit efficient exact
contraction and stable local manipulation. Their low treewidth,
however, restricts the pathways through which distant variables
interact. Loopy structures such as PEPS can encode richer spatial
and multiway correlations, but exact contraction is computationally
intractable in the general case [8], [20]. Consequently, a topology
that is more expressive in representation space may be less useful
as a trainable neural component if its forward and backward
contractions cannot be evaluated within practical resource limits.
This trade-off is not binary. Gray and Chan [112] show that
contraction for arbitrary geometries can be improved substantially
through hyperoptimization, while Alkabetz and Arad [139] connect
belief propagation with tensor-network contraction. Tindall et
al. [140] further demonstrate that message-passing approximations
can support large loopy simulations. These studies indicate that
the effective model class is determined jointly by topology
and contraction algorithm. Approximation error, contraction-path
quality, numerical stability, and hardware parallelism therefore
belong to the architectural analysis rather than to a separate
implementation stage. Future TNN studies should evaluate ex-
pressiveness under a fixed contraction budget. Relevant quantities
include maximum intermediate tensor size, contraction width, peak
memory, communication volume, and the error introduced by
approximate contraction. Hybrid structures may offer a productive
compromise: tree-like backbones can preserve tractability, while a
limited number of additional edges can encode selected long-range
dependencies. Another open problem is to connect local contraction
error with task-level prediction error and gradient bias. Such theory
would clarify when approximate contraction preserves the benefit
of a richer topology and when it merely introduces an uncontrolled
source of optimization noise.
7.3
The Algorithmic Shift in TNN Research
TNN research is increasingly defined by algorithms for con-
structing, searching, contracting, and learning tensor networks
rather than by the insertion of a fixed decomposition into another
neural architecture. Bharadwaj et al. [113] reduce the cost of
TT construction through leverage-score sampling, and Tao et
al. [114] formulate probabilistic tensor decomposition as an
undirected graphical model. Hameed and Rabusseau [250] develop
an automatic-differentiation-compatible procedure for probabilistic
tensor networks; because this work remains a preprint, its empirical
conclusions should be interpreted together with the peer-reviewed
probabilistic decomposition literature. These examples show that
sampling, uncertainty estimation, and numerical linear algebra are
becoming first-class parts of TNN methodology.
A parallel algorithmic development concerns contraction and
execution. Meirom et al. [233] learn contraction strategies with
reinforcement learning, complementing the broader demonstration
by Fawzi et al. [234] that search can discover efficient multilin-
ear algorithms. At the computation-graph level, Xu et al. [87]
combine hierarchical decomposition and fusion to search tensor-
oriented GPU kernels. These contributions blur the conventional
boundary between model architecture, compiler optimization, and
hardware mapping: changing the contraction schedule can alter
memory consumption by orders of magnitude without changing
the mathematical tensor network.
The next step is to optimize these layers jointly. A complete
pipeline would select the tensorization and topology, estimate ranks,
choose exact or approximate contraction, and generate hardware-
aware kernels under a shared resource objective. Such integration
requires reproducible benchmarks that separate algorithmic gains
from implementation effects and report wall-clock time, memory
traffic, numerical error, and energy consumption in addition to pa-
rameter count. Theoretical analysis is also needed for convergence
when the model structure or contraction approximation changes
during training. Establishing these foundations would position
TNNs as a general framework for structured computation rather
than a collection of compression techniques.
7.4
Tensorization as an Inductive Bias
Tensorization should be interpreted as an inductive bias, not
merely as a mechanism for reducing parameters. A decomposition
constrains which interactions are represented directly, which corre-
lations must be transmitted through intermediate cores, and how
parameters are shared across modes. When these constraints align
with the structure of the task, a tensorized model may generalize
better than a dense model even at a comparable parameter budget.
Wang et al. [115] provide theoretical and empirical evidence
that transformed low-rank parameterizations can improve robust
generalization. This perspective explains why compression ratio
alone is an incomplete measure of success: two equally compact
models may encode very different assumptions about the data.
Several application settings illustrate this principle. Xie et
al. [198] use shared Tucker-structured templates as transferable
priors across vision-model scales. Wang et al. [66] encode corre-
lations across multiple graphs through shared latent tensor factors
rather than treating graph layers independently. Klein et al. [61]
and Zhang et al. [62] impose structured low-rank interactions
PROCEEDINGS OF THE IEEE (ACCEPTED VERSION)
21
on attention and its key-value representations. In each case, the
tensor format specifies which forms of sharing and interaction are
preferred before optimization begins.
Matrix factorization provides a necessary baseline hierarchy for
evaluating this bias. Truncated SVD and related matrix-low-rank
methods operate on a selected unfolding and can be preferable
when a weight is intrinsically two-dimensional. TNNs generalize
this idea by retaining multiple modes and distributing capacity
across cores and topology, but the advantage is not automatic:
reshaping can introduce artificial correlations, and contraction
overhead can offset parameter savings. Evaluations should therefore
compare dense, matrix-low-rank, and tensor-network models under
matched parameter, compute, and data budgets, while also reporting
out-of-distribution performance, robustness, and calibration. Such
comparisons help distinguish task-aligned structural generalization
from gains caused only by regularization or reduced model size.
7.5
Unified Learning and Scientific Machine Learning
Tensor networks provide a common representation language for su-
pervised prediction, unsupervised density estimation, probabilistic
inference, and operator learning. Zeng et al. [83] connect supervised
and unsupervised tensor-network learning through mixed structures,
structure-search priors, and lightweight uncertainty quantification.
This perspective is important because many applications require
more than a point prediction: they must also represent latent
structure, generate samples, or quantify uncertainty. A unified
framework can share cores or intermediate representations across
these objectives while preserving the explicit multilinear structure
of the model.
Scientific machine learning places additional demands on such
a framework. Models must often respect separability, locality,
conservation structure, or symmetries while operating on high-
dimensional fields. Zhou et al. [178] decompose a high-dimensional
Fourier neural operator into lower-dimensional operators and estab-
lish a universal-approximation guarantee. Yang et al. [177] show
that Kronecker structure can also be used to generate parameter-
efficient connectivity patterns rather than merely factorize existing
weights. These examples indicate that tensorization can serve
simultaneously as a computational device and as a mechanism for
encoding scientific structure.
Important open problems concern the preservation of phys-
ical and statistical properties after factorization. Approximation
guarantees should be accompanied by analyses of stability, dis-
cretization transfer, uncertainty calibration, and conservation error.
Benchmarks should compare tensorized operators not only by
prediction accuracy, but also by memory, resolution generalization,
rollout stability, and sensitivity to rank and mode ordering. Another
promising direction is to combine probabilistic tensor networks
with neural operators so that uncertainty is represented in the same
structured space as the solution field. Such developments would
connect TNN research with inverse problems, surrogate modeling,
and data assimilation without reducing scientific machine learning
to another application-specific compression task.

Tensor networks originated as structured representations of quan-
tum many-body states, and this origin continues to provide both
mathematical tools and algorithmic intuition for TNNs. In quantum
simulation, tensor networks capture entanglement and high-order
correlations without explicitly materializing the exponentially large
state tensor [251]. Neural and tensor-network models therefore offer
complementary mechanisms: neural components provide flexible
nonlinear parameterization, while tensor structure provides con-
trolled correlation pathways and access to physically meaningful
quantities. The challenge is to combine these advantages without
discarding the tractability and interpretability that motivate tensor
networks in the first place.
Loconte et al. [70] place tensor factorizations and tractable
circuit representations in a generalized hierarchical framework, clar-
ifying how modular components can be composed while preserving
efficient inference. Physics-originated optimization algorithms offer
another connection. The density matrix renormalization group opti-
mizes an MPS locally through sweeping updates, providing a direct
analogue of alternating core optimization in TNN training [252].
Message-passing and approximate-contraction methods similarly
suggest ways to train models with loopy structures that would
otherwise be computationally inaccessible [139], [140]. MERA
provides a particularly instructive example because its multiscale
representation combines tensor contractions with disentanglers and
isometries [21], [253], [254]. These components encode scale-
dependent correlations that are not naturally represented by a
simple chain or ring. Translating such structures into practical
TNNs requires differentiable constraints, stable joint optimization,
and efficient contraction on classical hardware. Evaluation should
determine whether physics-inspired components improve represen-
tation or optimization beyond what can be explained by parameter
count. More broadly, future work should identify which physical
concepts, including entanglement measures, renormalization, gauge
freedom, and locality, remain meaningful when tensor networks
are embedded in neural systems. This distinction is necessary to
turn physical analogy into testable algorithmic design.

CONCLUSION
TNs and NNs provide complementary perspectives on high-
dimensional learning: TNs offer explicit multilinear structure and
controllable complexity, whereas NNs provide flexible nonlinear
representations and scalable data-driven optimization. This survey
has examined their theoretical connections, methodological integra-
tion, and principal applications within TNNs. In particular, tensor
convolution, convolutional tensors, tensorized parameterizations,
and tensor-network diagrams provide a common mathematical lan-
guage for analyzing neural operations and clarifying how network
structure affects representation, computation, and optimization.
The practical value of this integration extends beyond parameter
compression. For data representation, TNNs preserve higher-order
dependencies in multimodal, multi-view, multi-source, and multi-
task settings without requiring the underlying structure to be
flattened prematurely. For model design, tensor decompositions
replace dense transformations with structured multilinear mappings,
reducing parameter count, memory, and computational cost. More
importantly, tensorization acts as an inductive bias: mode organiza-
tion, core shape, rank allocation, and network topology determine
which interactions are represented efficiently and how information
is shared across inputs, tasks, layers, or modalities. Consequently,
a TNN’s effectiveness cannot be judged by compression ratio
alone, but also by predictive performance, robustness, contraction
cost, memory traffic, and compatibility with the target problem’s
structure. TNNs also connect machine learning with quantum many-
body modeling and physics-inspired computation. Concepts such

as entanglement, locality, renormalization, message passing, and
structured contraction provide useful principles for understanding
and designing tensorized learning systems. Conversely, differen-
tiable optimization and neural parameterization can extend the
range of tensor-network models that can be trained from data.
This relationship should nevertheless be treated as a source of
testable computational and representational principles rather than
as a purely conceptual analogy. Establishing when physics-inspired
structures provide measurable advantages over conventional tensor
decompositions remains an important research question.
Future progress will require treating tensorization, topology
selection, rank adaptation, contraction, and hardware execution
as a coupled design problem rather than optimizing them in
isolation. Recent advances in tensorized attention, mixture-of-
experts models, graph learning, neural operators, and multiscale
structures such as MERA illustrate the breadth of this design
space, but their practical value should be established through
matched-budget and hardware-aware evaluation. Parameter and
arithmetic reductions should therefore be assessed together with
predictive performance, robustness, memory traffic, latency, and
energy consumption. Under these criteria, tensor structure can
evolve from a post hoc compression device into a principled
architectural choice for efficient, interpretable, and structurally
grounded learning, while also clarifying when multilinear structure
provides measurable advantages in neural computation.
