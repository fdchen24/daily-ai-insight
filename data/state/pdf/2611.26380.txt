Abstract | Egocentric video captioning requires fine-grained detail and accurate timing, yet current multimodal
large language models (MLLMs) often miss brief actions, confuse their order, or misinterpret the wearer’s
motion. Existing caption formats also offer limited support for combining detailed narration with precise
timing. We present EgoCaptioner, which pairs a video-level overview of the setting, participants, and the
wearer’s overall activity with timed steps whose narration adapts to varying perceptual demands and marks
key moments at specific timestamps. We develop a coarse-to-fine annotation pipeline that establishes event
structure before expanding step descriptions, yielding 50,000 training examples from eight egocentric video
sources. Complementary rewards assess factual accuracy and coverage, measure step-level temporal alignment,

pose-derived motion facts when sensor data are available. Across five egocentric benchmarks, EgoCaptioner

complementary roles of factual and temporal rewards and show that coarse-to-fine annotation provides more

1. Introduction
Egocentric video records how people act in and interact with their surroundings, making it a valuable
source of data for embodied AI and video generation [10, 15, 37, 40, 48]. Captions make these
recordings searchable and allow downstream systems to identify when particular actions occur. For
example, personal assistants retrieve captions as memory to answer questions about the wearer’s
day [48], and robot learning uses timestamped action captions to curate pretraining data [15]. Both
applications depend on captions that preserve the details of an activity and associate them with the
right moments.
Current multimodal large language models (MLLMs) [3, 22, 28, 35, 36, 43–45, 52, 54] can produce
detailed video descriptions [4, 6, 18, 52, 56]. On egocentric footage, however, they often overlook
brief actions, confuse their order, misinterpret the wearer’s movements, or omit objects central to an
event [20, 26, 32, 34, 39].
Part of the problem is that current captioners struggle to decide what to describe and how much
detail each activity needs. General captioners [4, 52] cover the visible scene broadly without binding
the narrative to the wearer’s actions. Current egocentric captioners, meanwhile, typically produce a
brief narration or specialize in hand–object manipulation [21, 23, 47, 57], leaving activities such as
navigation and social interaction less fully described. A second challenge is that detail and timing are
rarely achieved together. Dense paragraphs often leave unclear when individual actions begin and
end, while timestamped captions typically summarize each event in a sentence [12, 31] or group
details by scene [50]. Furthermore, the wearer’s motion is difficult to interpret from the first-person
viewpoint, because the body is often only partially visible, and the captioner must infer travel direction
from changes in the scene [39], cues that can be misleading when head motion diverges from body
motion [19].
To address these challenges, we present EgoCaptioner, which describes an egocentric video on two
levels (Figure 1). A video-level overview establishes the setting and spatial layout, introduces the
people and objects involved, and summarizes the wearer’s activities and any purpose supported by
the footage. Detailed steps then narrate how the activity unfolds: EgoCaptioner segments the video
into temporally bounded events, divides each event into ordered steps with start and end times,
and marks key moments, such as object state changes, with timestamps. This two-level structure
supports three tasks: full-video captioning, overview captioning, and event-conditioned captioning, in
which the model localizes and describes an event named in the instruction. We train EgoCaptioner by
supervised fine-tuning on 50,000 curated examples from eight egocentric video sources, followed by
reinforcement learning with rewards for factual content, temporal alignment, and caption style.
We evaluate EgoCaptioner across several benchmarks on four dimensions: caption quality, caption-
conditioned QA, coverage of physical event details, and temporal alignment. Compared with its
Qwen3.5-9B backbone, EgoCaptioner improves by 27.6 points in PhysCapBench [42] Overall, surpass-
ing Gemini 3.1 Pro, and by 16.8 percentage points in EgoTempo [26] accuracy. It also achieves the
highest PSJ score among open-source models for step segmentation on EgoHome [25]. Our ablations
examine how training data scale, annotation format, and reward design each affect performance.
Further analysis shows that tailoring descriptive detail to the activity helps captions capture the
aspects that human observers find salient.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We formulate egocentric captioning as a video overview and timed steps that captures task-

2. Related Work
Multimodal Large Language Models
Recent multimodal large language models (MLLMs) advance
temporal understanding through scaled synthetic supervision [55], multi-granularity descriptions [43],
and time-aware position encoding [1, 2]. Yet egocentric benchmarks reveal persistent hallucinations
about object interactions [32] and temporal reasoning errors [26], calling for descriptions that
faithfully ground wearer actions and camera motion in the video’s temporal structure.
Egocentric Video Understanding
First-person video interleaves rapid head motion with hand–
object manipulation, making the wearer largely invisible and scene dynamics viewpoint-dependent.
Representation methods address this by grouping spatiotemporal tokens into scene entities [41] or
learning hand–object dynamics with motion adapters [23], while captioning approaches leverage
third-person references [47], cross-view transfer [21], and recursive composition across temporal
scales [14]. Such captions further serve as retrievable memory for question answering [48] and
as timestamped supervision for robot pretraining [15]. We produce a video-level overview with
timestamped steps and key moments, emphasizing hand roles, motion, and task-focused details.
3. Method
3.1. Task Formulation
We describe an egocentric video at two levels. A video-level overview establishes the setting and
summarizes what the camera wearer is doing. Steps then narrate how the activity unfolds, organized
by timed events that cover the video in consecutive, non-overlapping intervals.
Overview.
The overview sets the scene by describing the location, surroundings, lighting and the
time of day and weather. Within this setting, it describes the wearer, other participants, and relevant
objects, noting their positions relative to one another. Drawing on this context and the actions shown,
it also summarizes what the wearer is doing overall and, when the goal is apparent, what they are
trying to accomplish.
Steps.
We segment each video into non-overlapping events, each covering a coherent activity, and
divide each event into ordered steps with start and end times. The steps narrate what happens within
the event, emphasizing the details that matter for the activity. A step may also record a key moment
in a sentence beginning with At [t], marking the instant at which something relevant happens.
Tasks.
The two levels define three instruction-following tasks. (1) Full-video captioning produces
the overview followed by the steps of all events. (2) Overview captioning produces the overview alone.
(3) Event-conditioned captioning produces the steps of an event named in the instruction, requiring
the model to localize the event before describing it.

3.2. Data Construction
We collect egocentric videos spanning daily activities, procedural tasks, outdoor walking, and manipu-
lation from Ego4D Goal-Step [38], the Ego4D subset of EgoHOD [23], Sekai [16], EgoVid-5M [46], the
EgoPro subset of EgoSuite-Open100K [17], HumanPlus-1000 [13], the processed release of RekaDaily-
10k [30] and EgoLife [48]. We build the annotation coarse-to-fine with two complementary MLLMs,
Gemini-3.1-Pro [9] and Seed2.1 pro [3].
Task-focused narration.
Different activities call for different details, so we define four event types
and use each type’s narration emphasis to prompt the annotator, steering the description toward
the details most relevant to that activity. Hand–object manipulation (manip) centers on what the
wearer does to an object, each hand’s role, how the object changes, and where it is placed afterward.
Locomotion (locomote) records the travel mode, direction, and route relative to landmarks, using
camera motion cues such as bobbing or sway to identify the mode. Social interaction (social) covers
who participates, how they take turns acting and responding, and what gestures or expressions clarify
intent. Visual attention (attend) tracks what the wearer observes, how attention shifts between
targets, and when a target becomes occluded or reappears.
Within a step, key moments mark sudden transitions, such as an object state change, a mishap, or a
person entering or leaving the scene. Each is written as At [t], followed by what happens at that
instant.
Coarse-to-fine annotation.
The annotation proceeds in two stages (Figure 2(a)). (i) Structure.
Gemini writes the overview from the full video. Seed2.1 pro identifies the events, their temporal
boundaries and types, and an outline of steps for each event. (ii) Detail. Gemini expands each outline
into timestamped step descriptions with key moments, prompted with the narration emphasis of
the event’s type. It views an event crop with up to 1 s of surrounding context on each side. Videos
longer than 30 s are annotated in clips of at most 30 s with 0.5 s overlap, and Gemini merges the clip
annotations into a single timeline. The two stages produce a complete draft caption for verification.
Verification and correction.
Seed2.1 pro then reviews the draft against the full video (Figure 2(b)),
jointly checking the described actions, their timing, and the consistency of the events with one another.
It corrects localized errors in place, and a final format check yields the validated caption.
From each validated annotation we construct instructions for the three tasks in Sec. 3.1 (Figure 2(c)).
For event-conditioned examples we only provide the event’s name without its time localization.
Pose-derived motion facts.
For videos with pose or trajectory recordings, we convert sensor
measurements into discrete motion facts following MotionScript [51], each describing the wearer’s
body movement or a change in head-mounted viewing direction together with its temporal span.
Because these facts come from sensors, they provide motion evidence independent of the reference
captions and serve as additional reference facts during reinforcement learning (Sec. 3.4).

As shown in Figure 3, we guide the optimization with three complementary rewards. A fact-based
reward measures how faithfully and completely the caption describes the video. For steps, a temporal
alignment reward measures how well the predicted step boundaries and granularity match the
reference, and a style reward discourages fragmented or redundant writing within each step.
Fact-based reward.
We evaluate captions using four fact types defined by the caption structure in
Sec. 3.1. Motion facts describe the wearer’s body movement and posture, as well as changes or stability
in the head-mounted view. When pose data are available, we also include the pose-derived facts from
Sec. 3.2. Focus facts capture the task-focused details defined for each event type in Sec. 3.2. Macro
facts describe the scene, the visual style and the wearer’s overall activity and purpose. Key-moment
facts, pair a timestamp with the change occurring at that instant and count as correct only when
both parts are.

4. Experiments
To comprehensively evaluate the ability of EgoCaptioner to produce detailed, time-aware and task-
focused descriptions, we investigate three research questions (RQs): RQ1: How well does it understand
egocentric videos? RQ2: Which design choices in our annotation and training pipeline contribute to
the performance? RQ3: Beyond benchmark scores, how well do its descriptions reflect the detailed,
task-focused design in practice?
4.1. Experimental Setup
Data and training.
We initialize EgoCaptioner from Qwen3.5-9B [29] and fine-tune it with
LoRA [11] (rank 32) on the data described in Sec. 3.3 for 2 epochs with a learning rate of 1×10−4.
We then apply GRPO on 8,000 video–instruction pairs balanced across the three captioning tasks,
sampling 𝐺=8 rollouts per input with a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of 1×10−5. Both stages
use 16 GPUs.

4.2. RQ1: Egocentric Video Understanding
We first assess how well EgoCaptioner understands egocentric videos (Table 1). The benchmarks
probe two sides of this capability: how faithful and informative the descriptions are, measured by
caption quality against references and by detail-oriented QA, and whether the described events are
anchored to the correct moments.
Description quality and video QA.
EgoCaptioner delivers the strongest open-source results on
EgoMe, EgoTempo, and Minerva-Ego. On EgoMe-Overview it reaches the highest BLEU-4 and
METEOR among all models including proprietary ones, and on EgoMe-Steps it likewise leads in
METEOR and SODA-d. EgoTempo and Minerva-Ego probe fine-grained facts such as manipulated
objects, individual actions, and their order, so a text-only reader can answer only when the caption
records those facts. Reading from EgoCaptioner’s captions yields the best open-source accuracy
on both benchmarks, and on Minerva-Ego it surpasses all proprietary models. On PhysCapBench,
EgoCaptioner scores 78.84 Overall, the best among open-source models and above Gemini 3.1 Pro.
Temporal alignment.
On EgoHome, we compare our predicted steps with human-annotated subtask
timelines for household activities. The permissible subsegment Jaccard (PSJ) score measures the
fraction of time for which the two timelines agree on the action being performed. The judge can
match a step in either timeline to several finer steps in the other if their descriptions refer to the same
underlying action. EgoCaptioner scores 75.51 on PSJ, ranking first among the open-source models
8

evaluated and outperforming Gemini 3.8 Flash. Tarsier2, AVoCaDO, and EgoGPT score zero on all
step-level metrics because they rarely produce parseable timestamps.
Taken together, EgoCaptioner is competitive with proprietary models across all five benchmarks while
being the strongest open-source model on every one of them.
4.3. RQ2: Which Design Choices Are Effective?
To understand the gains in RQ1, we examine the contributions of the annotation procedure, reward
design, and amount of training data.
Training stages, data scale, and rewards.
Table 2 reports three groups of comparisons. The first
group compares the base model, SFT, and SFT followed by GRPO to show the contribution of each
training stage. The second varies the amount of SFT data (10k, 25k, 50k triplets, sampled by video
with the task mixture preserved) under the same recipe. The third removes one GRPO reward term
at a time from the full objective, keeping SFT initialization and data fixed.
On our sampled subsets, SFT alone already brings large PSJ gains over the base model: even 10k
triplets recover most of the PSJ improvement (67.79 vs. 71.63 at 50k), while METEOR and SODA-d
improve steadily as the data scale increases to 50k. GRPO then lifts METEOR, SODA-d, and PSJ
further. Without 𝑅fact, all metrics fall below the SFT initialization, as the remaining rewards supervise
timing and style but place no constraint on content. Without 𝑅time, PSJ falls below SFT while METEOR
is unaffected, confirming that content quality alone does not produce aligned timestamps. Without
𝑅style, SODA-d and PSJ both decrease relative to the full objective. Notably, PSJ responds to all three
ablations: it credits a step only when its description matches the video and its interval aligns with the
reference, PSJ reflects both content and temporal fidelity.
Coarse-to-fine annotation.
To isolate the effect of our multi-stage annotation pipeline on data
quality, we construct a single-pass baseline: we prompt Gemini 3.1 Pro to generate a complete caption
in our target format from a single call per video, bypassing the intermediate event segmentation
and per-event description stages. The two conditions share the same source videos, training volume,
output format, and training hyperparameters; the single-pass prompt is detailed in the supplementary
material. As shown in Table 3, the coarse-to-fine supervision improves every metric. The largest gains
appear on detail-oriented QA, where EgoTempo accuracy rises by nearly 10 points and Minerva-Ego
more than doubles, and on temporal alignment, where PSJ rises by about 9 points. In the single-
pass condition the annotator segments and describes each step in one reading, producing coarser
boundaries and fewer step-level facts. Our procedure fixes the event structure first and then describes
each step from its event crop, which yields both aligned boundaries and the fine-grained facts.

5. Conclusion
We presented EgoCaptioner, an egocentric video captioner trained with coarse-to-fine supervision
and reinforcement learning that jointly optimizes factual content, temporal alignment, and caption
style. EgoCaptioner achieves the strongest open-source results on five egocentric benchmarks and
is competitive with proprietary models across caption quality, detail-oriented QA, and temporal
alignment. Ablations show that coarse-to-fine annotation, which establishes event structure before
step descriptions, and joint optimization of factual and temporal rewards are both necessary for
consistent gains. Per-dimension analysis on PhysCapBench further reveals that task-focused narration
contributes most where understanding causality and temporal order is required, not merely naming
visible objects.

When pose data are available, the pose-derived facts of Sec. B.2 join

geometrically by the pose annotations, whereas a caption written from video only estimates it. Pose
facts are therefore the only reference facts that do not pass through caption text.
Deduplication.
Mixing pose and caption facts creates two problems. Both can state the same
movement, which would count it twice in recall, and they can disagree about it, which would reward
matching the wrong one. A single language-model pass over all reference facts of a sample removes
both (prompt in Sec. E.4). The facts are listed in two groups, the steps group with motion and focus
facts and the overview group with macro facts. Each fact is tagged as caption or sensor, where sensor
marks pose-derived facts, and carries its time range. A fact is removed in two cases.
• Duplicate. Two facts in the same group state the same thing about the same moment. The less
specific one is removed, and a pose fact is kept over a caption fact that says no more.
• Conflict. Two facts, in the same group or across groups, cannot both be true of the same person,
object or movement at the same time. The one less likely to be correct is removed, with a
preference for the pose fact on camera and body movement. Both are removed when the pass
cannot tell.
Facts at different times, facts about different objects or people, and facts that only add or omit details
of each other are kept. The model only names the facts to remove and does not rewrite any, and the
pass runs once when the reference is built.
Judging.
Reference facts are judged against the predicted caption, and predicted facts against the
reference caption. Each request covers one caption section. Motion and focus facts are judged together
against the steps as written, with the At sentences left in place so that the caption reads coherently,
and macro facts against the overview alone, so a macro fact cannot earn credit from a step. Facts are
listed under their time ranges and matched to the caption by content, since timestamps can differ
slightly. The judge reads each fact by its sentence structure. The subject, complement, verb and object
that the sentence has form its core, and a core part matches when the caption uses the same word, a
synonym, or a pronoun that clearly refers back to it (prompt in Sec. E.4). The verdict maps to credit
𝑠( 𝑓| 𝑦) as follows.
• Supported (1). Every core part and detail matches, whatever the wording.
• Partial (0.4). Every core part matches, but the caption leaves out some details.
• Contradicted (0). The fact and the caption cannot both be true under any reading.
• Absent (0). None of the above.

B.2. Pose-Derived Motion Facts
Pose-derived facts come from two kinds of recordings. EgoPro and HumanPlus provide a body skeleton
and a head orientation for every frame, while Sekai provides only a camera trajectory estimated from
the video. We turn these measurements into text in three steps. Each frame value is first assigned
to a discrete bin. A stretch of frames over which the bin changes in one direction then becomes a
motion event, and the bin difference between its first and last frame gives the event’s category. Finally,
each selected event is written as a short sentence together with its measured time span. The bins,
event categories and candidate lists follow MotionScript [51]. The camera bins and the few extra
thresholds stated below are our own.
From frames to bins.
For the skeleton, we measure the pelvis translation along the left–right,
up–down and forward–backward axes of the body’s horizontal heading. We also measure the torso’s
forward lean (pitch), its sideways tilt (roll) and its turn (yaw) relative to its orientation at the start of
the clip. The head is described by the forward axis of the head joint expressed in the torso frame,
which gives its yaw and pitch relative to the body. Measuring the head against the torso keeps body
turns and walking out of the head motion. Each track is smoothed with a median filter over at most
seven frames and binned with the MotionScript thresholds. Translation uses 5 cm bins. Lean, tilt and
turn use the MotionScript angle bins, for example ±25◦, ±60◦, ±90◦and ±135◦for the turn. Invalid
frames and temporal gaps split a track into separate pieces.
From bins to events.
Within each piece we follow the bin index over time. An event starts once
the bin leaves its starting value and ends at the furthest bin reached before the track turns back
by more than one bin. An event is kept only when its total change exceeds one bin. The signed
bin difference between its first and last frame gives the direction and the magnitude, which the
MotionScript motioncode thresholds grade from very short to very long for translations and from
slight to significant for rotations. A very long body translation, for instance, spans more than ten bins,
that is, more than 0.5 m.
Camera trajectories.
Sekai clips have no skeleton, so we use the relative pose between consecutive
camera frames. Each relative pose is expressed in the axes of the camera at the end of that step, so
left, right, up and down always follow the current view. Per-axis translation and rotation velocities
are averaged over a 0.2 s window, and values below a noise floor are set to zero. A pause longer than
0.5 s or a reversal larger than one bin ends an event. We drop events shorter than 0.15 s and events
whose net change is less than 65% of their total path. Camera translation has no metric scale, so one
translation bin is 0.2 of the clip’s median distance traveled per second of motion. One rotation bin is
15◦, and any rotation of at least 20◦is graded as at least moderate.
Selection and wording.
Table 5 lists the events that become facts and the sentence written for
each. Head facts also require an angle change of at least 35◦, because smaller head turns are hard to
see in the video. A head-mounted camera cannot tell a torso turn from a head turn, so a torso turn

B.3. Key-Moment Scoring
Each “At [𝑡],” sentence in a predicted step is one predicted key moment, and the key moments of the
reference caption form the reference set. Each predicted moment is classified as correct or wrong in
the following order.
Placement.
A moment whose time lies outside the window of its own step is wrong, and no judge
is called.
Reference match.
The reference moments within 1 s of 𝑡are its candidates. A text judge (Sec. E.4)
decides for each candidate whether the predicted sentence describes the same moment, and a
confirmed moment is correct. Confirmed pairs are assigned one to one, nearest in time first, so each
reference moment credits a single prediction. A confirmed prediction whose reference went to a
nearer prediction scores zero and is not counted as an error.
Unmatched moments.
When no reference confirms a moment, a second text judge decides whether
the sentence states one of the key-moment types defined for annotation (Sec. E.2). A sentence that
does not is wrong. A sentence that does is checked against frames sampled at 10 fps from [𝑡−2, 𝑡+2] s
and clipped to its step. The frame judge decides whether the stated event happens and marks the

B.4. Temporal Reward, Format Reward and Validity Check
Validity check.
Before any judge is called, a parser reads the rollout in the output format of its task.
The rollout receives 𝑅= −1 when any of the following holds.
• Generation stops at the limit of 16,384 tokens.
• The text contains a verbatim loop, a block of 4 to 200 words repeated three times in a row. Case
and text in square brackets, such as timestamps and section labels, are ignored in this check.
• A full-video caption does not contain exactly one overview section followed by one step section,
or either section is empty. An overview caption is empty.
• No step is found, a step line has no [start–end] window, or a window has no description.
• A step starts before 0 s or at or after the video duration, ends more than 0.05 s after the duration,
or does not start before it ends. An end within 0.05 s after the duration is treated as rounding
and clipped.
• Steps are not written in temporal order, or two step windows overlap. Adjacent windows and
gaps between them are allowed.
Key-moment times are not part of this check. A key moment outside its step is scored as wrong
(Sec. B.3).
Temporal reward.
𝑅time (Eq. 2) uses only the step windows. The tIoU of two intervals is the length
of their intersection divided by the length of their union. All predicted–reference pairs are sorted by
tIoU in descending order, and a pair is accepted when neither of its intervals has been used. Matching
stops at the first pair with zero overlap. The sum is divided by the larger of the two step counts, so
steps left over on the longer side add to the denominator without adding to the sum, and splitting a
reference step or merging several reference steps both lower the reward.
Format reward.
Overview captioning has no step windows, so 𝑅format takes the place of the temporal
and style rewards. It averages two checks on the overview text, whether it opens with “The video”
and whether its last sentence begins with “Overall,”, and each check scores 1 when it holds and 0
otherwise. An overview that contains a step window or an “At [𝑡]” marker receives 𝑅format = 0, since
an overview is plain scene-level text.

Verification and correction
<Task>
Review the caption below (overview + detailed steps) against the video and repair the concrete
problems you find. Check two things:
1. Correctness: actions, objects, states, order, and times match the video, and the overview does
not contradict the steps.
2. Flow: the steps were written separately per event, so read them in order as one continuous
narration and repair the seams between steps and events.
</Task>
<Rules>
What to do with each statement:
- No support in the video: delete it.
- Wrong: correct it. Never delete it instead.
- Everything else the caption covers: preserve it. Keep the same information, and repair the text
around it rather than cutting it down.
Typical seam problems and their fixes:
- Repeated introductions: the same person, object, setting, or unchanged state is re-introduced in
later steps. Keep the full introduction at its first mention; later steps keep only new
details and visible changes.
- Broken continuity: an action that continues across a step or event boundary reads as starting
fresh. Rephrase it as a continuation — but keep real pauses, restarts, and repetitions shown
in the video.
- Rough wording: different names for the same thing, unclear references, abrupt transitions. Unify
references and smooth the phrasing, using wording supported by the video and adjacent steps.
How to edit:
- Make the smallest edit that fixes each diagnosed problem; leave unaffected text unchanged.
- Never invent transitions, causes, or unseen actions to smooth the text.
- Keep step order, boundaries, windows, and labels as given, unless a boundary itself is a
diagnosed timing error. Each corrected step must still stand on its own for its time interval.
Keep "At [time]," opening a complete sentence.
- Ensure corrected time windows do not overlap.
</Rules>
