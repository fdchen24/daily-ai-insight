Abstract
3D Gaussian Splatting enables object-level operations on Gaussian primitives, yet
existing segmentation methods are largely trained and evaluated through rendered
views. This mismatch can produce accurate rendered masks from incomplete 3D
selections since alpha-blended feature supervision concentrates learning on dom-
inant contributors while leaving occluded or low-contribution Gaussians weakly
supervised. We propose VODA, a plug-and-play supervision objective that exploits
local depth structure to group per-pixel contributors and directly supervises all
Gaussians in selected depth clusters, bypassing contribution-weighted gradient
delivery. To evaluate volumetric completeness, we introduce Removal-3D, a syn-
thetic object-removal benchmark, and the Removal Depth Error (RDE), a metric
comparing predicted and ground-truth depth changes. Across four rendering-based
baselines, VODA consistently improves volumetric segmentation on Removal-3D
while maintaining or improving performance on LERF and ScanNet. Further
analysis shows that VODA recovers lower-contribution Gaussians, while simple
gradient reweighting and spatial propagation fail to reproduce comparable gains.
1
Introduction
Object-level editing of reconstructed scenes requires selecting objects as complete 3D entities.
Removing, relocating, or extracting an object depends on capturing its full spatial extent, including
its interior. For scenes represented by 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [12], this means selecting all
Gaussians associated with the object. Yet existing segmentation methods [22, 24, 29, 10, 28, 6] are
commonly evaluated by the accuracy of their rendered 2D masks, which does not establish whether
the underlying 3D selection is complete. Figure 1 illustrates this gap: a selection can produce an
accurate mask yet miss some of the object’s Gaussians, leaving visible remnants after removal.
This gap stems from rendering-based feature supervision. Existing methods [29, 10, 28, 6] learn
per-Gaussian instance features through rendered 2D feature maps, using external feature or mask
targets, or mask-based contrastive objectives. Despite their differences, these objectives deliver
gradients according to each Gaussian’s rendering contribution (§3.1). Dominant contributors can thus
suffice to render an accurate mask, while low-contribution Gaussians belonging to the same object
receive weak or no supervision and may remain unselected.
We therefore study volumetric 3D Gaussian segmentation, aiming to select object-associated Gaus-
sians beyond those needed to render a correct mask. We propose VODA, a plug-and-play objective
that extends object-level supervision beyond the dominant contributors. VODA modifies only feature
supervision, leaving the baseline’s reconstructed scene, feature representation, and inference-time
selection procedure unchanged.
Our key observation is that, after RGB optimization, Gaussians associated with the same object
tend to cluster at similar depths, while larger depth gaps often reflect separation between distinct

Evaluating volumetric segmentation requires measuring whether an object selection is complete
beyond its visible projection. Existing benchmarks score rendered masks or visible surface labels and
therefore do not directly measure this property. We use object removal as a diagnostic: removing a
complete selection should produce the same depth change as removing the target object itself. We
therefore introduce Removal-3D, a synthetic indoor-scene benchmark with paired ground-truth depth
maps before and after target removal. To quantify how closely a predicted selection reproduces this
change, we propose Removal Depth Error (RDE), which compares the ground-truth depth change
with the change induced by removing the predicted Gaussians.
Experiments across four rendering-based baselines [28, 6, 29, 10] show that VODA consistently
improves volumetric segmentation on Removal-3D while maintaining or improving performance on
standard benchmarks [13, 9]. Ablations identify direct Gaussian-level supervision as the primary
source of these gains, while additional diagnostics support the need to change which Gaussians
receive supervision rather than merely modifying the existing rendering-based signal.
In summary, our contributions are threefold. (1) We formulate volumetric 3D Gaussian segmentation,
distinguishing selection completeness from rendered-mask accuracy and identifying the bias in
rendering-based supervision. (2) We propose VODA, a plug-and-play objective that directly super-
vises all Gaussians in selected depth clusters. (3) We introduce Removal-3D and RDE, a benchmark
and metric that assess selection completeness through removal-induced depth changes.
2
Related Work
The explicit primitives of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [12] support object-level selection and edit-
ing. Recent methods attach semantic features or object identities to Gaussians for object-level scene
understanding [29, 33]. We focus on how 2D supervision is transferred to Gaussians, distinguishing
rendering-based feature learning from direct 2D-to-Gaussian association.
Rendering-based Feature Learning.
Earlier NeRF methods lift 2D semantics through feature
distillation, mask supervision, or contrastive learning [31, 16, 26, 25, 1, 19, 4, 14, 30, 2], including
distillation of pretrained visual features such as DINO [3]. Rendering-based 3DGS methods similarly
alpha-blend per-Gaussian features into 2D maps, with two main supervision paradigms. Supervised
feature learning matches these maps to external targets: CLIP features [22, 24, 32, 34, 23, 18],
SAM mask identities as in Gaussian Grouping [29], or both jointly [10]. Self-supervised feature
learning instead uses SAM masks as grouping cues, pulling features within a mask together and
pushing features across masks apart without explicit feature targets [28, 6, 5, 8, 17]. Although their

reconstructed scene, feature representation, and inference-time selection unchanged.
Direct 2D-to-Gaussian Association.
Another line of work associates 2D observations directly
with Gaussians rather than learning features through rendered-map losses. Lifting By Gaussians [7]
assigns 2D mask labels to the per-pixel maximum-contributing Gaussian and merges the resulting
fragments across views. Dr. Splat [15] registers CLIP embeddings to dominant or Top-k Gaussians for
optimization-free 3D search. Quantile Rendering [11] uses quantile-based sampling to associate high-
dimensional features with a sparse set of influential Gaussians. Despite their different association
rules, these methods associate each pixel with only a sparse subset of Gaussian contributors, as
illustrated in Figure 2b. Multi-view observations can expand this set, but object-associated Gaussians
that remain weak contributors across views may still receive no assignment. Thus, bypassing
rendered-feature optimization alone does not necessarily resolve the incompleteness induced by
sparse, contribution-based association. VODA instead uses per-pixel depth structure to identify the
target-associated cluster and directly supervises all Gaussians within it.
3
Method
We propose VODA, a plug-and-play supervision objective for volumetric 3D Gaussian segmentation.
We first analyze why standard rendering-based supervision is insufficient in §3.1. The depth-aware
rasterizer in §3.2 then groups per-pixel contributors and selects a target depth cluster. Direct Gaussian-
level supervision in §3.3 applies a feature loss to every Gaussian in the selected cluster, together with
a complementary pixel-level push loss that encourages separation between object instances.

3.2
Depth-Aware Rasterizer
To address this imbalance, we need to identify object-associated Gaussians beyond those retained
by maximum or Top-k selection (Figure 2). Instead of relying solely on individual rendering
contributions, we exploit the local depth structure of the reconstructed scene. During reconstruction,
cloning and splitting introduce new Gaussians around existing primitives [12]. This local refinement
motivates using depth proximity as a cue for object association: contributors representing the same
local object region can form a depth cluster containing both dominant and low-contribution Gaussians.
Larger gaps can indicate separation from other objects or the background.

3.3
Direct Gaussian-Level Supervision
Given the selected depth cluster, we directly supervise its Gaussian features to bypass the contribution-
weighted rendering path. Since there are no ground-truth instance features for individual Gaussians,
we construct Gaussian-level pseudo-targets from the full rendered feature F(p) defined in Eq. 1, rather
than rendering the selected cluster alone. This feature is dominated by high-contribution Gaussians
that receive strong baseline supervision, providing a learned feature signal for other Gaussians in the
same cluster. We detach the resulting Gaussian-level targets with sg[·] and apply the loss directly to
Gaussian features, while retaining the baseline’s original rendering-based supervision.

4
Experiments
We evaluate VODA on two standard 3DGS segmentation benchmarks, LERF and ScanNet, in §4.1,
and on our Removal-3D benchmark for volumetric evaluation in §4.2. We further ablate its design
choices in §4.3 and analyze recovered Gaussians and contribution-based alternatives in §4.4. We
evaluate four baselines spanning both feature-learning paradigms in §2: OpenGaussian [28] and
LaGa [6] are self-supervised, while Gaussian Grouping [29] and ILGS [10] are supervised.
Implementation details.
We implement VODA on the official codebase of each baseline and apply
it only to the feature-learning stage, leaving RGB reconstruction and inference unchanged. The depth-
clustering and loss hyperparameters are shared across all four baselines. VODA is applied within the
existing feature stage for OpenGaussian and LaGa, and as a short feature-only fine-tuning stage for
Gaussian Grouping and ILGS. Full implementation details are provided in the supplementary.

3D Semantic Segmentation on ScanNet.
Following OpenGaussian, we use 19, 15, and 10 Scan-
Netv2 [9] categories as text queries and assign each Gaussian to its most similar query. We transfer
these predictions to the ScanNet point cloud and report mIoU and mAcc against the ground-truth
semantic labels. ScanNet complements LERF by evaluating category-level semantic assignment over
the entire scene rather than object-level selection from individual text queries. Table 2 shows consis-
tent improvements for both OpenGaussian and LaGa across all three category splits as the semantic
label set changes from 19 to 10 classes. The gains remain consistent across different category splits,
suggesting that VODA is not tied to a particular semantic class partition. Gaussian Grouping and
ILGS are not applicable because their features are tied to per-view SAM mask identities, which the
ScanNet text-query protocol does not provide. Figure 5 shows that VODA produces cleaner semantic
assignments around object boundaries, with less label leakage across neighboring regions. However,
ScanNet ground-truth labels cover only visible surfaces in the reconstructed point cloud and therefore
cannot directly assess occluded object-associated Gaussians, motivating our volumetric benchmark.

Supervision delivery.
We compare VODA’s direct loss with a rendering variant that uses the same
selected depth clusters and target features, but delivers supervision through feature rendering. Thus,
the two variants differ only in how supervision reaches the selected Gaussians. Direct supervision
consistently performs better across both baselines on LERF and Removal-3D, with the largest
difference on Gaussian Grouping, where LERF mIoU rises from 12.40 to 43.73. This shows
that identifying the appropriate depth cluster is not sufficient. Supervision must also bypass the
contribution-weighted rendering path and reach the selected Gaussian features directly.
Pixel-level push.
We next isolate the pixel-level push loss while retaining direct supervision. The
push loss has little effect on standard LERF performance, but consistently improves volumetric selec-
tion on Removal-3D. This supports its complementary role to direct supervision. Direct supervision
improves Gaussian coverage, while the push loss reduces feature mixing between nearby instances
and improves volumetric selection. Boundary visualizations in the supplementary illustrate this effect.
Target aggregation.
Finally, we compare uniform averaging with the α-weighted aggregation in
Eq. 5. Alpha weighting yields modest but consistent improvements across both baselines, supporting
the choice to place greater trust in pixels where the selected Gaussian has a higher per-pixel alpha.
Overall, the ablation shows that direct supervision provides the primary benefit, while the pixel-level
push improves instance separation and α weighting improves target reliability.

Contribution reweighting and spatial propagation.
Table 6 tests whether the imbalance can be ad-
dressed without explicitly assigning supervision through depth clusters. Normalize and Clamp rescale
the rendering gradient, while KNN propagates features from nearby high-contribution Gaussians.
None of these alternatives matches VODA. All three substantially degrade volumetric performance for
OpenGaussian, while for Gaussian Grouping, Normalize improves LERF performance but severely
degrades Removal-3D. These failures reflect a limitation of contribution-based heuristics. Rendering
contribution alone does not indicate whether a Gaussian is associated with the target object. Reweight-
ing therefore cannot identify the relevant low-contribution Gaussians or recover those that receive no
rendered gradient. Indeed, across the six analyzed scenes, 8.5–18.7% of recovered Gaussians receive
zero contribution in every training view. KNN avoids this zero-gradient issue, but spatial proximity
likewise does not establish object association and may propagate features across object boundaries.
5
Conclusion
VODA closes the gap between rendered-mask accuracy and volumetric completeness by group-
ing contributors by depth and directly supervising every Gaussian in selected clusters. We intro-
duce Removal-3D and RDE to measure selection completeness from depth changes after object
removal. Across four baselines, VODA improves applicable standard benchmarks and both mRDE
and rendered-mask mIoU on Removal-3D. Reweighting and spatial propagation fail to match VODA,
suggesting that reaching low-contribution Gaussians matters more than scaling rendered gradients.
Limitations.
Depth clustering may merge contacting objects or fragment objects with sparse
interiors, and the pixel-level push only partly addresses these cases. Because occluded Gaussians
lack feature-level ground truth, we assess their supervision indirectly through downstream selection
and removal. Exact volumetric evaluation requires paired full-scene and object-removed depth maps,
available only for the three synthetic Removal-3D scenes.
