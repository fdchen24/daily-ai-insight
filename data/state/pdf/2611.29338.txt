Abstract
Recently, GPT-6 Astra demonstrates strong generaliza-
tion in robot control, yet limited disclosure of its train-
ing leaves open how such capabilities can be reproduced.
We introduce nanoAstra, an approach to training vision-
language models (VLMs) for generalizable manipulation
that combines semantic action modeling with capability-
preserving supervised fine-tuning (CP-SFT). nanoAstra
represents robot behavior as semantic steps composed of
reusable, parameterized action primitives, guided by ex-
plicit progress descriptions. Meanwhile, CP-SFT uses an
explicit robot-specific system prompt to constrain manip-
ulation outputs to a structured action space, aiming to
focus adaptation on robot-specific contexts and promote
specialization while minimizing interference with broader
pretrained capabilities.
With this formulation, we train
nanoAstra on more than 5,000 hours of robot data together
with general VQA data. On RoboTwin 2.0’s randomized
benchmark, nanoAstra-9B achieves a 17.8% zero-shot suc-
cess rate, substantially outperforming advanced VLA and
WAM baselines as well as similarly sized VLMs. With in-
ference faster than the corresponding motion execution on
a single H20 GPU, our model supports action-chunk-like
streaming execution that overlaps inference with motion, re-
ducing the robot’s idle time from 35.0% to 6.9%.

We hypothesize that

Many existing poli-
cies [14, 31], as shown in Fig. 1, predict action chunks [40]
composed of densely sampled control steps, such as
[(0.01, −0.03, . . . , 1.00), (0.02, −0.04, . . . , 1.00), . . .],
where each vector specifies motion and gripper commands
at a sampled instant.
Although physically well-defined,
these targets do not explicitly identify coherent motion
units or their roles in the task. As a result, they lack an
easily learnable and reusable structure for representing
behaviors. The same underlying behavior can yield differ-
ent sequences depending on control frequency, execution
speed, and local corrections. Learning from such super-
vision requires models to recover reusable behavior from
fragmented trajectory samples, potentially encouraging
dependence on demonstration-specific execution patterns.
In this paper, we present nanoAstra, an approach to
training VLMs for generalizable manipulation through two
complementary designs: semantic action modeling and CP-
SFT. As shown in Fig. 1, semantic action modeling orga-
nizes robot behavior from densely sampled control steps
into semantic steps, grouping reusable, parameterized ac-
tion primitives under explicit progress guidance.
Each
primitive specifies the acting arm, a motion type, and its
geometric parameters, as in move x +5.0, or a gripper
operation such as gripper open. The distinction lies
in both the content and granularity of supervision. We ex-
tract decision points from motion and gripper transitions,
organizing demonstrations into behavior-based steps rather
than uniformly sampled control targets.
Observations at
these decision points are paired with structured responses
containing progress descriptions and executable action se-
quences. This changes what constitutes a training exam-
ple: the model learns to select and parameterize meaning-
ful motion units in context. We hypothesize that this orga-
nization can reduce sensitivity to incidental trajectory de-
tails and promote the reuse of familiar primitives across dif-
ferent tasks and environments. Moreover, high-level task
instructions alone often lack sufficient detail about inter-
mediate goals and execution steps, providing limited guid-
ance for determining the next action. To address this, dense
progress descriptions specify what the robot should accom-
plish, while action primitives define how it should execute
the task.
The second design aims to equip VLMs with manipula-
tion skills while largely preserving their general VQA capa-
bilities. To this end, we adopt CP-SFT to acquire manipu-
lation skills while minimizing interference with the VLM’s
pretrained capabilities. A robot-specific system prompt de-
fines the embodiment, coordinate conventions, and exe-
cutable action space, framing manipulation as a context-
conditioned vision-language task. This conditioning is in-
tended to focus adaptation on manipulation-relevant behav-
iors while limiting interference with the VLM’s broader
pretrained capabilities. Besides, we jointly train on robot
demonstrations and general VQA data, providing continued
supervision for visual understanding and grounding along-
side action learning.
This design aims to specialize the
model’s behavior under robotic contexts while mitigating
catastrophic forgetting [17, 21] of its broader capabilities.
With this formulation, we train nanoAstra on more
than 5,000 hours of robot data together with general VQA
data, using the VLM’s native text-generation interface with-
out a separate action-prediction head. On RoboTwin 2.0’s
randomized benchmark, nanoAstra-9B achieves a 17.8%
zero-shot success rate, outperforming advanced VLA and
WAM baselines as well as similarly sized VLMs. On a
single H20 GPU, inference is faster than the correspond-
ing motion execution, enabling action-chunk-like stream-
ing execution that overlaps inference with motion to re-
duce waiting time and improve policy execution efficiency.
These findings support nanoAstra as a promising approach
to training VLMs as robot manipulation foundation models.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We develop a data pipeline that curates and transforms
more than 5,000 hours of heterogeneous robot demonstra-
tions into VLM-compatible supervision, organizing be-
havior into executable semantic steps with dense progress
descriptions.
• We introduce CP-SFT and a joint data recipe as an initial
exploration of training VLMs toward robot manipulation
foundation models, aiming to acquire manipulation skills
while largely preserving pretrained capabilities.
• We

streaming inference. These findings provide initial evi-

els and offer a reproducible path toward the generalizable

Data Filtering.
We filter demonstrations at both episode
and frame levels to obtain reliable supervision for seman-
tic step construction.
At the episode level, we discard
recordings with corrupted timing or content, such as abnor-
mal frame rates or durations, timestamp gaps, video–state
desynchronization, missing videos, or invalid values [32].
We also discard episodes whose states and actions are in-
consistent, whose arm or gripper signals are physically im-
plausible, in which the mobile base moves beyond what
our arm-only action interface can express, or whose cam-
era extrinsics fail quality checks and cannot be reliably re-
calibrated, e.g., when projected arm silhouettes or marker
points still deviate substantially from their observed image
locations (Fig. 3). At the frame level, we mask redundant
idle segments before, after, and between motions (pauses
longer than 0.5 s), together with action outliers, jerky mo-
tions, abrupt signal spikes [27, 32], and extreme values that
would distort normalization; an episode is dropped only
when such frames dominate it. Filtering at both levels re-
moves unusable recordings while retaining valid portions
for constructing temporally coherent action steps.

A shared action
vocabulary requires consistent geometric meanings for its
parameters. We standardize reference-frame conventions,
gripper-axis definitions, and pose representations for end-
effector states and action targets across datasets.

Segmentation
with
Dense
Captions.
An
episode-level instruction often omits the intermediate
goals of a long sequence. We use gripper transitions and
manipulation events to identify temporally localized oper-
ation segments, then annotate their associated video clips
with Qwen3.5-122B-A10B [24]. The captions specify the
action, manipulated object, and target, yielding a semantic
timeline aligned with the demonstration. These temporally
localized captions serve as dense progress descriptions for
the subsequent action steps: each step is associated with a
local goal that explains its role in the overall task.
Semantic
Step
Construction.
Subtasks
remain
too
coarse to serve as action units: grasping an object, for ex-
ample, can involve reaching toward it and then closing the
gripper. We therefore segment each episode using the uni-
fied end-effector trajectories and gripper states, placing cuts
at gripper transitions, pauses, sharp turns, and large wrist ro-
tations. Subtask boundaries are always retained as cuts, so
no step spans two subtasks. Each interval defines a coher-
ent movement and/or gripper operation, and its start frame
serves as a decision point for observation and action pre-
diction. Sampling at these points reduces redundant super-
vision from nearly identical control frames and organizes
targets around changes in behavior.
We express each interval as a semantic step composed
of reusable, parameterized action primitives for translation,
rotation, gripper control, and waiting.
Commands such
as move x +5.0 and gripper open explicitly spec-
ify motion types and their associated parameters. Each step
contains separate command lists for the left and right arms;
commands within an arm’s list specify a combined mo-
tion, and the two arms execute concurrently. An inactive
arm is represented by wait. For each training sample, the
structured response contains the current progress descrip-
tion in progress and an ordered list of executable se-
mantic steps in steps. This representation exposes shared
motion structure across demonstrations while retaining the
geometric arguments required to specify execution targets.

2.2. Capability-Preserving Supervised Fine-Tuning
We develop CP-SFT to acquire manipulation skills while
aiming to largely preserve the pretrained capabilities that
may support generalization. CP-SFT imposes a soft con-
straint on robot-conditioned outputs and supplements cross-
entropy with numerical supervision for action prediction.

the VLM predicts a task progress g and executable semantic
steps A. We define the structured response space as

3. Experiments
3.1. Settings
We initialize nanoAstra from Qwen3.5-9B [24] and imple-
ment training with ms-swift [41]. We train for one epoch
on 56 H20 GPUs using the data mixture in Sec. 2.1. The
effective global batch size is 56 data packs (∼188 original
samples on average) per optimizer step, with 1 gradient-
accumulation step. We use the AdamW optimizer [20] with
a peak learning rate of 1 × 10−5, a cosine learning-rate
schedule, a warmup ratio of 0.01, and weight decay of 0.1.
The maximum sequence length is 8,192 tokens, the image-
resolution budget is 392 visual tokens per image, and the
training precision is bfloat16 (mixed precision, DeepSpeed
ZeRO-2 [26]). The trainable parameter scope is full fine-
tuning of the entire 9B vision-language model.
3.2. Benchmarks
Zero-Shot Manipulation.
We evaluate on 50 tasks in
RoboTwin 2.0’s randomized setting [6], averaging success
rates over 10 runs per task to reduce evaluation variabil-
ity.
nanoAstra is trained exclusively on real-world ma-
nipulation data, without simulated trajectories, RoboTwin
demonstrations, or benchmark-specific fine-tuning; evalua-
tion is therefore zero-shot under the randomized configura-
tion. The model runs in closed loop: each query, termed a
turn, takes the current observation and returns a reply con-
taining the current progress and the semantic steps to ex-
ecute; an episode ends when the task succeeds or the turn
budget is exhausted.

Table 1 compares VLA, WAM, and VLM-
based models under the zero-shot randomized protocol on
RoboTwin 2.0. nanoAstra-9B achieves the highest suc-
cess rate of 17.8%, exceeding both the strongest evaluated
VLA, LingBot-VLA-2.0, and the strongest VLM baseline,
Qwen3.8-27B with thinking, by 7.0 percentage points. The
evaluated WAMs achieve at most 0.2%, highlighting the dif-
ficulty of transfer under this protocol.
Thinking improves every evaluated Qwen model, raising
success rates from 3.2–4.0% without thinking to 8.2–10.8%
with thinking. However, thinking generates additional rea-
soning tokens before action outputs, increasing autoregres-
sive decoding work and potentially delaying action execu-
tion in closed-loop control. Nevertheless, nanoAstra-9B
reaches 17.8% without thinking, outperforming Qwen3.5-
9B by 14.6 points without thinking and 9.6 points with
thinking, as well as the larger evaluated Qwen models.
By directly generating structured action responses with-
out an explicit thinking trace, nanoAstra avoids this ad-
ditional reasoning-token overhead while achieving higher
task success.
These results provide initial evidence that
training VLMs with semantic action supervision and CP-
SFT can improve zero-shot manipulation beyond the evalu-
ated general-purpose VLM baselines.

Streaming Inference.
When execution blocks on infer-
ence, the latency of a VLM forces the robot into a stop-
and-go pattern: it moves, then waits for the next reply. On
a single H20, inference is already shorter than the motion
it produces, so the two can overlap. We therefore adopt
streaming execution (Fig. 6): steps are executed as they ar-
rive in the token stream, and the next reply is requested be-
fore the current one has finished executing. At H20 latency,
this reduces the share of time the robot spends waiting from
35.0% to 6.9% with no loss in success rate (Tab. 3). Infer-
ence thus no longer interrupts execution: the robot moves
almost continuously through the task rather than pausing at
every turn.

3.6. Failure Analysis
Figure 8 presents two representative failure cases, both of
which make substantial progress before exhausting the turn
budget. In Fig. 8(a), the predicted progress largely follows
the grasp–open–release cycle of the microwave door. How-
ever, each opening step is executed as straight-line transla-
tions, and the door opens only partially rather than reaching
the success threshold.
In Fig. 8(b), the model places the can in the basket early
(Turn 2), but its progress then returns to the completed sub-
task (Turn 6). Although it later moves on to grasping the
basket (Turn 14), its progress drifts again to a distractor shoe
(Turn 35), so the basket is never lifted. These cases indicate
that errors in motion parameterization and task-state esti-
mation can accumulate during closed-loop execution. We
hypothesize that object-centric primitives for articulated ob-
jects and explicit tracking of completed subtasks could fur-
ther improve long-horizon manipulation.
4. Related Work
4.1. Foundation Models for Robotic Manipulation
VLA models transfer pretrained visual and linguistic
knowledge to robot control, as exemplified by RT-2 [44],
OpenVLA [16], π0.5 [14], X-VLA [42], and StarVLA [8].
However, action prediction alone does not ensure semantic
alignment across heterogeneous data, even when policies
perform well within their training domains. WAMs learn
8
physical dynamics through joint video and action predic-
tion, as exemplified by DreamZero [31], LingBot-VA [18],
Motus [4], and Fast-WAM [33]. However, predicted visual
dynamics remain tied to embodiment, even when video-
based learning improves transfer.
Recently, VLM-based
agents, such as RoboDawn [12] and Robo-Harness K1 [19],
leverage pretrained VLMs [3, 23] for manipulation, show-
ing early signs of generalization across embodiments, tasks,
and environments. These advances motivate exploring how
manipulation capabilities can be acquired through direct
VLM training.
4.2. Data and Data Pipelines for Embodied Models
Large-scale datasets expand the diversity of embodied ex-
perience, including robot collections such as Open X-
Embodiment [22], DROID [15], AgiBot [1], and ABC-
130K [2].
UMI [7], HiFi-UMI [29], and HY-UMI [36]
further broaden data collection through hand-held manip-
ulation interfaces. However, scale and diversity alone do
not resolve cross-source differences in embodiment, motion
representation, temporal granularity, and task semantics.

VPP2 [13] uses detailed captions and event-level manipu-
lation videos to support video pretraining for WAMs. For
VLA training, Qwen-RobotManip [32] harmonizes hetero-

learning while aiming to preserve pretrained capabilities.
5. Conclusion
We present nanoAstra, an initial exploration of train-
ing VLMs as foundation models for robotic manipula-
tion. Combining semantic action primitives with CP-SFT,
nanoAstra learns from over 5,000 hours of robot experi-
ence and general vision-language supervision. It achieves
promising zero-shot manipulation while largely retaining
pretrained capabilities, despite some degradation. Fast in-
ference enables streaming execution that overlaps inference
with motion, improving policy execution efficiency. To-
gether, these findings support structured action semantics
and capability-aware adaptation as a practical path toward
generalizable and efficient manipulation foundation mod-
els.
