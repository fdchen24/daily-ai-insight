Abstract
Video world models keep the world in the context of a generator, so a revisit is synthesized
again and earlier edits can drift or disappear. We present WMMLLM, the first interactive
world model built on multimodal large language models (MLLMs), for persistent, editable
world generation from a single image. WMMLLM keeps the world state outside every gen-
erator, as a panorama with a revision history, and lets the MLLMs write it: they turn each
request into operations on this state, select where a local edit is written, and check every
candidate before it becomes the next state. Image generators only propose views, and video
only presents them. Users can walk to new places and return, edit objects through text or
a box, place subjects, and change lighting, weather or season. By construction, a revisit at
the same revision reproduces the earlier view exactly, a local edit leaves every panorama
pixel outside its region unchanged, and each request starts from the result of the previous
one. Against six world model baselines on MBench-A, WMMLLM has the highest mean on
all four environment-recovery metrics (Epipolar 91.5 versus 0.1 to 74.0) and completes the
most camera round trips (85.3% versus at most 50.9%). It has the best value in all 15
CompBench columns against 15 baselines. On 112 local edits judged blind, selecting the
region to write after generation raises completion from 34.8% to 83.0%. On 258 WBench
and MBench-A items, all 46 revisits recover their earlier views, and all 97 local edits that
pass the check leave panorama pixels outside their region unchanged.

1 Introduction
A user opens a container, relights the room and turns away. Turning back should show the open container
under the new light (figure 1).
A world model keeps a persistent state of its environment that actions
update [1], so an interactive world model needs three properties: a revisit shows the content saved there
(return), a local edit changes nothing outside its region (locality), and every request builds on the result of
the previous one (persistence).
Where the world lives decides whether these properties hold.
Video world models generate observations
from camera moves, actions and event prompts, and streaming variants extend them to long, interactive
rollouts [2–5]. Their world lives in the context of the generator, so a revisit synthesizes the view again and
earlier edits can drift or disappear; after one camera round trip, five baselines, all video world models, end
on a different scene (figure 1, bottom row). 3D world models build an explicit scene, such as a mesh or a
set of Gaussian surfels, that a renderer displays from new viewpoints and that supports regional edits [6–8].
Among the systems with an explicit world state in table 1, none documents all four of an MLLM planner,
object edits, exact return and checks of an edit before it enters the state. World models for control, such
as vision-language-action models [9, 10] and joint-embedding predictive architectures [11], output actions or
embeddings and no editable images.
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) and image generators such as Nano Banana and GPT-Image [12–
14] understand a scene, follow detailed instructions and accept both text and image inputs. We investigate
whether these models can be turned into an interactive world model that users keep editing and exploring.
WMMLLM turns MLLMs into such a world model by keeping the world state outside every generator and
letting the MLLMs write it (figure 2). For each scene revision, the state stores a panorama and patches at
native resolution; it also stores scene relations and facts observed in images. The MLLM GPT-6.1-sol [15]

A local edit is written inside a region, its support, and WMMLLM selects this support after generation. An
image generator can move a subject or change its silhouette beyond a region predicted in advance, so such a
region can cut off part of the change (figure 4). An MLLM therefore compares the source and the proposal
and selects the old and new footprints and necessary effects.
WMMLLM achieves state-of-the-art scene consistency and camera return and the best CompBench editing
scores. Against six world model baselines under the same MBench evaluator, it has the highest mean on
all four environment-recovery metrics, with Epipolar 91.5 versus 0.1 to 74.0. It completes 85.3% of camera
round trips versus 0.4% to 50.9% (table 3). On CompBench, it has the best value in all 15 metric columns
against 15 baselines (table 4).

• The first interactive world model built on MLLMs. WMMLLM composes navigation, object editing, envi-

every generator, and view reuse, compositing inside the support and lookup by revision give return,

2 Related Work
2.1 World Representation and Memory
Video world models maintain interaction by conditioning new frames on actions and visual context. Ge-
nie 3 supports navigation and promptable events [2], and LongLive updates its context cache when the
prompt changes [5]. Camera-aware context improves spatial continuity through memory retrieval in Matrix-
Game 3.0 [3], RGB-D generation in HunyuanWorld-Voyager [17], and reframing of historical observations in
HY-WorldPlay [4]. WorldDirector adds planning of object and camera trajectories by a language model and
preserves the appearance of dynamic objects that leave and return [18]. Genie 4 uses Gemini models to plan
and verify steps on the frames it generates [19].
Memory extends the visual context available to these generators. WorldMem retrieves frames by pose and
time [20], Context-as-Memory by field-of-view overlap [21], and VMem through a surfel index [22]. Alaya-
EVOKE maintains a geometry bank indexed by camera [23], while ActWorld stores event and object tokens
with visual anchors in a memory of constant capacity [24]. Other approaches use spatial or planar memories
or compress visual context [25–28]. Persistent Computational State saves and restores the generator’s runtime
state [29]. These memories condition a new synthesis on return.
Explicit 3D worlds support rendering and editing through scene representations. HunyuanWorld 1.0 recon-
structs a mesh from a panoramic proxy [6], and WonderWorld connects scenes through Gaussian surfels [7].
Marble edits and expands persistent navigable worlds by region, including weather, time of day and sea-
son [8].

2.2 MLLM Planning and Feedback
Visual agents connect language planning, image generation and feedback. Yang et al.’s WorldGPT expands
a request, edits a target image and animates the initial and final frames [35].
GenArtist plans through
tool trees [36], and EditAgent routes steps to general and task-specific editors and then judges parallel
candidates [37]. Agent Banana retains the editing paths that pass its checks, rolls back image history and
edits layers in isolation [38].
IMAGAgent corrects multi-turn edits through feedback from several VLM
experts [39]. Ge et al.’s WorldGPT learns multimodal state transitions [40], and World Agent tests whether
language models update an explicit world state from events in an ongoing narrative [41].
Agentic video systems connect this feedback to worlds that change over time. LingBot-World v2 uses a
vision-language director for semantic rules, events and environmental changes, with SAM tracking objects
for interaction [42]. Regions that leave its context window tend to be regenerated on return. AgiWorld
updates a world state from video chunks stored with geometry in spatial memory [43]. ATAS adds clips to
scene and event memories after review and state checks [44]. WMMLLM gives MLLMs control over edits and
navigation in a panorama state, and its transition videos read the endpoints of each turn.
2.3 Image Editing and Consistency
Image editors such as Nano Banana and GPT-Image follow complex instructions [12–14]. Spherical editors
extend this capability to panoramas. SE360 and World-Shaper edit spherical imagery [45, 46], FocusDiff edits
regions of indoor panoramas [47], and Mover360 moves, inserts and removes objects [48]. ZONE extracts the
region that an edit changes as a layer and blends it into the source image [49]. WMMLLM selects the support
from the source and the proposal to cover the old and new footprints of an object and their effects. It installs
this region in a world that later operations navigate, revisit and edit again.
CompBench measures local, multi-object and implicit-reasoning edits [50]. Scene-memory benchmarks mea-
sure recovery after the camera leaves and returns [51, 52], while WRBench tests whether events evolve outside
the view [53]. WorldFoundry provides infrastructure for inference and evaluation across video, 3D, 4D and
interactive world systems [1]. We evaluate both image editing and scene recovery. Table 1 compares world
representations, MLLM planning, object edits, exact return and edit checks.

3 Method
WMMLLM stores each scene as a panorama with local patches and a revision history. A deterministic ren-
derer produces its views (figure 2). GPT-6.1-sol plans operations, selects the support of local edits, checks
candidates and records observations. GPT-Image proposes endpoint images, and Sol-H3 generates transition
videos. The executor applies each operation through one of five routes, and each route changes a fixed part
of the state (table 2). The state after each turn becomes the source of the next turn, and these update rules
give return, locality and persistence.
3.1 Problem Formulation
A session is a sequence of requests u1, . . . , uT , each a free-form instruction with an optional box on the current
view. A world model keeps a state Wt and a camera Ct, shows the view ot, and answers each request with a
new state, camera and view and a transition video,
(Wt+1, Ct+1, ot+1) = F(Wt, Ct, ut),

persistence (section 1). In a video world model, one generator computes both F and Present, so these
properties depend on what it reproduces from its context.

3.4 Rotation and Return
Rotation renders the current revision at a new orientation and calls no image or video generator. A revisit
reuses a view in memory only when its physical revision, view identity, camera and rendering profile match.
The executor checks that the hashes of the panorama state and arrival view agree (figure 2b), and a return
from another scene is also compared with a render at the target profile.

3.6 Video Presentation
Presentation never writes the state. Rotations and revisits around the same center render the panorama along
a camera path between the two views with equation (3). Other transitions, including returns from another
scene, use Sol-H3 conditioned on the source and endpoint views and the request caption. SoL-Refiner [54]
can optionally super-resolve the video. A failure hold repeats the current view. A view at rest can also play
as a loop: Sol-H3 takes the same view as its first and last frame, with a caption that names motion in place.
A loop writes nothing to the state, and no benchmark uses one.
3.7 Properties
Three properties follow from table 2 and equation (5). A rotation or revisit reads its endpoint from the state,
a local edit writes only inside its support, and each request starts from the preceding endpoint.
Proposition 1 (Return). A revisit that completes with the same physical revision, view identity, camera and
rendering profile reproduces the earlier image pixel for pixel. Proof. The revisit reuses the image in the state
after checking that the hashes of the panorama state and arrival view agree; a revisit from another scene is
also compared with a deterministic render of the same state, camera and profile.
Proposition 2 (Locality). A local update keeps every panorama pixel with At = 0. With B, the earlier patches,
the camera and the profile fixed, it also keeps every pixel of the render whose ray has zero alpha in the new
patch. Proof. Equation (7) copies Et where At = 0, including its 8-bit values, and the executor aborts on
any violation. For a ray with zero alpha, the renderer skips the new patch, so cK+1 = cK.
Proposition 3 (Persistence).
Each request starts from the endpoint of the preceding request, and a view
lookup never installs a different physical revision. Proof. Retrieval and Commit require the source to equal
the preceding endpoint, and the executor advances the source and visual state after each request. A hold
keeps the visual state and camera, presentation never replaces the visual state, and the lookup rejects views
of another revision.
4 Experiments
We use two public benchmarks, ablations on fixed image proposals and session studies. MBench-A measures
environment memory [51]; 224 of our 227 environment items have a video from all six baselines, public world
models conditioned on actions. CompBench [50] measures image editing on 2,709 GPT-Image proposals
across local, multi-object and implicit-reasoning edits. We score all methods with the same public MBench
evaluator, which preserves the ranking of the six baselines on 30 items per baseline. The benchmark run
contains 158 WBench [55] navigation items and 100 MBench-A causal items, with 817 segments. Two paired
studies with sessions of eight turns examine observation memory, the planner, the source of the next state
and the endpoint check. A blind Claude Opus 5.5 judge assesses camera motion, edits and session outcomes
from frame samples. Appendices A2 and A3.3 give the protocols.
4.1 MBench-A Memory and Camera Return
We first test scene recovery after the camera leaves and returns (figure 5, top row): WMMLLM has the
highest mean on all four environment-recovery metrics against the six baselines (table 3). Its Epipolar mean
is 91.5, 95% confidence interval [89.6, 93.1], against 0.1 to 74.0, and on all four metrics its interval does not
overlap that of any baseline. This result comes from reusing the state exactly: of the 454 segments in the
227 environment items, 88.3% are geometric (215 rotations, 186 scene restores). On the 191 items whose
WMMLLM segments contain no hold or terminal failure, Epipolar is still 93.9 versus at most 74.5, so holds
do not explain the lead.

4.2 Image Editing
WMMLLM has the best value in all 15 CompBench columns, five metrics on three editing tasks, against 15
baselines in 16 configurations (table 4). The baseline scores are those that CompBench reports, with HIVE
in two configurations. LC-T is the local CLIP text score ×100, and LC-I is the foreground CLIP similarity
to the target. PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS measure background preservation. The LC-I of WMMLLM is 0.845,
0.911 and 0.880 on the local, multi-object and implicit-reasoning groups, against 0.838, 0.883 and 0.874
for BAGEL, the strongest baseline on this measure. Because edits stay inside their support, background
preservation shows the largest margins: PSNR is 30.440, 29.104 and 32.868 against at most 27.692, 26.849
and 28.891 for the baselines. Table 4 lists five of the baselines, including the strongest one of every column,
and direct editing, which keeps the whole GPT-Image proposal.

4.4 Verification of the State Properties
The benchmark run meets the properties of section 3.7 in every case. All 46 revisits match their panorama
state and arrival view hashes, and all 195 geometric segments run without a generator. Of the 112 local edits,
97 pass the verifier, and all 97 leave every panorama pixel outside their support unchanged (equation (7));
in 92, the judge finds that the region matches the request (appendix A4). Over 21 sequential requests in one
world, the state keeps every earlier change, and requests 1–20 change only 10.7% of the panorama (figure 8).
Figure A5 shows five more worlds with 20 or 21 sequential requests each. An agent writes each request of
these sessions from the current state, and candidates that fail the checks are generated again, so these sessions
do not measure success at the first attempt.

4.6 Cost and Latency
The median time for state processing is 120 s for rotation, 215 s for navigation and 267 s for multiple op-
erations. Sol-H3 generates a 124-frame clip in a median of 71.9 s on four H100 GPUs. In the multi-turn
component study (appendix A3.3), with two A6000 GPUs per job, the full system takes a median of 609.4 s
from request to first playback. Once a state is committed, every view of it renders from equation (3) without
a model call. Appendix A4 defines these measurements.
5 Limitations
The main limitation is that WMMLLM does not complete every request: 276 of the 280 failure holds of the
benchmark run follow a rejection of the final endpoint, and these holds raise appearance scores while the
motion in the request is missing. The endpoint verifier also passes some partial changes and rejects some
complete ones: a blind judge labels as complete 190 of the 309 endpoints that pass the verifier and 83 of the
276 that it rejects (appendix A4). Because each request starts from the endpoint of the preceding one, an
error carries forward: the next segment completes in 100 of 153 cases after a correct endpoint commits and
in 25 of 95 after an incorrect one. Weak camera motion and incorrect placement relations also cause failures.
Figure 9 shows a motorcycle that stays beside the slide and a coastal view that shifts in the wrong direction.
The panorama represents a world around one camera center (appendix A4). Translation and unseen re-
gions require generation, and the generation log flags a seam in 208 of the 227 environment items. Video
presentation also takes a median of about ten minutes from request to first playback (section 4.6).
The endpoint verifier and the blind judge lack human calibration, and frame samples do not establish the
motion between two samples. The session studies have wide intervals and use new scripts on worlds from
earlier studies.
6 Conclusion
WMMLLM turns MLLMs into a persistent, editable world model by keeping the world state outside every
generator: the MLLMs write it under update rules that give return, locality and persistence, and generators
only propose. Its panorama state carries object edits, environmental changes and navigation across turns, and
over 21 sequential requests in one world it keeps every earlier change. Selecting the support after generation
raises the completion of local edits from 34.8% to 83.0%. WMMLLM achieves the best mean on all four
MBench-A recovery metrics against six baselines, completes the most camera round trips and has the best
value in all 15 CompBench columns.

LLM Usage Statement
In our system, GPT-6.1-sol [15] plans operations, selects the support of local edits, checks endpoint candi-
dates and records observations. GPT-Image 2 [13] supplies the CompBench proposals, GPT-Image 2.5 [14]
generates the endpoints in the other experiments, and GPT-Image also generates the initial panoramas.
Sol-H3 [16] generates the transition videos, and SoL-Refiner [54] optionally super-resolves them. Diagnos-
tic images from earlier development also used Qwen-Image-Edit-2511. In the evaluation, Claude Opus 5.5
supplies all blind judgments and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct supplies the WBench [55] judge fields described in
appendix A2.2; no human raters take part. Claude Code and OpenAI Codex serve as coding and writing
assistants: they help to implement the system and the evaluation scripts, run the experiments, analyze the
results, prepare the figures and tables, and draft and edit the text of this report. The authors direct and
check this work and are responsible for the methods, results and final manuscript.
