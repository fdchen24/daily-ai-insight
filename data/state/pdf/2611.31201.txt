Abstract
Frontier multimodal models exhibit extraordinary gener-
alization in generating robot actions from observations
and instructions, enabling a new paradigm in which they
serve directly as policies within closed-loop robotic har-
nesses. However, the design principles of such harnesses,

and the capabilities underlying their strong performance,
remain unclear. We present Embo-Harness, a framework
for systematically studying frontier-model-as-policy har-
nesses. We investigate four components of the robotic agen-
tic loop, namely, prompting, planning, action execution,
and tool use, and distill six empirical findings into a practi-
cal recipe. This recipe prioritizes interfaces that exploit the
model’s native visual reasoning and tool-use capabilities

1. Introduction
Recent advances in frontier models, such as GPT-6-
Astra [49], are reshaping robotic manipulation [62, 74].
Their remarkable generalization capabilities enable them to
handle complex instructions, diverse environments, long-
horizon manipulation, and failure recovery, substantially
outperforming existing robot foundation models (RFMs),
such as Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) [6, 36, 38,
67, 78] and World Action Models (WAMs) [42, 71, 73], on
recent benchmarks [14, 46, 68, 77], offering a different pos-
sibility for future robotic policies.
A robotic harness is the software loop that connects
observations and instructions to planning, tool use, ac-
tion generation, execution, and feedback.
As illustrated
in Figure 2, existing robotic harnesses can be broadly di-
vided according to how actions are produced.
Tool- or
code-as-policy [16, 20, 24, 29, 31, 33, 34, 44, 65] har-
nesses use multimodal models [3, 27] for high-level plan-
ning but execute actions through predefined tools or gen-
erated code.
Model-as-policy [56, 58, 75] harnesses in-
stead delegate action generation to specialized learned poli-
cies. Recent frontier-model-as-policy harnesses take a fur-
ther step by using the frontier model itself to plan and gen-
erate robot actions within a closed loop. Recent studies
have established the feasibility of this paradigm [15, 43, 61–
63, 74]. For example, SenseNova-RoboRSI [62] improves
a frontier-model policy through recursive self-improvement
based on prior training datasets and test-time execution roll-
outs, while GPT-as-Policy [61] and related studies [17, 49]
profile the native robotic capabilities of frontier models.
However, these works primarily demonstrate or improve the
performance of particular systems. They do not systemat-
ically study which harness components are responsible for
the gains, or why general-purpose frontier models can out-
perform policies specialized for robotic control.
We address these questions through Embo-Harness,
a controlled study for frontier-model-as-policy harnesses.

Starting from a common baseline, we vary individual design
factors and progressively integrate the effective choices,
rather than optimizing the complete system as a single

Our study covers four dimensions of the
robotic agentic loop: prompting, planning, action execu-
tion, and tool use. The resulting six findings can be summa-
rized into three principles. First, suitable action representa-
tions and execution strategies are essential: frontier models
more effectively generate end-effector actions than joint-
space actions and favor short, fixed-length action chunks
over adaptive chunking. Second, their performance bene-
fits substantially from stronger reasoning and richer visual
observations, including higher resolutions and complemen-
tary viewpoints. Third, tools and learned policies should
complement the model’s native capabilities rather than re-
place them: manually designed plans and failure-derived
reminders provide limited benefits, whereas short multi-
action sequences and VLA refinement improve effective-
ness and efficiency. Guided by this recipe, Embo-Harness
achieves an 83.3% success rate on RoboLab-120, compared
with 57.1% for SenseNova-RoboRSI, the strongest existing
baseline. It also completes 15 of 20 zero-shot trials across
four real-world manipulation tasks.
Beyond performance, we further investigate why fron-
tier models succeed as robotic policies. Although they can
generate joint-level and trajectory-level actions commonly
learned by VLAs and WAMs, explicitly requiring such gen-
eration degrades performance. In contrast, directly predict-
ing the final EEF destination outperforms short-range action
generation, suggesting a stronger reliance on spatial percep-
tion than low-level action generation. We further validate

ments, observing broadly aligned trends with manipulation
performance across reasoning efforts and visual resolutions,
as well as comparable errors between direct position esti-
mates and gripper positions in failed grasps, both measured
against ground-truth target positions. These findings further
highlight the central role of spatial perception in frontier
models’ robotic capabilities.
2. Revisiting Agentic Robotics
Robot policies map task instructions and observations to ex-
ecutable actions [2, 40, 47, 51]. Traditionally, these poli-
cies relied on handcrafted rules and specialized tools [8,
19, 59, 60], while recent advances in neural networks and
large-scale robot data have enabled learned policies [7, 18,
26, 55, 76] and robot foundation models (RFMs), including
Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) [6, 36, 38, 67, 78]
and World Action Models (WAMs) [42, 71, 73].
Beyond standalone policies, agentic robotics employs
software-based harnesses to coordinate perception, plan-
ning, action execution, and tool use within a closed-loop
framework. As illustrated in Fig. 2, existing robotic har-
nesses can be broadly categorized into three paradigms
based on how robot actions are generated.
Tool/code-as-policy.
These harnesses typically leverage
vision-language models for perception and high-level plan-
ning, while relying on predefined tools or generated code
for action execution [10, 12, 16, 20, 21, 24, 25, 29–
31, 33, 34, 41, 44, 57, 65, 72].
Such tools include ob-
ject detection [48], segmentation [9, 39, 52], grasp detec-
tion [11, 22, 66], and motion planning [19, 60]. Code-as-
Policy methods further enable flexible composition and in-
vocation of these tools through code [24, 25, 29, 44].
Model-as-policy. Rather than relying solely on predefined
tools, these harnesses delegate action generation to learned
policy models, while VLMs coordinate high-level planning,
execution, and feedback [28, 32, 35, 53, 56, 58, 64, 70, 75].
By integrating specialized policy models into the agentic
loop, these approaches combine high-level reasoning with
learned low-level control .
Frontier-model-as-policy.
Recent advances in frontier
models, such as GPT-6-Astra, have introduced a new
paradigm in which frontier models directly generate robot
actions, integrating perception, planning, and action gener-
ation within a unified agentic loop [15, 17, 37, 43, 45, 61–
63, 74]. Recent studies show that frontier models exhibit
remarkable generalization, outperforming several VLA and
WAM baselines on robotic manipulation benchmarks [62,
74]. However, existing studies have primarily focused on
demonstrating their capabilities, leaving open how to effec-
tively design frontier-model-as-policy harnesses and what
underlying capabilities drive their performance.

3. Developing Embo-Harness
Frontier models have reshaped robotic harnesses while in-
troducing a fundamental design challenge: how to fully
leverage their capabilities within an agentic loop. Toward
this end, we systematically explore four key dimensions
of the robotic agentic loop: prompting, planning, action
execution, and tool use, as illustrated in Fig. 3.
Specif-
ically, prompting covers text prompts and visual inputs;
planning involves context management, planning strategies,
and model selection; action execution considers action rep-
resentations, action chunk sizes, and asynchronous infer-
ence; and tool use further extends the harness through com-
bined action generation and integration with robot policies.
Baseline Setup. For our exploration, we follow the ex-
perimental setup of π0.5 [36]. Specifically, we adopt abso-
lute joint positions as the action representation with an ac-
tion chunk of 15, providing only task instructions and dual-
view images at a resolution of 224 × 126 as inputs. We
use GPT-6-Astra with medium reasoning effort as our base-
line, operating in goal mode for task execution. We adopt
a progressive evaluation protocol, where each experiment
varies a single design factor while keeping all others fixed.
The best-performing configuration identified at each stage
is then carried forward to subsequent experiments, progres-
sively refining the harness recipe. The full exploration re-
sults are shown in Table 1. All experiments are conducted
on a subset of 20 tasks from the RoboLab benchmark [68],
with 3 trials per task to reduce the impact of randomness.

3.1. Action Execution
Action Format. We first investigate action execution, the
dimension most closely related to robot control, by exam-
ining whether frontier models can understand and gener-
ate different action representations and identifying the most
effective one. In robotic manipulation, two common ac-
tion representations are end-effector (EEF) actions, which
specify the spatial position of the robot end-effector, and
joint-space actions, which specify the angles of individual
robot joints. Given the robot’s kinematic parameters, EEF
actions can be converted into joint-space actions through in-
verse kinematics (IK). Both representations can be further
expressed as absolute values or relative offsets (delta) from
the previous timestep, resulting in four action formats. As
shown in Table 1, frontier models can handle all four for-
mats with comparable success rates. However, generating
joint-space actions is more challenging and incurs longer
execution times. We observe that frontier models often re-
sort to writing code to use IK to compute joint values, di-
rectly generating them in only approximately 30% of cases,
typically for simple or repeated actions. Absolute and delta
formats exhibit comparable performance and time cost.
Action Chunk. We next investigate whether frontier mod-
els can adapt their generated actions to different action
chunk sizes. We observe that frontier models tend to predict
similar target positions regardless of the specified chunk
size, suggesting limited awareness of action execution hori-
zons (detail in appendix). Consequently, larger chunks re-
sult in slower movements and may exhaust the episode step
budget, while smaller chunks may provide insufficient exe-
cution time to reach the target, requiring repeated action ex-
ecution to the same target. As shown in Table 1, a chunk size
of 8 achieves the best performance by balancing movement
speed and execution horizon, outperforming both larger
and smaller chunk selections.
Asynchronous Execution.
We next investigate whether
frontier models can support asynchronous inference, a tech-
nique commonly used in robot policies to reduce execution
latency. Specifically, asynchronous inference starts generat-
ing the next action chunk before the current chunk finishes
executing, allowing the next chunk to be ready upon com-
pletion and thereby reducing the idle time between consec-
utive chunks. For frontier models, this requires anticipat-
ing the future robot state after executing the current chunk
and generating subsequent actions accordingly. However,
Table 1 shows that frontier models struggle to anticipate
such future states and instead focus primarily on immedi-
ate movements. Therefore, we do not employ asynchronous
inference in subsequent experiments.
3.2. Planning
Model Types.
Having established the recipe for action
execution, we next turn our attention to model selection
4
and planning.
We first investigate the impact of differ-
ent frontier models and reasoning efforts.
For instance,
GPT models [49] offer different variants (e.g., 6-Astra, 6.1-
Sol, 6.1-Terra) and reasoning efforts (e.g., xhigh, high, and
medium). As shown in Table 1, stronger model variants
and higher reasoning efforts generally achieve better per-
formance. Interestingly, medium reasoning effort outper-
forms high, suggesting that more reasoning does not neces-
sarily translate into better action execution.
Explicit Planning.
We next investigate whether ex-
plicit planning improves robotic manipulation performance.
Specifically, we compare two strategies: implicit planning,
where the goal model directly plans and executes the task,
and explicit planning, where an additional model call in
plan mode decomposes the task into subtasks, which are
then sequentially executed by the goal model. As shown
in Table 1, explicit planning provides no performance im-
provement while increasing execution time due to addi-
tional planning overhead.
Context Management.
We further investigate whether
frontier models require the full interaction history for ac-
tion generation. During task execution, the interaction his-
tory continuously grows with new observations, although
earlier observations may not always be necessary for sub-
sequent actions. Our experiments show that removing his-
torical observations reduces execution time while maintain-
ing comparable performance. However, this substantially
decreases the KV-cache hit rate from over 95% to approxi-
mately 25%, resulting in a 7–8× increase in uncached token
consumption.
3.3. Prompting and Visual Inputs
Notice Reminder.
Having explored model selection
and planning, we next investigate whether additional
prompt guidance improves robotic manipulation perfor-
mance. Specifically, we employ recursive self-improvement
(RSI) to analyze failure cases from baseline rollouts and
distill 12 task-agnostic textual reminders, also removing all
task-specific information to retain only task-agnostic guid-
ance. As shown in Table 1, these reminders decrease per-
formance and also increase execution time, suggesting that
introducing failure-derived robotic skills offers limited ben-
efits to frontier models. Details of reminders are provided
in the appendix.
Visual Views. Multi-view observations are a key design
choice in robotic policies, providing complementary visual
information for manipulation. We next investigate whether
this design is equally important for frontier-model-as-policy
harnesses. Following conventional robot policies, our base-
line uses both third-person and wrist-view observations,
which we compare against using only a single third-person
view. As shown in Table 1, removing the wrist view leads
to a performance drop, highlighting the importance of com-
plementary visual viewpoints for robotic manipulation.
Visual Resolution. We next investigate the impact of vi-
sual input resolution on robotic manipulation performance.
Specifically, we evaluate different input resolutions to ex-
amine their effects on action execution. As shown in Ta-
ble 1, higher-resolution visual inputs provide finer spa-
tial details, leading to substantial improvements in harness
performance. For the remaining experiments, we adopt a
higher visual resolution of 640 × 360.
3.4. Tool and Policy Integration
Short Multi-action Sequences.
We further investigate
whether additional tools can further enhance harness per-
formance, focusing on short multi-action sequences that en-
able multiple consecutive actions to be executed together.
Specifically, we introduce an additional tool that allows
frontier models to generate multiple consecutive actions in
a single output without observing intermediate states. As
shown in Table 1, this strategy further improves both task
performance and execution speed. We further analyze the
short multi-action sequences employed during task execu-
tion and identify three common patterns: (1) approach,
grasp, and retreat; (2) lower, release, and withdraw; and (3)
repetition of previously executed action sequences. Over-
all, short multi-action sequences are used in approximately
30% of cases, with an average of three actions generated
per sequence. These results motivate the inclusion of short
multi-action sequences as a tool in our harness.
VLA Integration. Finally, we investigate how to integrate
VLAs into the harness to assist action execution. Specifi-
cally, we explore two strategies: (1) VLA selection, where
frontier models decide at each step whether to execute ac-
tions themselves or delegate execution to a VLA; and (2)
VLA refinement, where a VLA proposes an action at each
step, which frontier models then refine and execute. As
shown in Table 1, VLA selection substantially reduces ex-
ecution time but also degrades task performance. We ob-
serve that frontier models struggle to accurately assess VLA
capabilities and tend to delegate most actions to the VLA,
even when its predictions are unreliable. In contrast, VLA
refinement achieves the best performance, suggesting that
VLAs and frontier models possess complementary capabil-
ities and that refining VLA-proposed actions leads to more
effective task execution.
It is noteworthy that this result does not rule out selection
as a strategy: calibrated exposure to the VLA’s successes
and failures, or an explicit estimate of its confidence, may
enable more reliable delegation. We leave this possibility to
future work.

3.6.1. Benchmark Evaluation Results
We evaluate Embo-Harness on the full RoboLab-120
benchmark against three categories of baselines: VLAs,
WAMs, and agentic methods.
Among the agentic base-
lines, SenseNova-RoboRSI [62] is a closely related frontier-
model-as-policy approach that, like ours, employs GPT-6-
Astra for robot action generation. As shown in Table 2,
Embo-Harness achieves an SR of 83.3% and a Score of
89.2, outperforming all compared methods on both metrics
where reported. Notably, relative to SenseNova-RoboRSI,
the strongest baseline, it improves SR by 26.2 percentage
points and Score by 19.3. These results demonstrate the ef-
fectiveness of our systematically developed harness recipe,
highlighting the importance of leveraging frontier models’
native capabilities rather than imposing handcrafted robotic
priors. We note that the concurrent work AMP1 [10] also
reports strong performance. However, its reliance on an ex-
ternal high-accuracy depth estimator makes the evaluation
settings different, precluding a direct comparison.

4. Why Frontier Models Succeed in Robotics
The emergence of frontier models as competitive robot poli-
cies raises a broader question for embodied intelligence:
what capabilities enable general-purpose multimodal mod-
els, not specifically designed for low-level robot control, to
rival or even surpass specialized VLA and WAM policies?
Answering this question is important not only for under-
standing current systems, but also for determining which ca-
pabilities and training signals future robot foundation mod-
els should prioritize. We investigate three related questions:
whether frontier models encode low-level joint and trajec-
tory patterns, whether such knowledge contributes to suc-
cessful manipulation, and, if not, which more general capa-
bility accounts for their performance.
4.1. Evaluating Low-level Action Generation
To answer the first two questions, we investigate whether
frontier models exhibit characteristic biases of low-level
robotic actions and whether these biases contribute to their
manipulation capabilities.
Specifically, we consider two
levels of low-level actions: 1) trajectory-level bias, which
captures frame-to-frame motion patterns, and 2) joint-level
bias, which captures the movements of individual robot
joints. Existing VLAs and WAMs typically learn low-level
action patterns through imitation learning, with most meth-
ods modeling trajectory-level patterns with an action chunk-
ing strategy and some, such as π0.5 [36], additionally mod-
eling joint-level patterns. We therefore examine whether
frontier models exhibit these two biases and whether explic-
itly leveraging them improves manipulation performance.
Joint-level bias.
We compare direct joint-angle genera-
tion (disable inverse kinematics (IK) usage) with joint-angle
generation assisted by inverse kinematics (IK). As shown
in Table 4, direct joint control achieves a 20.0% zero-shot
success rate, indicating that frontier models possess some
understanding of joint-level actions.
However, this per-
formance is 23.3 percentage points lower than that of IK-
assisted control, suggesting that explicitly generating joint-
level actions does not improve manipulation performance.
Trajectory-level bias. We further compare explicit multi-
frame action generation, where frontier models predict an
action for each frame, with predicting a future target pose
several frames ahead. As shown in Table 4, explicit multi-
frame action generation (indicated by the row ‘direct chunk
8’) achieves a 40.0% zero-shot success rate, demonstrating
that frontier models can generate low-level trajectories. But
this approach underperforms target pose prediction by 18.3
percentage points (indicated by the row ‘chunk 8’), sug-
gesting that explicitly modeling frame-level motion patterns
also fails to improve manipulation performance.
Overall, these results show that frontier models possess
some ability to generate joint-level actions and frame-level
trajectories. However, the evaluated harness performs sub-
stantially better when the model predicts spatial targets and
delegates low-level execution to IK or trajectory interpo-
lation. This suggests that its strong manipulation perfor-
mance does not primarily depend on explicit low-level ac-
tion generation, although the model may still use implicit
action knowledge during spatial reasoning and target selec-
tion. We further find that frontier models generate similar
action displacements across different execution horizons,
indicating limited sensitivity to frame-level temporal infor-
mation. Their rollout trajectories also exhibit broader spa-
tial distributions than those of π0.5 [36], suggesting distinct

shown in Table 5, directly predicting the final destination
achieves slightly higher success rates than short-range ac-
tion generation, suggesting that spatial perception may play
a more fundamental role than low-level action generation.

4.3. Discussion
Frontier models are most effective when used to infer task-
relevant spatial targets, rather than low-level actions. Three
observations from our experiments support this view. First,
direct joint and frame-level trajectory generation is possi-
ble but consistently underperforms IK-assisted or target-
pose control.
Second, stronger reasoning and higher vi-
sual resolution reduce 3D localization errors in parallel with
improvements in task success. Third, real-world failures
are concentrated in tasks requiring precise localization and
alignment. These findings do not imply that frontier models
lack implicit action knowledge or that low-level action data
are unnecessary, as the observed performance gaps may also
reflect limitations in action interfaces and numerical gener-
ation. Nevertheless, they highlight the importance of spa-

tial perception and motivate greater emphasis on spatially
grounded models, datasets, and benchmarks. More broadly,
these insights suggest a promising direction for developing
new robot policy paradigms beyond conventional VLAs and
WAMs, potentially achieving strong manipulation perfor-
mance and efficient execution without relying heavily on
low-level action imitation.
5. Conclusion
We have introduced Embo-Harness to investigate how to
build strong frontier-model-as-policy harnesses and why
frontier models achieve remarkable robotic manipulation
performance. Through a systematic exploration of prompt-
ing, planning, action execution, and tool use, we distilled
six key findings into a practical recipe that emphasizes
leveraging frontier models’ native capabilities rather than
imposing handcrafted plans, skills, or action priors. Guided
by this recipe, Embo-Harness achieves state-of-the-art per-
formance on RoboLab-120 and demonstrates strong perfor-
mance in real-world experiments. Beyond harness design,
we further investigated the underlying capabilities of fron-
tier models, revealing that their manipulation performance
primarily stems from accurate spatial perception rather than
low-level action understanding.
These findings not only
provide practical guidance for developing stronger robotic
harnesses but also suggest a promising direction for future
robot datasets and foundation models.
Limitations and Future Research. Our findings depend
on the capabilities of current frontier models and may
evolve with future advances. While spatial perception may
enable general-purpose gripper-based manipulation, dexter-
ous manipulation, locomotion, and tactile perception re-
quire additional low-level and dynamic capabilities, pre-
senting promising directions for future research.
