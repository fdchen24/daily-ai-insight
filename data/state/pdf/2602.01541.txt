Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have made rapid progress in open-vocabulary visual
understanding, yet they often struggle with cognitive visual reasoning tasks that require maintaining,
transforming, and simulating abstract visual states. Existing approaches primarily scale Chain-of-
Thought (CoT) reasoning in language, forcing inherently spatial or visual computations into discrete
text tokens. We introduce Cognitive Supersensing, a training paradigm that equips MLLMs with latent
visual reasoning by integrating a Latent Visual Imagery Prediction (LVIP) head that progressively
learns refined visual latent states aligned with the evolving reasoning context and target answer,
thereby providing a structured visual grounding signal for internal reasoning. We further introduce
a reinforcement learning stage that optimizes rationale trajectories using both answer evidence and
LVIP-based latent grounding, encouraging reasoning paths that remain visually consistent. To evaluate
the cognitive capabilities of MLLMs, we present CogSense-Bench, a comprehensive visual question
answering (VQA) benchmark assessing five cognitive dimensions. Extensive experiments demonstrate
that MLLMs trained with Cognitive Supersensing significantly outperform state-of-the-art baselines
on CogSense-Bench and exhibit superior generalization on out-of-domain mathematics and science
VQA benchmarks, suggesting that internal visual imagery may be key to bridging the gap between
perceptual recognition and cognitive understanding. Causal analyses through noise injection, latent
patching, and linear probing show that LVIP states progressively encode task-relevant visual structure
and influence downstream answer probabilities, supporting their role as active reasoning signals.

Introduction
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have rapidly advanced open-vocabulary visual understanding,
enabling strong performance across recognition, grounding, and compositional perception tasks [147]. Yet,
existing models can describe what is present but struggle to mentally operate on visual information, e.g.,
by explaining abstract layouts, simulating future transformations, or inferring visual rules in diagrams [111].
These failures expose a gap between high-level cognitive reasoning and low-level perception; this gap has
become a key bottleneck in modern VQA and visual reasoning benchmarks [18, 89] and even extends to
reasoning-driven image generation [87, 123].
Current approaches often attempt to narrow this gap by eliciting Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning as
natural-language reasoning traces, encouraging models to articulate intermediate steps explicitly [124, 139, 176],
or by relying on external tools [51]. However, even in multimodal settings, the intermediate computation is
externalized in text, which is a poor interface for many visuospatial operations. Many subroutines underlying
abstract visual reasoning, such as mentally rotating shapes, simulating dynamics, or inducing rules in pattern

relations rather than as a sequence of discrete tokens. Expressing these intermediate states solely in linear
text can introduce representational bottlenecks, where spatial relations are compressed into discrete tokens,
increasing the risk of information loss and brittle reasoning.
To quantify this cognitive gap, we introduce CogSense-Bench, a comprehensive benchmark that operationalizes
visual cognition along five core dimensions: fluid intelligence [23, 45], crystallized intelligence [23, 109],
visuospatial cognition [11, 140], mental simulation [8], and visual routines [129], which together underpin
intuitive theories of physics and psychology [60]. Using CogSense-Bench, we systematically re-evaluate
state-of-the-art (SoTA) MLLMs and find that substantial weaknesses persist across all dimensions, even
when models are equipped with CoT prompting, suggesting that text-only reasoning is indeed often a brittle
interface for tasks that require multi-step visual transformation and manipulation.
We therefore seek to shift part of the intermediate reasoning from discrete tokens to a representation space
that better preserves geometry, continuity, and structured visual relations. This direction is consistent with
cognitive science accounts of a visuospatial sketchpad (the “mind’s eye”) that supports maintaining and
transforming internal visual representations during problem solving [43, 125, 144], and with recent progress
showing that latent visual states can serve as effective substrates for prediction and world modeling [119, 148],
as well as with brain-inspired designs that draw on hippocampal mechanisms to enhance video vision language
models [16]. Motivated by these insights, we explore whether equipping MLLMs with an internal visual
reasoning substrate can better support multi-step, non-linguistic manipulation of visual information. To
this end, we introduce Cognitive Supersensing, a training paradigm that encourages MLLMs to construct
latent image-based internal reasoning chains. Concretely, we augment an MLLM with a Latent Visual Imagery
Prediction (LVIP) head that predicts progressively refined latent visual representations, i.e. latent “imagery”
states that encode intermediate visual reasoning, and aligns them with image representations derived from
the target answer and supervision signal, thereby grounding the latent chain toward the correct solution. This
design enables multi-step reasoning in a representation space that better preserves visual structure, while
still allowing language to provide high-level semantic guidance and final explanations.
To train models under this paradigm, we further curate the train split of CogSense-Bench, a targeted
training set spanning five categories and eleven sub-tasks, and adopt a multi-stage training pipeline. First,
in a supervised fine-tuning stage, we optimize an LVIP objective that provides an auxiliary learning signal
encouraging intermediate latent visual states to stay predictive of the final answer’s visual representation,
tightly coupling the reasoning process with the solution. Second, we introduce a reinforcement learning
stage that optimizes rollout trajectories in a latent space conditioned on these visual cognitive latents,
encouraging coherent latent dynamics and discouraging brittle text-only reasoning paths. Across extensive
experiments, CogSense-8B achieves SoTA performance on all tasks and the strongest overall accuracy (73.8),
surpassing GPT-5.2 by +33.5, while also demonstrating improvements in out-of-domain (OOD) generalization,
outperforming strong baselines on challenging science and open-ended VQA benchmarks. Taken together, our
results suggest that latent visual imagery can serve as an effective internal scaffold that bridges perceptual
recognition and cognitive-level multimodal reasoning. Overall, our contributions are:
• We propose Cognitive Supersensing, a latent-space reasoning-and-learning framework with supervised
fine-tuning and reinforcement learning stages that endows MLLMs with visual imagery capability by
aligning semantic reasoning with latent visual world modeling.
• We introduce CogSense-Bench, a comprehensive benchmark spanning five cognitive dimensions: fluid
intelligence, crystallized intelligence, visuospatial cognition, mental simulation, and visual routines, providing
a systematic testbed for evaluating visual cognition beyond perceptual recognition and supporting future
research in this direction.
• Through extensive experiments, we show that reasoning and planning purely in text space is often insufficient
for visual cognition: CogSense-8B achieves SoTA performance on CogSense-Bench and exhibits strong
OOD generalization on challenging mathematics and science VQA benchmarks, outperforming competitive
baselines that rely on text-only planning.

Related Work
Our work is closely related to Abstract Reasoning [30, 31, 62], Social Reasoning [19–21, 61], Visual Cog-
nition [7, 12, 13, 106–108, 163], VLMs for Visual Reasoning [5, 6, 48, 76, 84, 105, 133], and Latent Visual
Reasoning [63, 77, 131, 150, 173]. Cognitive Supersensing differs from prior reconstructive and latent-visual-
reasoning methods by making latent visual states part of the reasoning trajectory. Instead of reconstructing
the input image or using visual latents as generic intermediate scratchpads, LVIP predicts visual hypothe-
ses conditioned on intermediate reasoning prefixes, and the latent-rationale optimization stage uses their
consistency to shape the reasoning policy. Additional related work is discussed in Appendix A.
3
CogSense Benchmark
While numerous VQA benchmarks evaluate MLLMs across various tasks, they predominantly focus on semantic
recognition or description [29, 79]. There remains a lack of systematic evaluation protocols specifically designed
to assess high-level visual cognition [114, 118], i.e. the ability to reason over visual inputs through distinct
cognitive mechanisms such as abstract reasoning, spatial structuring, mental simulation, and attention-driven
operations. To address this gap, we introduce CogSense-Bench, which consists of a comprehensive and
large-scale train split together with a unified multi-task test split.
Visual cognition supports high-level visual reasoning and inference processes, such as causal reasoning, intuitive
physics, and intuitive psychology [8, 111]. These capacities reflect central dimensions through which humans
interpret, predict, and reason about visual environments. To systematically evaluate whether MLLMs exhibit
analogous competencies, we assess visual cognitive intelligence across five theory-grounded cognitive categories.
These categories are selected to align with established constructs in cognitive science and psychology, and to
capture complementary mechanisms underlying human visual reasoning as articulated in prior theoretical and
empirical work.
Fluid Intelligence evaluates the capacity to solve unseen reasoning problems independently of prior knowl-
edge [23]. Grounded in Structure Mapping Theory [45], this requires the model to transcend surface attributes
and map higher-order logical rules.
Crystallized Intelligence targets the utilization of learned world knowledge [23], relying on Inductive Reasoning
and Prototype Theory to abstract semantic concepts from visual variance [109].
To assess 3D spatial understanding, Visuospatial Cognition tests the reconstruction of structural relationships,
requiring the ability to group discrete visual elements into coherent, holistic structures based on Gestalt
laws [140] and the decomposition of shapes into geometric primitives (geons) consistent with Recognition-by-
Components Theory [11].
Distinct from static recognition, Mental Simulation demands that models act as “simulation engines”, employing
Hypothetico-Deductive Reasoning to synthesize programs and infer hidden dynamics [8].
Finally, Visual Routines evaluates efficiency in visual search, requiring the composition of elementary operations
and Focused Attention to bind features and exercise inhibitory control [129].
Figure 1 depicts examples of each category, and Figure 2 shows the data distribution of the train split of
CogSense-Bench. The theoretical grounding of the five cognitive dimensions is detailed in Appendix B.
Detailed statistics are provided in Appendix C, while Appendix D provides the implementation details for the
data pipeline.
CogSense-Bench is designed to probe high-level visual cognition beyond recognition, including abstract rule
induction, spatial structuring, mental simulation, and attention control. These categories often require (i)
explicit multi-step reasoning that composes elementary operations, and (ii) maintaining and manipulating
answer-oriented internal visual states during inference. Motivated by these requirements, we propose Cognitive
Supersensing, which couples chain-of-thought-style rationale generation with latent visual imagery prediction,
enabling MLLMs to better support the simulation-like and stateful reasoning demanded by CogSense-Bench.

Method
4.1
Preliminaries
Let V and Q denote the visual input and the textual prompt, respectively, and denote the multimodal input
as X = (V, Q). Our goal is to learn a multimodal model parameterized by θ, which induces a conditional
generation distribution qθ(· | X) over an output consisting of a reasoning rationale Z (a token sequence) and
a final answer y.
Specifically, V is processed by a pre-trained visual encoder Encvis(·) to extract visual features, denoted as
VV = {vi}T
i=1 with vi ∈RP ×dvis, where T is the number of images, and P is the number of patches per
image. These features are mapped into the language embedding space via a projection layer P(·), yielding
projected visual tokens HV = P(VV). Simultaneously, the prompt Q is tokenized and embedded using the
LLM embedding layer to obtain textual tokens HQ. The LLM backbone Enctxt(·) takes the concatenated
token sequence [HV, HQ] as input and produces hidden states used by (i) a text decoder to generate (Z, y)
autoregressively and (ii) a Latent Visual Imagery Prediction (LVIP) head to predict answer-oriented latent
imagery.
Training follows a three-stage pipeline, shown in Figure 3: (1) Reasoning Chain Generation to synthesize
high-quality rationales (Section 4.2), (2) SFT with Latent Visual Imagery Prediction to jointly learn text
generation and latent imagery prediction (Section 4.3), and (3) RL with Latent Rationales to further optimize
rationale sampling (Section 4.4).
4.2

ˆy, which we then filter to ensure alignment with the ground-truth answer y. Specifically, for each example, we

and produce a step-by-step reasoning chain based on (V, Q, Qgen):

4.3
Stage II: SFT with Latent Visual Imagery Prediction
We propose Cognitive Supersensing as an approach for acquiring visual cognitive capabilities. Motivated by the
human constructive matching process, we introduce an auxiliary module, Latent Visual Imagery Prediction
(LVIP), to predict hy, the latent representation of the ground-truth answer option image. The latent visual
imagery generation is synchronized with the autoregressive next-token prediction. At each decoding step of
the textual CoT, the LVIP head generates a corresponding visual latent embedding based on the current
context.

4.4
Stage III: RL with Latent Rationales
In this stage, we leverage diversity-seeking RL by performing amortized variational inference over latent
rationales using a Generative Flow Network (GFlowNet) [9, 59]. Concretely, we refine the rationale policy
qθ(Z | X) to sample rationale trajectories Z in proportion to an unnormalized trajectory score, rather than
committing to a single deterministic chain. We define the corresponding reward-induced target posterior over
rationales as
p∗(Z | X, y) ∝exp(R(Z; X, y)) ,
(3)
and train qθ(Z | X) to approximate this posterior with a GFlowNet objective, detailed below. We define the
trajectory score as a weighted combination of answer evidence and LVIP-based representation grounding:
R(Z; X, y) = α Rans(Z; X, y) + γ Rlvip(Z; X, y).
(4)
Importantly, the LVIP term provides answer-oriented grounding in representation space, which complements
discrete answer supervision and facilitates exploration over long rationale trajectories.

Experiments
Baselines and Metrics. We compare CogSense-8B with several mainstream open-source and closed-source
MLLMs and VLMs with strong multimodal performance: Gemini 2.5 Flash [32], GPT-o3 [102], GPT-5.2 [101],

be found in Appendix F.
Implementation Details. All experiments are conducted with 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs. We use Qwen3-VL-8B
as the backbone to train CogSense-8B. We use AdamW as the optimizer with a learning rate of η = 10−5
and a weight decay of 10−5. We use GPT-5 [120] as the teacher model in Stage I.

5.3
Ablation Studies
Module Ablation. As summarized in Table 3, to isolate the contributions of LVIP and our proposed reinforce-
ment learning mechanism, we perform a hierarchical evaluation across various variants. Compared to the
vanilla Qwen3-VL-8B, applying standard SFT substantially improves the average accuracy from 35.5 to 62.3.
The integration of LVIP introduces a further improvement, raising the average accuracy to 68.0. This suggests
that LVIP helps the model align semantic reasoning and abstract world modeling, enabling the model to gain
initial “supersensing” capabilities. To validate the necessity of our specific RL design, we introduce GRPO
as a reinforcement learning baseline. Although applying GRPO to the SFT models (w/o or w/ LVIP) yields
observable gains, achieving improvements of +3.2 and +2.8 compared to models without GRPO, it serves
primarily to refine the policy within a standard optimization scope. GRPO plus Latent Rationales further
improves performance to 66.2 and 71.5 compared to GRPO only, suggesting that the two RL objectives
are complementary. Finally, CogSense-8B achieves the best average performance of 73.8, outperforming the
strongest baseline (SFT w/ LVIP + GRPO + Latent Rationales) by a clear margin. This result highlights
that our elaborately designed RL method is not merely an optimization trick but a specialized reasoning
refiner that is more effective than general GRPO in leveraging the visual supersensing established by LVIP.
Latent Visual Imagery Learning. To verify that LVIP learns meaningful intermediate visual states rather than
answer-conditioned shortcuts, we perform three experiments. (i) Noise Injection [81]: Perturbing the latent state
at Step 30 increases the MSE loss between intermediate latent visual imagery at multiple steps of the CoT
and the final ground-truth latent visual imagery (Table 4a), demonstrating that the uncorrupted intermediate
state is vital for reasoning. (ii) Latent Patching [82]: Swapping latent states between correct and incorrect runs
shifts the rationale and answer probability toward the patched state (Table 4b), showing that these states
carry causal information that steers inference. (iii) Linear Probing [1]: A linear classifier trained on intermediate
latent visual imagery at multiple steps of the CoT shows that accuracy grows progressively (Table 4c). This
confirms that visual logic is constructed step-by-step, ruling out trivial answer-conditioned shortcuts. Detailed
settings, more experiments, and discussions of latent visual imagery learning can be found in Appendix J.

Conclusion
We propose Cognitive Supersensing, a
paradigm designed to bridge the gap be-
tween perceptual processing and complex
cognitive reasoning in MLLMs. By inte-
grating a Latent Visual Imagery Predic-
tion (LVIP) head and employing a training strategy that combines SFT and RL with Latent Rationales, we
enable CogSense-8B to simulate internal visual imagery aligned with semantic reasoning chains. We further
introduce CogSense-Bench, a comprehensive evaluation suite targeting five dimensions of visual cognition. Our
experiments demonstrate that CogSense-8B achieves superior performance compared to SoTA MLLM baselines
on these cognitive tasks. These results indicate that modeling the interaction between visual simulation and
logical deduction is an effective direction for advancing the reasoning abilities of multimodal systems.

A
Related Works
Bongard Problems. The Bongard Problems (BPs) were introduced by M. M. Bongard [12, 13] as a novel
challenge to machine vision. A typical BP includes two sets, positive and negative, with each side consisting
of six images that share a common pattern. It requires a system to induce the logical rule that distinguishes a
set of positive examples from negative ones [12, 13]. This task evaluates core properties of human cognition:
identifying the underlying rule that differentiates the two sides and articulating it in natural language [94]. To
modernize this benchmark for deep learning, the Bongard-LOGO dataset was introduced, transforming the
concept learning challenge into a few-shot binary classification problem [100]. Recent works have expanded
the field of BPs to include real-world images, such as Bongard-HOI [56], Bongard-OpenWorld [141], Bongard-
RWR [94], and Bongard-RWR+ [103]. These works expand the range of presented objects, attributes, and
relationships, illustrating human-object interactions and incorporating free-form concepts in the real world,
thus increasing the diversity of featured scenes.
Matrix Reasoning. Matrix reasoning tasks play a critical role in assessing human intelligence, particularly in re-
lation to visual cognition and working memory [41, 54, 110]. These tasks are widely used in psychology through
Raven’s Progressive Matrices (RPMs) [106–108], the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC) [57, 137],
and the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS-IV) [138] to evaluate human fluid intelligence and abstract vi-
sual reasoning. Early works, such as PGM [7] and RAVEN [163], were proposed to test whether neural networks
can learn abstract reasoning and to lift machine intelligence by associating vision with structural, relational, and
analogical reasoning in a hierarchical representation. Subsequent works further demonstrated that deep learn-
ing models can be trained to solve simple matrix reasoning tasks [91, 93, 145]. Several datasets and benchmarks
have also been introduced to enlarge this field, such as I-RAVEN [50], RAVEN-FAIR [10], and CVR [162]. How-
ever, these studies tend to overlook the ability of humans to solve such puzzles in a zero-shot manner, without
the need for explicit training on extensive datasets. Therefore, some useful zero-shot visual reasoning inference
datasets such as RAVEN-IQ [52] and Visual Reasoning Benchmark [175] have been proposed to deal with
the issue. The latest MaRs-VQA [18] addresses the limitations of previous works, namely the lack of rigorous
human experiments as a reference and the reliance on relatively small datasets without psychometric validation.
Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus. Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus for Artificial General Intelligence (ARC-
AGI) is a novel general AI benchmark designed to measure a human-like form of general fluid intelligence and
enable fair general intelligence comparisons between AI systems and humans [30]. Its measurement standard
emphasizes skill-acquisition efficiency and out-of-domain generalization over mere task performance. The
tasks require systems to solve grid-based visual program synthesis problems by abstracting the underlying
rules from only a minimal set of input-output examples (typically three to five pairs). A robust estimate of
human performance [62] on the full ARC public evaluation set places the average human accuracy at 64.2%,
far exceeding that of current state-of-the-art AI methods. Its updated version, ARC-AGI-2 [31], is more
challenging. Even the most powerful models at the time of its release, such as OpenAI’s o3 [102], achieve only
negligible performance on the newly released, adversarially selected ARC-AGI-2 challenge. This reinforces
that modern frontier reasoning models fundamentally lack the structural inductive biases that are necessary
for human-like skill-acquisition and generalization.
VLMs for Vision Reasoning. Visual recognition-related Vision Language Model (VLM) studies have made great
progress since the development of CLIP [105]. Prior work has shown that VLMs are capable of addressing
vision reasoning tasks [14, 64, 65, 72, 80, 86, 99, 113, 130, 161, 172, 177]. VLMs are the dominant architecture
for bridging perception and language, typically integrating a visual encoder (e.g., CLIP) with pretrained
LLMs via a cross-modal connector to align visual features with text space [17, 42, 83, 112, 164, 165, 169].
The training pipeline for VLMs often involves pre-training for modality alignment, followed by instruction
tuning or Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to enhance general multimodal capabilities [83, 151]. Models such
as Qwen-VL [3, 5, 6], LLaVA [83, 84], MiniGPT-4 [178], InstructBLIP [33], and CogVLM [133] highlight
the significance of employing high-quality visual instruction tuning data. However, current VLMs still face
challenges in adapting to high-resolution [22, 78] and visually complex images in vision reasoning. This is
because most current vision reasoning approaches are primarily text-level, with the LLM exploring textual
tokens while the visual input remains static [63]. This absence of a dynamic visual search mechanism limits

Latent Visual Reasoning. While standard VLMs rely on static visual encoding, a new paradigm of Latent Visual
Reasoning has emerged, shifting the focus from passive perception to active, internal simulation. This approach
draws inspiration from World Models [68, 122, 170, 171, 179], enabling systems to “think” in a compressed
latent space before generating a response. Recent advancements extend this paradigm to predict dynamic
changes via temporal latent reconstruction [117, 167, 168] and embed reasoning within goal-directed egocentric
simulators [71, 115]. Rather than mapping pixels directly to text, these models perform intermediate reasoning
steps, akin to System-2 cognition, by predicting future states or manipulating visual abstractions [55, 122, 179].
For instance, recent frameworks introduce “visual scratchpads” or latent tokens that allow the model to
sketch out reasoning traces implicitly [74, 173]. Methods such as Mirage [150] and Latent Sketchpad [173]
exemplify this by empowering models to generate and refine mental imagery during the inference process,
effectively bridging the gap between visual grounding and abstract logic. Further advancements include Latent
Visual Reasoning (LVR) [63] and implicit reasoning tokens [77], which allow models to perform autoregressive
planning in the visual embedding space without explicit supervision. To effectively untangle complex visual
environments, approaches like ROSS [131] complement this paradigm by explicitly modeling spatial topological
structures within the latent space via a denoising objective to reconstruct latent representations of the question
image as vision-centric supervision, augmenting the model’s structural comprehension. These methods
overcome the limitations of text-centric reasoning by maintaining rich, high-dimensional visual information
throughout the decision-making chain [63, 77]. Furthermore, similar principles of latent semantic grounding
have also driven advances in continuous 3D spatial modeling [154–157] and generative modeling [34–40, 66].
While these prior works demonstrate the potential of utilizing latent spaces for intermediate reasoning, they
mainly rely on either unconstrained autoregressive planning or vision-centric supervision derived from the
input context. In contrast, our work predicts visual hypotheses conditioned on intermediate reasoning prefixes,
and the latent-rationale optimization stage uses their consistency to shape the reasoning policy.
B
Theoretical Grounding of the Five Cognitive Dimensions
As shown in Table B1, the first three dimensions represent the traditional psychometric approach, while
the last two dimensions belong to the process-level tradition of visual cognition. Together, these dimensions
cover complementary operations required for high-level visual cognition: inducing rules, retrieving concepts,
organizing spatial structure, simulating changes, and executing attention-based visual operations. We exclude
other traditional psychometric dimensions such as Working Memory (Gwm) and Processing Speed (Gs). These
dimensions measure cognitive capacity and execution speed rather than reasoning operations. In the context
of MLLMs, they are largely confounded with architectural hyperparameters (e.g., context window length and
KV-cache) rather than inner visual cognitive capabilities.

D
Implementation Details for Data Pipeline
D.1
Data Extraction
We curate visual cognition data from a broad set of datasets spanning Bongard-style reasoning, abstract visual
reasoning, spatial cognition, and ARC-style program induction, including Bongard-OpenWorld [141], Bongard-
HOI [56], Bongard-LOGO [100], Bongard-RWR+ [103], CVR [162], KiVA [153], MaRs-VQA [18], PGM [7],
RAVEN [163], I-RAVEN [50], RAVEN-FAIR [10], A-I-RAVEN [92], I-RAVEN-Mesh [92], I-RAVEN-X [15],
STARE [79], ARC-AGI [30], and ARC-AGI-2 [31]. From these sources, we manually select examples that
match our five predefined cognitive categories, ensuring that each retained instance is relevant to the target
reasoning skills evaluated in our benchmark. Building on these seed sources, we develop automated extraction
and cleaning scripts to expand the collection at scale and produce the raw dataset. To standardize evaluation
across heterogeneous task formats, we further convert tasks that were not originally multiple-choice into a
unified multiple-choice format. For Bongard-style problems, we randomly select one image from the positive
set as the correct answer, combine it with negative samples, and shuffle the resulting candidates as answer
options; the remaining positive samples are used to define the query context. For ARC-AGI-style problems,
we perform data augmentation on the ground-truth images and present both the original ground-truth image
and augmented variants as candidate options.

Human Study Design and Setup
Participant Recruitment. To establish a human performance baseline, we recruited a total of 20 participants
through online platforms using a random sampling strategy, with ages ranging from 18 to 34 (average 24.5).
They participated purely out of interest in AI cognitive evaluation and to test their own performance against
the benchmark. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and no financial compensation or monetary
incentives were provided to the subjects.
Questionnaire Design. The questionnaire design involved a rigorous selection process to ensure representative-
ness. We constructed a test set comprising 100 multiple-choice questions derived from the CogSense-Bench.
These questions were manually selected by stratified sampling to strictly maintain the category proportions of
the original benchmark. The study was implemented and distributed digitally via the Google Forms platform,
allowing for remote administration.
Time Spent and Compensation. The survey took participants an average of 45 minutes to complete. Participants
proceeded entirely voluntarily and were free to drop out at any point without consequence. To ensure data
quality, we monitored response times and confirmed that none of the participants rushed through the survey.
The high consistency in their answers also indicates rigorous engagement with the task. Since uncompensated
volunteer recruitment limits the representativeness of the human baseline, we acknowledge this limitation and
do not present the 20-participant result as a population-level estimate of human cognitive performance.
Consent Procedure. Before evaluation, all participants were presented with a digital informed consent form
that outlined the purpose of the study, the estimated time commitment, the voluntary and uncompensated
nature of the task, and the data anonymity protocols. Participants were required to explicitly agree to these
terms before proceeding. The study protocol, including the consent process and volunteer framework, was
reviewed and approved by our Institutional Review Board (IRB) under an existing IRB approval for visual
cognition research at our institution.
Compliance. Regarding experimental standards and ethical compliance, all protocols were reviewed and
approved by the IRB prior to the study.

Comparison between ROSS and CogSense
We compare CogSense against ROSS [131] by conducting two more experiments, where we train the same

train the model with the proposed RL method on the same dataset. We evaluate both models on the listed
benchmarks. We further ablate our method with and without LVIP under this setting. We then evaluate this
newly trained model across a range of established general-domain and fine-grained multimodal benchmarks

models on the test split.

adding LVIP consistently improves performance across all five general-domain benchmarks. Moreover, under
the same setting, Qwen3-VL-8B backbone, and dataset, our framework also outperforms ROSS. These results
isolate the effect of LVIP from backbone or data differences, showing that LVIP provides robust gains beyond
our cognitive benchmark and transfers to broader multimodal understanding and fine-grained visual reasoning.
G
Statistical Significance Analysis
Bootstrap Confidence Intervals. We compute 95% confidence intervals using a stratified paired bootstrap over
the benchmark. Each bootstrap sample preserves the category proportions of CogSense-Bench. We use 10,000
bootstrap resamples.
McNemar Test. For pairwise comparison between CogSense-8B and a baseline, we report McNemar’s test [96]
on paired correctness outcomes over all N benchmark instances. Let b denote the number of instances
that CogSense-8B gets correct while the baseline gets wrong, and c denote the number of instances that
CogSense-8B gets wrong while the baseline gets correct. The test is conducted on the nd = b + c discordant
pairs. We compute the two-sided exact McNemar p-value using a binomial test under the null hypothesis
that both models have equal accuracy, i.e., b ∼Binomial(nd, 0.5). The p-value is defined as the probability of
observing a value at least as extreme as the observed b under this distribution.
Our statistical analysis validates the efficacy of our specific training pipeline and architectural choices. As
detailed in the paired performance comparisons, CogSense-8B significantly outperforms the strongest same-
backbone baseline (Qwen3-VL-8B SFT w/ LVIP + GRPO + LR, ∆= +2.3%) under both the stratified paired
bootstrap (95% CI: [0.7, 3.9]) and the exact McNemar test (p = 0.0265). This supports that the observed
improvement is unlikely to be explained by benchmark sampling noise alone, confirming the robustness and
effectiveness of our proposed approach.

Ablation Study on Intermediate State Perturbation. If a model relies on an answer-conditioned shortcut, its

each latent visual state is functionally necessary by selecting successful reasoning trajectories and injecting
random noise into the latent visual state at a specific step (Step 30). The model then continues decoding from
the perturbed state. We measure the MSE between the intermediate latent visual imagery ˆhy,i at different
CoT steps and the final ground-truth latent visual imagery hy, averaged over 10 sampled trajectories. As
shown in Table 4a, the trajectory remains stable before the intervention. Once the Step-30 state is perturbed,
downstream latent error increases, and the trajectory fails to reconverge to the clean path. This provides
evidence that intermediate latent visual states play a causal role in sustaining the reasoning trajectory, rather
than merely being correlated with answer confidence.
Ablation Study on Latent State Intervention. To further test whether LVIP actively steers reasoning, we conduct
a latent patching experiment inspired by [82]. We intervene during intermediate decoding by swapping the
latent visual state between correct and incorrect reasoning runs. We measure the change in the probability
of generating the correct answer after the intervention (∆p = pafter −pbefore). As shown in Table 4b, when
we patch a distractor’s latent state into a correct trajectory, the downstream rationale shifts toward that
distractor. Conversely, patching a correct latent state into an incorrect run actively shifts the trajectory
toward the correct answer. This provides strong evidence that the patched intermediate state carries vital
causal information and actively steers the inference.

Ablation Study on Rationale Sampling Strategies
To ensure that the performance gains of CogSense-8B do not simply come from a larger sampling budget of
rationales during inference, and to explore the effectiveness of different sampling strategies, we conduct a
comprehensive ablation study comparing various sampling and selection strategies. To ensure a fair comparison,
we fix the total sampling budget for all multi-sample baselines to n = 8. We maintain consistency across all
experiments by using the same decoding temperature, maximum rationale length, and the same frozen scorer
for reranking when applicable.
We evaluate the following strategies to isolate the impact of our proposed approach:
• Greedy Decoding: A single deterministic decoding baseline representing the standard performance
without multiple samples.
• SC-Majority: Following the Self-Consistency method from [136], we sample n = 8 rationales and perform
a majority vote on the final answers, treating each path with equal weight.
• SC-Reranking: We sample n = 8 rationales and use the frozen scorer to select the path with the highest
reward score, using only that path’s final answer.
• Ours: Our proposed sampling strategy described in Section 4.4.
As shown in Table L1, across all variants from the base model up to GRPO models, sampling strategies such
as Self-Consistency and Scorer Reranking consistently provide marginal improvements over Greedy Decoding.
However, these improvements show diminishing returns. For instance, applying SC-Reranking to the standard
SFT models cannot close the performance gap to models trained with trajectory-level optimization (GRPO).

Broader Impacts
This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the fields of machine learning and cognitive science,
specifically in multimodal reasoning and visual cognition. The proposed methods aim to improve MLLMs’
ability to solve complex visual reasoning tasks, which could have broad applications in education, scientific
discovery, and assistive technologies. While we believe the potential benefits are substantial, we acknowledge
that improved visual reasoning capabilities could be misused in contexts such as automated surveillance or
manipulation. We encourage the research community to consider ethical implications and develop appropriate
safeguards as these technologies advance.
Q
Limitations
While CogSense-8B achieves strong visual cognitive performance at the 8B scale, our experiments focus on
validating the proposed Cognitive Supersensing paradigm within a moderate-scale MLLM setting. Future
work could investigate scaling properties to substantially larger 30B- or 70B-style MLLM backbones, larger
training corpora, and distributed training setups.
