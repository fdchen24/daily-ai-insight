Abstract
Spatial intelligence, the ability to reason about 3D space from visual observations, becomes essential as
multimodal large language models (MLLMs) move into the physical world. Beyond perceiving each view,
it rests on two cornerstones of human cognition: fusing multiple views into one coherent 3D scene and
mentally transforming that scene to new viewpoints as reasoning unfolds. Yet our spatial probes reveal
that MLLMs largely lack both capabilities, even after spatial fine-tuning. We therefore present S-MIND,
which internalizes multi-view geometry within the MLLM through a compact set of spatial tokens. It
supervises these tokens to hold the fused 3D scene and re-emits them to re-express this scene from
every viewpoint the reasoning adopts. Across four spatial benchmarks, S-MIND improves its Qwen3.5
backbones by up to 22.26 points and surpasses frontier general models such as GPT-6-Astra (+0.45),
Kimi-K3.1-Pro (+1.97), and GLM-5.3-Flash (+7.80) in average accuracy. Moreover, the same probes
confirm that it acquires both capabilities, reaching fusion and transformation accuracies of 65.11% and
82.17%, against at most 16.02% and 37.28% for the baselines. At inference, S-MIND also runs about
100× faster than a tool-based agent on the same backbone. Instilled through these spatial tokens during
training, spatial capability becomes intrinsic to the backbone and persists even when the tokens are
withheld, offering a promising path toward spatial intelligence in MLLMs.

1 Introduction
Two snapshots of a room, one facing the window and one facing the door, are enough to walk through
it in the mind: stand by the sofa, face the shelf, and the window is on the left (Figure 1). Behind this
lie two operations: assembling the snapshots into one model of the room, and mentally rotating that
model to the new standpoint (Shepard and Metzler, 1971). As multimodal large language models
(MLLMs) (Huang et al., 2026a; Aneja et al., 2026; Qwen Team, 2026a) advance rapidly in visual
understanding and reasoning, real-world tasks such as embodied AI (Wu et al., 2026; Shi et al.,
2026a), autonomous driving (Luo et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026b), and extended reality (Yang
et al., 2026c; Li and Yao, 2026) increasingly hinge on such spatial intelligence.
Despite its importance, spatial intelligence remains a major bottleneck for MLLMs (Wang et al.,
2026b; Cai et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b), requiring the model to mentally construct a coher-
ent 3D scene rather than merely recognize 2D visual patterns. As Figure 2a illustrates, existing
approaches generally fall into two paradigms: (i) External-geometry methods construct the scene
outside the model, via 3D reconstruction and rendering (Zhang et al., 2026f; Yang et al., 2025; Cho
et al., 2026) or an attached geometry encoder (Wu et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026).
While providing explicit geometry, they fail to internalize spatial capability and incur high inference
latency (Li et al., 2026g,e). (ii) Spatial fine-tuning methods aim to train the MLLM to construct
the scene internally, with supervision progressing from the output text alone (Li et al., 2026d; Yang
et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026f) to latent states aligned with 3D geometry (Chen et al., 2026; Shi et al.,
2026a; Wang and Huang, 2026; Oi et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b). This paradigm has drawn growing
attention for its promise of internalizing spatial capability within the model itself.
However, the evidence for this promise rests largely on end-task accuracy. We therefore introduce
spatial probes, extending recent analyses of the spatial representations inside MLLMs (Shi et al.,
2026b), to decode the scene a model holds internally. Our probes reveal that, despite substantial
gains in accuracy, spatially fine-tuned models retain two persistent deficiencies in spatial capability,
as illustrated in Figure 2b. (i) Limited multi-view fusion: these models encode each view well
but in isolation, and the views are barely fused into a coherent scene. (ii) Incomplete perspective
transformation: as in base models (Shi et al., 2026b), their internal representation still largely fails
to follow when the reasoning shifts to a new viewpoint. This mismatch raises a natural question:
Research Question
How can an MLLM truly internalize spatial capability, assembling its views
into one scene and rotating that scene to a new viewpoint, as humans do?
To address this question, we introduce S-MIND, a framework that endows an MLLM with an internal
scene representation, built from its input views and transformed as its reasoning unfolds. (I) Multi-
view fusion. S-MIND introduces a compact set of spatial tokens whose hidden states consolidate
the views into a unified 3D scene, encoding both the geometry each image observes and the pose of
each camera in a shared coordinate frame. (II) Perspective transformation. With the views fused
into one scene, adopting a new viewpoint reduces to re-expressing that scene in a different frame
of reference. Whenever the reasoning shifts to a new viewpoint, the model therefore re-emits the
spatial tokens, which now encode the fused scene as observed from that viewpoint, and conditions
its subsequent reasoning on them. By training the model to construct and manipulate an entire 3D
scene, S-MIND internalizes genuine spatial capability rather than superficial heuristics.
Extensive evaluations across four spatial reasoning benchmarks and two model scales demonstrate
the effectiveness of S-MIND. (I) Strong performance: S-MIND reaches an average accuracy of up
to 74.03%, surpassing the strongest fine-tuned baseline by 6.76 points and the best released spatial
reasoning model by 19.11 points, and rivaling frontier general models such as GPT-6-Astra (+0.45)
and Kimi-K3.1-Pro (+1.97). (II) Internalized spatial capability: At the representation level, S-MIND

Contributions. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
Representation-Level Diagnosis. Through our spatial probes, we identify two deficiencies in the
spatial capability of current MLLMs, limited multi-view fusion and incomplete perspective trans-
formation, which persist even after spatial fine-tuning substantially improves their accuracy.
Practical Solution. We propose S-MIND, which addresses both deficiencies through spatial tokens
that consolidate the views into a unified 3D scene and are re-emitted at each viewpoint shift to
re-express that scene in the new frame.
Experimental Validation. We validate S-MIND across four benchmarks and two model scales,
showing that it internalizes spatial capability, achieves state-of-the-art accuracy among spatial rea-
soning models, rivals frontier general models, and attains high inference efficiency.

2 Related Work
External-Geometry Methods. External-geometry methods compute the scene outside the MLLM
and supply it as input. Tool-based designs invoke specialist modules at inference: some build and
operate on an explicit scene representation, such as a 3D reconstruction, an object-level abstraction,
or a vector sketch (Zhang et al., 2026f; Cho et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026); others
synthesize views from new standpoints using generative models (Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al.,
2026c) or warp visual tokens toward them (Lee et al., 2026). Encoder-based designs instead integrate
geometry into the architecture, pairing the visual encoder with a feed-forward reconstruction model
such as VGGT (Wang et al., 2025) and fusing their features (Wu et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2026; Zhao
et al., 2026); subsequent work refines where and how these features are injected (Wang et al., 2026a;
Zhang et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026c). However, both designs offload scene construction to external
modules, failing to internalize genuine spatial capability while incurring extra inference overhead (Li
et al., 2026g,e). S-MIND keeps the geometry but moves it from inference into training, where it
teaches the model itself to fuse the views and transform the scene.
Spatial Fine-Tuning Methods. Spatial fine-tuning methods train the MLLM to form the scene in-
ternally, with no geometry at inference. Most methods shape only what the model says: they scale
up spatial instruction data (Chen et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026b,a), train with
progressive curricula, process-level rewards, or interaction trajectories (Li et al., 2026d,f; Yao et al.,
2026), elicit textual cognitive maps or cross-view alignments before the answer (Wang et al., 2026b;
Hua et al., 2026; Tao et al., 2026), or distill tool-using agents and privileged geometry (Li et al.,
2026g,e). A growing body of work, closer in spirit to latent reasoning (Hao et al., 2025; Yang et al.,
2026d; Zhang et al., 2026d), shapes what the model holds instead: it aligns latent states with 3D
foundation models or across views (Chen et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026a; Wang and Huang, 2026;
Oi et al., 2026), or adds dedicated tokens for positions, directions, and an observer’s orientation (Li
et al., 2026b; Leonard and Murray, 2026). Yet these targets largely encode the geometry of the input
views, leaving viewpoint changes unsupervised. S-MIND instead supervises its spatial tokens to fuse
the views into one frame and to re-express the scene at each viewpoint the reasoning adopts.
3 Does Fine-Tuning Really Internalize Spatial Capability in MLLMs?
Higher answer accuracy after fine-tuning does not by itself establish that a model has internalized
spatial capability. We therefore propose spatial probes, which extend recent analyses of spatial rep-
resentations in MLLMs (Shi et al., 2026b), to examine what a model encodes about a scene before
and after spatial fine-tuning. They target two capabilities fundamental to spatial intelligence (Cai
et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b): a geometry probe tests whether multiple views are fused into a uni-
fied internal scene, and a viewpoint probe tests whether that scene is transformed to the viewpoints
adopted during reasoning. More details are in Appendix A.
Finding 1: Limited multi-view fusion. To isolate what the model itself encodes, the geometry
probe appends learnable tokens to the frozen model and decodes from their hidden states the 3D
point of every visual token, expressed in the first image’s frame. As Figure 3a shows, on Qwen3.5-4B
(Base) it recovers 60.62% of the first image’s own points (single-view) but only 11.23% of the other
images’ points (fused), which can be placed only by relating the views. Fine-tuning barely closes
this gap: the + SFT and + GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) variants lift the fused points only to 14.19%
and 16.02%, and the released 3DThinker and GPD reach 10.65% and 11.82%. Crucially, the probe’s
verdict is borne out by behavior. Questions on how the camera moved between two views hinge on
exactly this fusion, yet as the top of Figure 3c shows, SFT and GRPO are right on only 53.28% and
56.57% of such questions in MindCube-Tiny, every error placing the second camera on the mirrored

Finding 2: Incomplete perspective transformation. Shi et al. (2026b) find that base MLLMs fall
short in transforming spatial representations across viewpoints. We further use the viewpoint probe
to test whether spatial fine-tuning closes this gap: the probe reads the relation between two objects
directly in the queried target frame and after an external rotation into it, separating transformation
from perception. As Figure 3b shows, the model perceives the scene but largely fails to transform
it. On SpinBench’s single-image relation questions (Zhang et al., 2026e), the decoded state gives the
correct relation for 95.14% of them once rotated to the queried viewpoint, yet for only 33.44% when
read directly in the target frame at the first mention of the objects, and 47.15% at the last. The
same holds when the reasoning itself declares the viewpoint: the scene read in the frame of each
declared viewpoint is recovered with only 30.02% accuracy on average, barely above the chance level
of 25%. The failure is reflected in the answers: as the bottom of Figure 3c shows, the Qwen3.5-4B
models reach 85.71–98.73% accuracy on questions whose final state lies in the target frame, but only
42.11–57.14%, near chance, on the rest. Spatial fine-tuning thus does not close the gap: the scene
stays largely anchored to the camera instead of following the reasoning to new viewpoints.
4 S-MIND: Fusing Views, Transforming Perspectives
In this section, we detail the two components of S-MIND, each together with its training: multi-view
fusion (⊲Section 4.1), which consolidates the input views into one scene held by a compact set of
spatial tokens, and perspective transformation (⊲Section 4.2), which re-expresses that scene in the
frame of every viewpoint the reasoning adopts. The overall pipeline is illustrated in Figure 4.

5.1 Setup

of spatial intelligence: mental modeling with MindCube-Tiny (Wang et al., 2026b), relational rea-

Following EASI (Cai et al., 2025), we report the accuracy on each benchmark and the average over
the four, with the evaluated subsets and the protocol detailed in Appendix C.1.

5.2 Main Results
S-MIND Achieves the Strongest Performance among Spatial Reasoning Models. Table 1 shows
that S-MIND built on Qwen3.5-4B reaches an average accuracy of 72.83%, surpassing the strongest
method trained in our setting, ProSR, by 7.65 points and the best released spatial reasoning model,
Spatial-Interactor-4B, by 17.91 points. The gains are consistent: S-MIND ranks first on all four bench-
marks, ahead of the strongest baseline on each by 8.67 points on MindCube-Tiny, 3.10 on SPAR-Bench,
6.25 on BLINK, and 6.44 on ViewSpatial.
S-MIND Scales to a Larger Backbone and Rivals Frontier General Models. Trained with the same
recipe on Qwen3.5-9B, S-MIND reaches 74.03%, 6.76 points above GRPO and 11.76 above SFT, and
again ranks first on all four benchmarks, by up to 8.48 points on MindCube-Tiny. As Table 1 shows, a
compact open model stands on par with frontier general models: at 9B, S-MIND matches GPT-6-Astra,
Qwen3.8-Flash, and Claude-Opus-5.5 (73.58%, 73.98%, and 74.15%), and even at 4B it surpasses
GLM-5.3-Flash and Kimi-K3.1-Pro (66.23% and 72.06%).

with that of SpatialClaw (Cho et al., 2026), an agent on the same backbone that reconstructs the scene

5.3 Framework Analysis
Beyond accuracy, we further examine whether the gains of S-MIND stem from the two capabilities
that Section 3 identifies as lacking, namely multi-view fusion and perspective transformation. We
first probe whether the model acquires both capabilities and where its backbone fuses the views,
then intervene on the spatial tokens to test whether these capabilities causally affect the answers.
The Views Are Fused into One Scene. As in Section 3, we retrain the geometry probe on the frozen
backbone of S-MIND for a fair comparison with the baselines. Figure 6a shows that S-MIND recovers
65.11% of the fused points and 86.32% of the single-view points, whereas the baselines recover at
most 16.02% and 60.62%. Cross-view bearing and camera facing improve accordingly, reaching
85.35% and 93.55%. This lead persists as views accumulate: with two images the baselines recover
at most 30.28% of the fused points and with eight fewer than 5%, while S-MIND still recovers 50.52%
with eight. More importantly, Figure 8b shows that this fusion carries over to the model’s answers,
raising the accuracy on the 274 two-view questions of Section 3, which hinge on relating the two
views, from only 53.28% and 56.57% for SFT and GRPO to 96.35% for S-MIND.

The Scene Turns with the Reasoning. As in Section 3, we retrain the viewpoint probe on the frozen
backbone of S-MIND for a fair comparison with the baselines. S-MIND reaches 82.17% accuracy,
whereas the baselines remain between 25.22% and 37.28%, close to the probe’s controls. Figure 6b
breaks this gap down by direction: all models recover the points ahead of the declared viewer, but
the baselines recover at most 10.29%, 9.78%, and 40.69% of those behind it, to its left, and to its
right, compared with 77.38%, 69.51%, and 85.29% for S-MIND. The decoded scene is bound to the
declaration: as the controls in Figure 6b show, the accuracy falls to 30.10% when another viewpoint
of the chain is the target and to 29.43% when the probe reads tokens before the declaration.
The Backbone Fuses the Views in Its Middle Layers. The readout leaves the fusion to the backbone
by design: its queries carry no image content, and each point query attends only to the scene tokens
of its own view and to the camera tokens (Appendix B). To locate where the backbone fuses the views,
we take S-MIND on Qwen3.5-4B after geometry pre-training and refit its readout on the states of z0 at
one layer at a time, scoring it on the 416 ScanNet image sets of the geometry probe. Figure 6c shows
that the fused points stay at the level of a readout fit on all-zero states through layer 8 (7.64–11.12%
against 9.21%) and then rise through the middle layers, with their three largest gains, of 12.13 to
14.58 points, at the full-attention layers 11, 15, and 19 of the hybrid backbone. From layer 19 on,
every single layer recovers nearly as many fused points as the learned mix of all layers (57.33–63.54%
against 63.99%), so the fused scene, once formed, is carried through to the last layer.
The Answers Depend on the Scene. Decodability, however, does not imply use. We therefore in-
tervene on the RL model at inference, replacing the states of the last re-emitted spatial tokens in its
own generation with those of z0. As Figure 7a shows, this state swap changes 9.05% of the answers,
mostly from correct to wrong, against only 1.62% for a random perturbation of the same norm (Ap-
pendix D.1). The effect is strongest where no input image can substitute for the transformed scene:
20.86% of the answers change at imagined standpoints and 28.00% in the rotation setting, whose
views barely overlap. Withholding all spatial tokens at inference likewise lowers the four-benchmark
average by 2.37 points, from 72.83% to 70.46%. Despite this drop, Figure 7b shows that S-MIND
without the tokens outperforms the best baseline on every benchmark, by 5.28 points on average, in-
dicating that the capability is internalized in the backbone, not merely carried by the spatial tokens.

5.4 Ablation Study and Sensitivity Analysis
Geometry and Viewpoint Supervision Each Contribute. Figure 8a builds S-MIND from text SFT
one component at a time. (i) Unsupervised spatial tokens, trained solely through the language
loss, alter the accuracy by at most 1.24 points, indicating that extra tokens and computation alone
contribute little. (ii) Geometry supervision, in contrast, yields the largest gain, raising MindCube-
Tiny from 74.00% to 81.81% and BLINK from 68.25% to 71.75%. (iii) Viewpoint supervision of
every re-emitted z𝑘improves both benchmarks further and is most beneficial on BLINK, whose multi-
view questions hinge on how the cameras relate, lifting it to 75.00%. Figure 8b further shows the
gains on the questions that relate the views: geometry supervision alone lifts accuracy on the 274
two-view questions of MindCube-Tiny from 56.57% to 94.89% and on the 133 multi-view questions
of BLINK from 40.60% to 52.63%, which viewpoint supervision then raises to 59.40%.
Preserving the Scene Requires Only a Small Geometry Weight. Figure 8c varies the weight 𝜆on
the two spatial losses during SFT. Without these losses (𝜆=0), SFT erodes the geometry acquired in
pre-training: the 3D points recovered from z0 fall to 48.79%, against 76.13% at 𝜆=0.1. The re-emitted
spatial tokens are not transformed either: only 39.85% decode nearest the declared camera’s view of
the scene in z0, against 97.49%, and 42.55% nearest its view of the true scene, against 96.67%. The
average accuracy drops accordingly, to 65.54%, 1.38 points below that at 𝜆=0.1. Any positive weight
preserves the geometry (76.13–76.41%), but larger weights steadily lower accuracy, from 66.92% at
𝜆=0.1 to 64.01% at 𝜆=1. We adopt 𝜆=0.1, the smallest weight that preserves the scene.
6 Conclusion and Future Work
Conclusion. We introduced S-MIND, a framework that internalizes multi-view geometry in MLLMs
through a compact set of spatial tokens. Our spatial probes first showed that the accuracy gains
of spatially fine-tuned models conceal two persistent deficiencies: the views are barely fused into
a unified internal scene, and this scene rarely follows the reasoning to new viewpoints. S-MIND
addresses both by supervising the spatial tokens to hold the fused 3D scene and re-emitting them
to re-express this scene from every viewpoint the reasoning adopts. Across four spatial benchmarks
and two model scales, S-MIND achieves the strongest performance among spatial reasoning models,
12
rivals frontier general models, and remains highly efficient at inference. The same probes and causal
interventions trace these gains to their source: both capabilities take shape within the model and
directly steer its predictions. These results establish internalized multi-view geometry as a learnable,
efficient, and inspectable source of spatial intelligence in MLLMs.
Future Work. We view internalized geometry as a cornerstone of spatial intelligence, and S-MIND
as one step toward models that think in space. The present work supervises only the geometry that
the input views observe. A natural next step is an internal scene that reaches beyond these views,
completing unseen regions, persisting across interactions, and evolving with dynamic environments,
so that the same tokens can support embodied agents over long horizons. As this paradigm matures,
spatial intelligence may shift from geometry computed outside the model to a property that models
carry within themselves, opening a research agenda around internal world models, inspectable spatial
states, and the joint scaling of perception and reasoning.

A Diagnosis Details
This appendix details the probes of Section 3 and reports their full results.
A.1 Probe Setup
In this section, we describe the models we diagnose and the probes we apply to them:
• Models. Base is Qwen3.5-4B (Qwen Team, 2026a). + SFT is Base fine-tuned with LoRA (Hu et al.,
2022) on reasoning chains written by a teacher model, and + GRPO is trained for 375 steps of
GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) with a reward on the final answer. They stand for the two common ways
of teaching an MLLM spatial reasoning from text and answers alone. We also probe two released
spatial reasoning models: 3DThinker (Chen et al., 2026) (its released MindCube checkpoint), built
on Qwen2.5-VL-3B, and GPD (Li et al., 2026e), built on Qwen3-VL-4B. For them, the S-Space axes
are those of each model’s backbone, and each model answers in its own reasoning format, decoded
greedily with at most 4,096 new tokens and a strict extraction of the answer.
• Geometry probe. We freeze the whole model and append probe tokens after the images, two per
image and four for the cameras. Only the input embeddings of these tokens are trained, together
with a readout of a single cross-attention layer, on posed image sets, with the 3D point behind every
visual token and the pose of every camera in the frame of the first image as targets. Whatever is read
from the states of the probe tokens was therefore computed by the frozen layers from the images.
We then refit a fresh readout on the frozen states and score it on 416 held-out ScanNet (Dai et al.,
2017) image sets, 364 of them with more than one view. We report four quantities. Single-view
points are the points of the first image, counted as recovered when they fall within a quarter of the
set’s mean point distance. Fused points are the points of the later images under the same criterion,
which can be recovered only by relating those images to the first. Cross-view bearing is the floor-
plane direction, in four classes, between the points seen at the same grid position in the first image
and in another one. Camera facing records whether the optical axis of a later camera is recovered
within 45◦. Three controls bound what a readout achieves without the geometry, namely shuffling
the targets among sets that share source, task, and number of views, setting the states to zero, and
replacing the images with gray ones.
• Viewpoint probe on SpinBench. S-Space (Shi et al., 2026b) finds three linear axes in a middle
layer of an MLLM along which the positions of objects are encoded. We take the axes of Base
at layer 15 and use them unchanged for all three models, so that a difference between models
is not a difference between axes. SpinBench (Zhang et al., 2026e) asks for the relation between
two objects as seen from a viewpoint other than the camera’s, with or without a stated premise
(144 and 146 questions). On it we compare two answers to the same question: the readout, which
decodes the coordinates of the two objects from the state and rotates them to the queried viewpoint
outside the model, and the model’s own answer. We then replay each model’s own reasoning and
read the state at every mention of the two objects. A camera-frame readout is right when the
state gives the answer after the external rotation, whereas a target-frame readout is right when the
state gives the answer with no rotation, that is, when the model has already transformed it to the
queried viewpoint. This part uses the questions on which the reasoning mentions both objects at
least twice, so that the first and the last mention can be compared.
• Viewpoint probe on reasoning chains. Reasoning chains are teacher-forced through the frozen
model, and the probe tokens of the geometry probe, which were never fit to a transformed target,
are inserted after every viewpoint a chain declares. A fresh readout is fit to the scene in the frame
of the declared viewpoint and scored on 296 held-out chains with 653 declared viewpoints. We
score the points that come from the images other than the viewer’s own by the egocentric quadrant
they fall in, as a balanced accuracy over the four quadrants. The controls are the targets of the
18
other viewpoints declared in the same chain, the tokens before any declaration, zero states, and
targets shuffled among chains. Table 3 gives each model’s strongest control in parentheses.

C Experimental Details
C.1 Benchmark Descriptions
In this section, we describe the four benchmarks used in our experiments:
• MindCube-Tiny (Wang et al., 2026b) has 1,050 questions over two to four views of a scene, in
three settings: among (600), around (250), and rotation (200).
• SPAR-Bench (Zhang et al., 2025) is evaluated on its relational and viewpoint-reasoning tasks
(R&V): 11 of its 20 tasks, 3,969 questions, covering spatial relation, spatial imagination, position
matching, camera motion, and distance comparison. As in its official overall score, we report the
mean of the per-task accuracies.
• BLINK (Fu et al., 2024) is evaluated on its three spatial tasks, multi-view reasoning, relative depth,
and spatial relation, on the validation split (400 questions), whose answers are public.
• ViewSpatial-Bench (Li et al., 2026a) has 5,712 questions that ask for spatial relations from the
perspective of the camera (2,769) and of a person in the scene (2,943). Each group is scored by
the accuracy over its questions, as in the benchmark’s own results.
Evaluation Protocol. Every model in Table 1 that we run from its weights answers without thinking
mode, with greedy decoding and at most 4,096 new tokens, and Appendix C.2 gives the settings
of the general models. The answer is read by strict matching of the option letter in the requested
answer format, without an LLM judge, and an answer that cannot be read is counted as incorrect.
This follows the protocol of EASI (Cai et al., 2025), and the cited numbers are taken from its public
leaderboard1. The average in Table 1 is the mean of the four overall scores.

C.3 SFT Data Construction
In this section, we describe how the data of the SFT stage are constructed: the questions, the reasoning
chains, and the viewpoints that the chains declare, which give the targets of the view loss:
• Question bank.
All methods trained on our backbones draw their questions from one bank
of 16,808 multiple-choice questions, namely 8,808 questions from the training set of Mind-
Cube (Wang et al., 2026b) over two to four views, 4,000 questions of SpatialLadder (Li et al.,
2026d) on relative direction and relative distance over a single image or eight views, and 4,000
multi-view questions of SPAR-7M (Zhang et al., 2025) in seven task types spanning object relation,
spatial imagination, camera motion, position matching, and distance comparison. The questions
of SpatialLadder and SPAR come from ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017) training scenes. SpatialLadder’s
are sampled round-robin over scenes, with the multi-view direction questions stratified so that
no option holds more than 35% of the answers, and SPAR’s with at most five questions per scene
and task type. The boxes and points that a SPAR question refers to are drawn onto its images
with SPAR’s official script. All questions share one format: the wording of the source, one option
per line, and a single instruction to reason first and then answer with an option letter, with every
image resized to at most 640 × 480 pixels. For development we hold out 560 MindCube, 200
SpatialLadder, and 350 SPAR questions by scene, which no training stage uses.
• Reasoning chains. The reasoning chains are written by GPT-6-Astra. For each question the teacher
receives the images, the question as the student sees it, and a top-down map of the scene in text:
the official layout of MindCube or, on ScanNet, the floor positions and extents of the annotated
objects visible in the images together with the camera poses. The student never sees the map. The
teacher writes one sentence per viewpoint: each sentence opens with a phrase, free of commas,
that states where the viewer stands and which way it faces, followed after a comma by what is
seen from there, and a final sentence starting with “So” draws the conclusion and gives the answer.
A chain may mention neither the map nor any coordinate, and may use only the numbers that
the question and its options contain. A chain is kept if its answer is correct and it obeys these
rules, and each question is sampled at most three times. Of the 7,824 questions sent to the teacher,
7,500 chains are kept, 2,500 per source. For S-MIND, a run of spatial tokens is placed after the first
comma of every sentence but the last, i.e., right after each declared viewpoint, without changing
the teacher’s words, and the same chains without spatial tokens are the SFT data of the baselines.
• Declared viewpoints. The pose of a declared viewpoint is a position and a heading on the floor
plane of the first image’s frame, and the view target is z0’s points re-expressed around it: the origin
moves to the position and the forward axis to the heading, while heights are unchanged. When
the chain adopts the viewpoint of an input image, the pose is that camera’s pose as decoded from
z0. When it stands at a camera but faces elsewhere, the position is the decoded camera’s and the
heading points to the named object, or follows another camera’s decoded heading turned by the
stated angle. When it imagines a standpoint, e.g., standing at one object and facing another, the
position is the first object’s and the heading points to the second. The floor positions of objects
come from the annotations of the training scenes, scaled to z0’s frame, and are the only ground
truth in the view target. When it turns relative to where it stands, the previous pose is rotated by
24
the stated angle. The viewpoint is read from the declaring phrase by rules, using only the phrase,
the question, and the scene’s annotations, never the rest of the chain or the answer, and it serves
only as a training target: it never enters the model’s input.
C.4 Training Details
This section details the three training stages of S-MIND, whose SFT data are described in Appendix C.3.
The parameter settings of all stages are in Table 5, and every model is trained once. All experiments
are conducted on four NVIDIA H100 GPUs.
• Geometry pre-training. The spatial tokens of an image set are placed after the images and before
the question. Their input embeddings, the readout, and a LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) on every linear
layer of the language model are trained together on 16,292 posed image sets from ScanNet and
MindCube. The readout is described in Appendix B. After training, the readout is refit on the
frozen model, and this refit readout is the one that SFT keeps frozen.
• SFT. SFT starts from the geometry pre-trained model and trains a new LoRA on the reasoning
chains, whose viewpoint phrases are each followed by a run of spatial tokens. The loss is the
chain’s cross-entropy, which leaves out the inserted tokens except the first of every run, plus the
geometry loss on z0 and the view loss of every z𝑘against z0’s own scene seen from the declared
viewpoint (Appendix C.3), both weighted by 𝜆. Since the first token of every run is predicted, the
model learns to write it, and at inference the rest of the run is appended wherever it does.
• RL. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) starts from the SFT model with a fresh LoRA, on multiple-choice
questions from the same sources and from ScanNet training scenes. The reward is the correctness
of the final answer under strict extraction, with group-normalized advantages and a KL penalty to
the starting model. The spatial tokens inserted during a rollout are context and are not trained.

C.5 Efficiency Setup
Inference. We time SpatialClaw and S-MIND end to end on the same GPU, one NVIDIA H100 PCIe,
with eight questions in flight, the default of SpatialClaw’s official code (Cho et al., 2026). The time of a
question runs from its start to its final answer, the span that SpatialClaw’s runner records. SpatialClaw
runs its official code in its default configuration: Qwen3.5-4B is served by vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023)
with thinking enabled at temperature 0.6, and its 3D reconstruction and segmentation tools run on
the same GPU. S-MIND decodes greedily as in Table 1, and its accuracies are those of Table 1 on the
same questions. As SpatialClaw takes about 100 s per question, ViewSpatial is scored on the first 850
of its 5,712 questions in a fixed random order interleaving its five tasks in proportion to their sizes.
Training cost. Among the methods trained in our setting, 3DThinker (Chen et al., 2026) is the one
that also trains geometry into the model’s states, and its supervision is far heavier than ours. It aligns
latent states in the reasoning with the full aggregator features of VGGT (Wang et al., 2025) for every
training image, 1,374 tokens of 2,048 channels per image (2.8M numbers), which take 192 GB for
our reproduction, through a projector of 36.2M parameters. Its latent steps feed the last hidden state
back as the next input, which standard serving engines do not support, so its rollouts and its inference
decode step by step. S-MIND supervises two 3D points per visual token, global and local, and one
26
pose per camera, at most about 1.8K numbers per image and 44 MB for all 16,292 pre-training sets,
through a readout of 1.9M parameters, and it runs natively in vLLM.

D.3 Design Choices in Geometry Pre-training
Figure 11 and Table 8 compare the design choices of geometry pre-training by the geometry that
the spatial tokens hold on held-out image sets, with differences paired over the same sets and 95%
intervals from resampling them:
• A separate geometry stage with a trainable backbone. Geometry is learned in a stage of its
own, before the reasoning chains, so that the tokens are fit to the images rather than shaped by
the language loss. Pre-training also trains a LoRA on the backbone: with the backbone frozen the
tokens cannot relate the views to each other, as the probe of Section 3 shows for the baselines.
• A light readout that can only read the tokens. The readout is one cross-attention layer without a
residual path. Its queries encode only a grid position or derive from the scene tokens of a view, and
its keys and values are the spatial tokens alone (Appendix B). Whatever it decodes must therefore
be in the tokens, and a stronger readout would only make that less certain.
• Tokens per image instead of a fixed set. A fixed set of 16 tokens is shared by every image of a set,
so each image gets fewer of them as images are added, and the number of images a model can take
is bounded by what the tokens were trained on. With two tokens per image plus a camera group,
every image has its own tokens, carrying the same two ids, and any number of images uses trained
embeddings only. Figure 11a shows where sharing hurts most: on the sets of eight images, two
tokens per image recover 10.11 more points than 16 shared tokens (95% interval 3.47 to 17.73),
and on the sets of three images, where they are 8 tokens in all, they still recover more (55.44%
against 53.02%). It is sharing, not the count, that hurts: halving the shared tokens to 8 costs 2.36
points (−3.31 to −1.32). Four camera tokens instead of two leave the camera position and heading
essentially unchanged and raise the cross-view bearing by 1.05 points.
• More image sets. Figure 11b shows that widening pre-training from 7.3k to 16.3k ScanNet and
MindCube sets, without changing the reasoning data, is the largest single gain: 16.66 more points
on held-out ScanNet sets (15.28 to 18.05), 34.52 more on the sets of eight images, and 5.16 more
on MindCube rooms not seen in training.
• Redundant per-view targets. Besides each visual token’s point in the first image’s frame, the
readout also predicts the same point in the frame of that token’s own camera. The extra target
is redundant, since the first follows from the second and the camera, but it gives the tokens a
supervision of depth that does not depend on the camera being right. On 7.3k sets it raises the
28
recovered points by 5.83 (4.75 to 6.97) and far depth by 9.87 points. In the final setting, both
point targets are weighted by a learned confidence.

E A Benchmark for Spatial Capability beyond Answer Accuracy
We construct a compact benchmark that measures, independently of the answers a model generates,
how accurately object positions can be read from its representations and whether they follow a change
of reference view. Figure 14 summarizes the protocol: the model stays frozen, and a linear probe
reads the position of each object from the hidden state of its name.
Scenes. The test set contains 100 scenes, 50 real rooms and 50 synthetic scenes, each with six
objects and three views. The real rooms use the RGB views released with SpatialLadder (Li et al.,
2026d), with object centers and camera frames from the ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017) annotations. The
synthetic scenes hold compound objects on platforms and shelves, with partitions, height differences,
and partial occlusion, and their geometry and cameras are recorded by the renderer. Objects are
identified by name in the real rooms and by the labels A–F in the synthetic scenes.
Paired inputs. The central comparison presents the same ordered images and object list twice,
changing only the instruction from “Use image 1 as the reference view” to “Use image 2 as the ref-
erence view”, and examines the object pairs whose front–back relation reverses between the two
camera frames. Further pairs keep the geometry and reorder the images or the object mentions;
synthetic pairs also recolor the objects, relabel them, or move one of them.
Readout. Following the object-token readout of S-Space (Shi et al., 2026b), we take the hidden state
at the last subword of each object name and fit a linear ridge probe to the object’s 3D position in the
right–up–forward frame of the reference camera:

F S-MIND as a Spatial Expert for General Models
We further examine whether S-MIND can serve a larger general model through language: as Figure 15
outlines, the 4B RL model acts as a spatial tool for Qwen3.5-27B, with both models frozen.
Pipeline. Qwen3.5-27B first routes the question in a separate pass, given its text, answer choices,
and number of images. For a visual spatial question, S-MIND receives the question, the choices, and
the ordered images, runs once, and returns its spatial analysis with the final answer selection removed.
The 27B model then reads this analysis alongside the original inputs, checks the reference viewpoint
and the object identities, and produces the answer itself; if the analysis is unusable, it answers from
the original inputs alone. Any other question is answered by the 27B model with its original prompt
and decoding settings, outside the routing conversation.
