Abstract
Learning for dexterous manipulation is limited by the scarcity of high-quality demonstrations:
retargeting from human video is imprecise, teleoperation gives no force feedback to the operator,
and exoskeleton-based collection is hard to scale up. We present PyRUA-Dex, a framework that
enables coding agents to develop dexterous manipulation through programmatic policy, without the
need of demonstrations. The programmatic policy is developed in two stages: during exploration,
multiple agents explore in parallel for task skills and ways to compose them, and a distilling agent
condenses the successful attempts into a task library and one program. During refinement, this
program is improved through randomized layouts for enhanced robustness and execution efficiency.
In simulated environments, PyRUA-Dex achieves SOTA on Bench2Dex with a score of 72.9 and
reaches 81.0% success on DexJoCo under full randomization, ahead of every trained policy on both
benchmarks. In real world experiments on a bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, PyRUA-Dex
learns to play piano, write calligraphy, and other tasks efficiently: learning a new piece from sheet
music takes as little as 30 minutes. These results suggest that PyRUA-Dex is an effective framework
for developing dexterous manipulation policies with coding agents in the absence of high quality
dexterous manipulation demonstrations.

the strongest trained policies succeed in little more than half of the episodes [2, 19], and all of them degrade
when a layout’s appearance or object placement is perturbed.
Coding agents offer an alternative to collecting demonstrations. A programmatic policy represents behavior as a
program rather than as network weights, so it can be read, tested and verified [39, 40]. Language models already
write programs that control robots [16, 20, 34] and improve them from environment feedback [23, 32, 41, 45],
and general coding agents run, test and debug software for hours [26, 48]. Robot programs, however, are
typically written once from a prompt [20] or found by search in a small domain-specific language [21, 38], and
are not refined for robustness across layouts. For dexterous hands, the agent has so far stayed in the loop
instead: it observes and acts in every test episode [31], consumes millions of tokens per episode, and still fails
most episodes within the benchmark’s step budget. We ask whether a coding agent can learn a dexterous task
once, during development, and produce a program that then runs on its own reliably without per-episode cost.
Learning such a program poses two distinct problems. The first is discovering a solution: the grasps, the
2
ordering of the two hands and the motions that complete each stage of the task. This is a search problem that
requires many attempts on a few layouts, and it proceeds faster when parallel agents share their progress. The
second is making the solution robust. A dexterous task consists of consecutive stages, and its success rate is
the product of the stage success rates, the probability of completing each stage given that the preceding stages
were completed. A program that succeeds on one layout can therefore fail on most others: five stages at 80%
each give an overall success rate of only 33%. In our experiments, most failed runs end with steps to spare,
at a stage that was not completed (Section 4.3), so failures come from unreliable stages rather than from
the step budget. Because the overall rate cannot exceed the lowest stage rate, robustness requires measuring
every stage over many layouts and improving the least reliable one.
PyRUA-Dex addresses these two problems in two stages (Figure 2). In the exploration stage (Stage 1),
multiple coding agents work on one task in parallel in simulation. They write and run code, rewind the
simulator to retry a stage, use the number of completed stages as feedback, and share their progress with one
another. A distilling agent then condenses the successful programs into a task library and one program. In
the refinement stage (Stage 2), an agent refines this program over many randomized layouts. It measures
the stage success rates, improves the least reliable stage, and keeps a revision only if it performs no worse
on layouts that no agent has seen. Agents and programs control every robot through the same codebox, a
layer of hand, arm and sensing calls built from the robot’s description, so the procedure applies to any hand
the codebox can model. The resulting policy is general Python and runs frozen. It takes the inputs of an
ordinary policy (calibrated cameras, joint positions and fingertip touch), calls no language model, and runs at
the robot’s control rate. Because it measures the scene in every episode, it remains reliable when a layout is
perturbed.
On Bench2Dex, the frozen programs reach a score of 72.9, a new SOTA ahead of every trained policy, and at
the benchmark’s step budget they succeed 2.4 times as often as the same model, GPT-6 Astra, acting online
(Sections 4.2 and 4.4). On DexJoCo, a second benchmark with Allegro hands [42], they succeed in 81.0% of
episodes under full randomization, against 40.3% for the strongest trained policy, and are above each trained
policy on every task (Section 4.5). On a real bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, the same two stages
run in a calibrated simulation that is updated with the robot’s feedback after each round, and the frozen
programs then run on the robot (Section 5). They play piano, write calligraphy and unpack objects, and
learning a new piano piece from sheet music takes about 30 minutes.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• An agent-driven framework for dexterous manipulation. We propose PyRUA-Dex, which enables
coding agents to develop programmatic policies for dexterous manipulation without demonstrations.
The resulting policy runs as frozen code at test time, with no trained model and no online agent.
• Two-stage programmatic robot policy development using tactile feedback. Parallel agents first explore
in simulation for task skills and their composition. The skills are then refined over randomized layouts
for robustness and efficiency, with fingertip tactile feedback used throughout.
• Comprehensive simulated and real-world experiments across diverse robot hands and tasks. PyRUA-
Dex achieves SOTA on Bench2Dex. On a real bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, with system
identification of the simulated hands, PyRUA-Dex develops code programs to perform piano playing,
calligraphy and other tasks efficiently.

Related Work
Our work lies at the intersection of dexterous manipulation, programmatic policies and coding for robots.
Learning dexterous manipulation.
Reinforcement learning has produced dexterous skills in simulation
and on hardware [8, 28], at the price of task-specific rewards and large-scale training per skill. Imitation
learning from teleoperated demonstrations [10, 51] and vision-language-action models [3–5, 18, 25] are now
the dominant recipe, with data generators reducing the number of human episodes [17, 24], and recent
benchmarks evaluate them across hands and settings [7, 22, 42, 43, 49]. To reach hardware, a common route

Programmatic policies.
Programmatic reinforcement learning represents a policy as a program in a
symbolic language, which is easier to interpret and verify than a neural policy [39, 40]; to keep the search over
programs tractable, the languages are small and the programs short [21, 38]. Language models write such
programs directly: they compose perception and motion primitives from a task description [16, 20, 34], keep
libraries of programs that worked [41], refine reward code from training outcomes [23] and generate tasks and
simulations [44, 47]. These systems write a program once or improve it over a handful of trials, mostly for
parallel-jaw grippers. PyRUA-Dex writes general Python programs for multi-fingered hands and develops
them against success rates measured over hundreds of randomized layouts.
Coding agents for robots.
A coding agent can control a robot directly, observing and issuing commands
throughout every episode [12, 30, 31]; it keeps its full reasoning at test time but pays for it in tokens, time
and step budget in every episode. The alternative is to let agents develop programs that then run without
them. RAX scales exploration by coding agents to as many as 256 episodes per task and compiles what
it finds into modular programs [14], and the PyRUA family studies token-efficient execution [33], data
generation [50], self-improvement on real robots [45] and what agents learn from a benchmark’s success
criteria [15]. PyRUA-Dex belongs to this second line and targets dexterous hands: parallel agents explore
a task in simulation, a second stage refines the resulting program stage by stage, and the program that is
handed over calls no language model.
3
Method
PyRUA-Dex learns one programmatic policy per task in simulation and deploys it as a frozen program (Figure 2).
Learning proceeds in two stages, both carried out by general coding agents: Codex CLI sessions [26] of GPT-6
Astra [27], each equipped with a shell, its own simulator instance and a library of perception and control
primitives shared by all robots (the codebox). Exploration (Stage 1) searches for a program that completes the
task at all; refinement (Stage 2) improves how reliably that program completes the task on unseen layouts.

in the form of a program πτ. At step t it maps the observation history to an action,

3.2
Policy Representation
Program structure.
Every program has the same staged form: a setup function that measures the
scene, and one function fk per stage, executed in order by a fixed runner within the episode’s step budget
(Appendix B, Figure 7). Each fk starts from where fk−1 left the robot and the scene, works toward stage k
and returns whether, by the program’s own measurement, the stage holds. A failed function does not abort
the episode: later functions check on entry what they need, and the runner calls the failed functions again
while steps remain. This form lets measurements be reported per stage, functions written by different agents
be combined, and a single stage be executed in isolation.
Perception and control primitives.
Hands are what makes these tasks hard to program: the twelve
hands of Bench2Dex have 6 to 22 driven joints, named and arranged differently on each robot, and whether a
5
grasp holds is revealed only by touch. Agents and programs therefore control every robot through one library
of primitives, which is generated from the robot’s description file. It models each hand as fingers and a thumb
whose joints are grouped by function (bend, curl, spread), derives which object widths each set of fingers can
hold, and exposes hand primitives at that level: shape the hand, grasp an object of a measured size, close
until it is held, check that it is still held. Contact is read from the fingertip tactile maps and from the lag
between commanded and measured joint positions. Arm primitives move a frame of the hand to a target
and report an unreachable or colliding pose before moving, and perception primitives locate objects from the
calibrated cameras with SAM 3 [6]. Every primitive returns an outcome with its reason, so a program can
check each step and retry. The same primitives drive all hands in our experiments (Appendix B).
3.3
Stage 1: Exploration
Exploration runs eight agents on a task in parallel. Each receives the task library, initialized from the task’s
definition, a training layout of its own, and one of the stages not yet solved as its assignment.
Exploration with state resets.
An agent controls the robot interactively through the primitives and
queries the simulator’s stage count after each attempt. It can checkpoint the simulator’s state during an
episode and reset to a checkpoint, eight times per layout, so that it retries one stage many times without
repeating the stages before it.
Shared memory.
Every attempt an agent finishes is written to a memory shared by all agents of the task,
with the stages it reached and the code that reached them. A dedicated agent curates the memory, and each
agent sees what is new in its next tool reply, so a stage solved by one agent becomes the starting point of the
others.
Program evaluation and distillation.
Each agent ends by writing a program that reproduces what it
achieved, and every program that reached a stage is evaluated without the agent on eight fresh training
layouts. A distilling agent then reads all evaluation records and writes the task library: what is established
about the task, how each stage was solved and how often, with code, and what remains unsolved. It also
selects the task’s program: the evaluated program with the best record (completions, then mean stage rate,
then fewer steps), or a composition that takes each stage function from the agent whose program reaches that
stage most often, itself evaluated before it can be selected. The selected program replaces the current one
only if it is no worse on the validation layouts.
3.4
Stage 2: Refinement
Refinement starts from the program and task library of exploration, and a single agent edits the program.
Stage-wise failure analysis.
Before the agent starts, the program runs without the agent on 32 fresh
training layouts, and the result is broken down by stage: for each stage, how many layouts entered it, how
many reached it, how its function ended on the others and how much of the step budget was left. Layouts
are grouped by the first stage they missed, which gives the stage success rates of equation 2 and shows the
bottleneck before any change is made.
Bottleneck refinement.
The agent works on one stage at a time, the bottleneck first. It opens failing
layouts interactively, repairs the stage function with the help of state resets, and then runs the program
without itself in the loop on tens of fresh layouts. To keep these measurements cheap, a partial rollout executes
the program only up to the stage under work, so thirty layouts return in about the time those stages take on
one. During refinement an episode has twice the benchmark’s step budget, so that a stage is made reliable
before it is made fast.
Program selection.
After every 160 training layouts, the current version is evaluated without the agent, at
the benchmark’s step budget, on 32 fresh layouts and on a fixed held-out set of 32 layouts of which the agent
6
sees only the counts. The agent stops when the task completes on most held-out layouts, when the stage
success rates stop improving, or when its 1,500 layouts are used up. A distilling agent selects the version
with the best held-out record, and, as in exploration, this version replaces the current program only if it is no
worse on the validation layouts; otherwise the program from exploration is kept.
3.5
Sim-to-Real Transfer
For a real robot, both stages run in a simulation of the robot’s scene, and the robot itself only executes
frozen programs (Figure 2, right). We build the simulation from the platform’s calibration and from objects
reconstructed from photographs (real-to-sim), randomize in every layout what the calibration leaves uncertain,
and run exploration once. Refinement then alternates with the robot: after each round the program is
executed on the robot, the discrepancies it reveals are written back into the simulation as updated physical
parameters or task rules, and the next round refines the same program in the updated simulation (Section 5).
4
Experiments
We evaluate PyRUA-Dex on two simulated benchmarks for dexterous manipulation and, in Section 5, on a
real bimanual platform. The simulation experiments answer four questions, each about a separate comparison:
• Q1: Do programs written and iteratively refined by coding agents outperform policies trained on demon-
strations? (Section 4.2)
• Q2: What does refinement contribute beyond exploration? (Section 4.3)
• Q3: Is a frozen program better than the same model acting online? (Section 4.4)
• Q4: Does the method generalize to another benchmark, with a different hand and simulator? (Section 4.5)
4.1
Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and metrics.
We use Bench2Dex [49], with 26 bimanual tasks, each paired with one of
12 arm–hand embodiments, and DexJoCo [42], with 11 tasks for Allegro hands; together they cover twelve
dexterous hands with 6 to 22 driven joints (Figure 1e,f). On Bench2Dex we follow the leaderboard protocol [2]:
every task is evaluated on four channels of 50 episodes, the unperturbed layouts (None) and layouts perturbed
along the invariance axis, which leaves the correct action unchanged (Inv.), along the equivariance axis, which
changes it (Equi.), or both (Full). We report the success rate (SR), the latched stage completion rate (LSCR,
the mean fraction of stages reached), the efficiency (Eff, the expert’s steps divided by the steps of successful
episodes) and the leaderboard’s Score = 0.45 SR + 0.45 LSCR + 10 Eff, with SR and LSCR in percent. On
DexJoCo we report the success rate over three seeds of 50 episodes per task, with the objects randomized
(rand-obj) and with cameras, lighting and textures randomized as well (rand-full). Appendix A describes
both benchmarks.
Implementation.
All agents are Codex CLI sessions of GPT-6 Astra at the highest reasoning effort, with
no memory across sessions. Exploration uses eight agents per task, each with eight training layouts and
eight state resets per layout. Refinement uses one agent per task and at most 1,500 training layouts, with
an evaluation on 32 fresh and 32 held-out layouts after every 160 (the budget B of equation 4). A program
replaces the current one only if it is no worse on the 32 validation layouts. No budget limits time or tokens.
The final program of each task is evaluated once on the benchmark’s test layouts, with access to the program’s
own inputs only.
Baselines.
We compare with two groups of methods (Appendix A lists them individually). Trained policies
learn from the benchmarks’ teleoperated demonstrations: on Bench2Dex, every leaderboard entry that reports
all four channels, covering imitation learning (IL), vision-language-action (VLA), world-action (WAM) and
visuo-tactile action (VTA) models [1, 2, 13, 19]; on DexJoCo, the benchmark’s five trained policies [42]. The
online agent is the GPT-6 Astra agent of SenseNova [31], which uses the same model as PyRUA-Dex but
controls the robot in every test episode after preparing in context on the task’s training data.

4.2
PyRUA-Dex Sets a New SOTA on Bench2Dex
PyRUA-Dex scores 72.9 on Bench2Dex, a new SOTA (Table 1, Figure 3a). Its success rate of 60.6% and
stage completion of 82.3% exceed those of every trained policy, including ME-Dex 1.5, which fine-tunes one
checkpoint per task. The lead is largest in stage completion (Figure 3b): when a program fails, it has usually
completed part of the task, because each stage function checks its own result and the next one starts from
what is there.

4.3
Refinement Makes Explored Programs Reliable
Refinement raises the success rate from 44.6% to 60.6% and the score from 59.1 to 72.9, with gains on 20 of the
26 tasks (Appendix C). The largest gains are on stationery sorting (6.5% →49.0%), baking-tray preparation
(26.0% →68.0%) and the hammer task, which exploration never completed (0% →37.0%).
Explored programs fail at a single stage, with budget to spare.
We classify the 341 failed validation
runs of the programs after exploration on the 22 tasks they completed at least once (Figure 4a). In 68% of
them the program had returned with at least 30% of the step budget unused, and in 59% one stage function
had failed once and every later function had returned at once; only 2% ran out of steps. Calling failed stage
functions again puts the unused budget to work, and refining the bottleneck targets the stage that fails. On
task 12 (Figure 4c), the program after exploration places both screwdrivers on almost every held-out layout
but strikes the wooden block on 4 of 32 and returns the hammer on none; after refinement, on 17 and 15.
The gain carries over to unseen layouts.
Held-out completions rise over successive versions on most
tasks (Figure 4b). After exploration and after refinement the programs complete 43.6% and 61.7% of the 832
validation layouts, close to their test success rates of 44.6% and 60.6%. In answer to Q2, refinement adds 16.0
points by repairing the stage at which an explored program fails, and the gain holds on unseen layouts.

4.5
The Method Generalizes to a Second Benchmark
DexJoCo differs from Bench2Dex in its simulator (MuJoCo), its hand (the 16-DoF Allegro) and what it reveals
during development: it defines no stages, so an agent learns only whether an episode succeeded. PyRUA-Dex
succeeds in 88.5% of the rand-obj episodes and 81.0% of the rand-full ones, against 54.1% and 40.3% for
the strongest trained policy, π0.5 (Table 2), and it exceeds the best trained policy on each of the 11 tasks
in both settings (Appendix C, Figure 9). One program serves both settings and loses 7.5 points under full
randomization, whereas the trained policies, each trained for one setting, lose 9.7 to 30.4.
The two stages and the comparison with the online agent behave as on Bench2Dex: refinement adds 29.8
points under rand-obj and 16.9 under rand-full, and at the benchmark’s step budget the online agent succeeds
in 78.2% of the rand-full episodes at 7.5M tokens per episode, against 81.0% for the frozen programs at none,
and needs twice the budget to match them (Figure 9c). In answer to Q4, the method generalizes: unchanged,
and with an episode’s success as its only feedback, it leads on a second benchmark with a different hand and
simulator.

refinement is repeated in rounds. After each round the program is executed on the robot, where a safety

6
Limitations and Future Work
Limitations.
Coding agents write motion well and sustained contact poorly. The programs are strongest
where geometry, order and timing decide a task, and weakest where continuous control of force and slip is
required: trained policies lead on the frying pan, the pour from hand to hand and the jigsaw puzzle, and
assembly is the lowest of the DexJoCo tasks (34.0% under object randomization). A program acts through
explicit targets and thresholds, and the simulated fingertip sensors render contact coarsely (the tactile maps of
Bench2Dex respond only on flat faces square to the pad), so the programs infer contact mainly from joint lag.
Refinement strengthens stages that already hold on some layouts; a stage that never holds remains a problem
of exploration. Finally, each task is learned separately, and on the real robot we show the frozen programs
running but measure their reliability in the calibrated simulation rather than over repeated hardware trials.
Future work.
The most direct extension is to let a program hand its contact phases to learned controllers
and to develop it against a more faithful tactile simulation. A second direction is transfer across tasks: a task
library stores what worked, with code and measured rates, and the piano pieces show that a skill distilled
from it carries over within a family; seeding the exploration of a new task with the libraries of related tasks
would extend this across families. A third direction is a systematic evaluation on hardware, with success rates
over repeated trials on the real robot.
