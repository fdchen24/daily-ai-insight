World Models as Native Latent Action Models
Qinyue Li1*, Weijie Qiao1,2*, Qingyi Tao1*†, Zerui Gong1, Zhe Yang2, Quanzhou Li2,
Taolin Zhang2, Jinglin He1, Zhonghua Wu1, Chen Change Loy2,3
1SenseTime Research, Singapore, 2S-Lab, Nanyang Technological University, 3ACE Robotics
*Equal contribution, †Project lead
Large and diverse video sources, such as egocentric human video and recordings from heterogeneous robots,
carry rich dynamics but come either without actions or without a shared action space. Latent action models
(LAMs) can recover these dynamics, but they train their inverse- and forward-dynamics models (IDM,
FDM) from scratch, trading off pixel reconstruction against latent capacity and accuracy. Pretrained video
world models natively generate dynamics and produce high-fidelity subsequent frames, yet represent them
densely, entangled across the hidden states of every layer. Viewing the world model as a LAM, we show
that these dynamics can be extracted with minimal changes to the model. Native LAM operates one
pretrained video diffusion transformer as both IDM and FDM, with learnable dynamics registers as the
only causal bridge between the two, and trains on counterfactual temporal pairs that differ in motion alone,
so that the registers distill a video’s dynamics into a compact native latent action. We further propose a
pretraining paradigm for vision-language-action (VLA) policies, Pretraining Actions from World models
(PAWlicy), in which Native LAM serves as a dynamics teacher on large-scale video; unlike world action
models, it generates video neither during training nor during inference. Native latent actions outperform
existing LAMs on the LARY benchmark in both action classification and control regression, and replay the
same motion on new robots, in new scenes, and from human hands to robots, although no transfer objective
is used in training. With a 3.8B model, PAWlicy reaches a 93.6% average success rate on RoboTwin 2.0
trained with domain-randomized demonstrations, the highest among VLAs. On RoboDojo, it achieves
competitive results on every dimension we evaluate, and its Precision, evaluated by us, is on par with the
leading entries of the public leaderboard. On real robots, it improves over the π0.5 baseline on both tasks.
Both stages are trained only on publicly available data on top of a publicly released world model.
Keywords: Latent Action Models; Video World Models; Vision-Language-Action Models; Robot Learning.

Introduction
Video is the most abundant record of how agents act in the physical world, spanning robots of many embodiments,
human manipulation, and simulation. Much of it cannot serve directly as action supervision, since egocentric human
video provides no robot action labels and robot datasets record actions in embodiment-specific spaces. Latent
action models (LAMs) [9, 71, 94] address this gap. An inverse-dynamics model (IDM) infers a latent action from
an observed change, and a forward-dynamics model (FDM) reproduces the change from it. The resulting shared
action space is used to pretrain vision-language-action (VLA) policies [11, 20, 45, 62, 94] and to control world
models [29, 30, 74].
The quality of this space, however, is limited by how it is learned. A typical LAM trains its IDM and FDM from
scratch (Fig. 1a), so the objective trades off pixel reconstruction against the capacity and accuracy of the latent.
Training is also fragile [60, 94]. A loose bottleneck can leak the future frame [71], a tight one cannot describe the
motion [60, 94], and in between the latent drifts toward camera motion or distractors unrelated to the agent [11, 60].
We start from a simple observation. A video world model is natively an FDM (Fig. 1b). Pretrained on large-scale robot
and human video, it carries a strong forward-dynamics prior on how scenes evolve, renders the future faithfully, and
implicitly captures inverse dynamics, which world action models (WAMs) exploit to decode actions [44, 46, 95, 108].

A pretrained world model can thus be viewed as a unified LAM whose learning signal need not be spent on rendering.
Its dynamics, however, are entangled with appearance across dense hidden states, with no compact representation
from which actions can be read. The challenge is to expose these dynamics natively through a compact action
interface.
Native LAM (Fig. 1c) appends a short, temporally ordered sequence of learnable dynamics registers to a pretrained
video diffusion transformer and runs it in two modes, with no separate encoder or decoder and the registers as
the only causal bridge between them. In IDM mode, the registers read a clean clip and yield the latent action. In
FDM mode, the same network regenerates the future from the first frame and this latent alone, with its original
flow-matching objective and no text. Training on counterfactual temporal pairs, clips that share a first frame but
differ in motion, forces the latent to carry the motion. We term the resulting representation native latent actions, as
it is produced and consumed by the world model itself rather than an auxiliary encoder–decoder pair. Native LAM
thereby condenses the thousands of high-dimensional tokens that the world model processes for a clip (3,456 tokens
of dimension 2,560, using Kairos 3.1 [37] as the pretrained video DiT) into 32 latent action tokens of dimension 128,
without changing the network architecture or adding a separate encoder or decoder.
We validate native latent actions in two respects. Quantitatively, they outperform existing LAMs in both action
classification and control regression on the LARY benchmark (Sec. 4.2.1). Qualitatively, a latent action extracted
from a robot video and replayed in FDM mode from a different first frame reproduces the same motion in a new
scene, on another robot, or with a human hand (Fig. 1d), although training uses no transfer objective.
Because they can be extracted from any video, native latent actions also enable a new way to pretrain policies

(Fig. 1e). We propose Pretraining Actions from World models (PAWlicy), in which the frozen Native LAM labels
large-scale robot, human, and simulated video, and a VLA learns to predict these latents and to act on them.
Unlike WAMs [44, 46, 95, 108], which make the policy generate appearance-dominated future video through a
multi-billion-parameter generator that is optimized during training and sometimes also run at inference, PAWlicy
keeps the world model’s dynamics and discards its rendering in both phases. Teacher latents are computed once
and cached, supervision is compact, and inference runs a standard-size VLA. Both Native LAM and PAWlicy are
trained only on publicly available datasets. On RoboTwin 2.0 [19], PAWlicy reaches a 93.6% average success rate,
the highest in Tab. 5, and with clean demonstrations alone it still outperforms π0.5 [64] trained with the same past
frame and augmentation by 5.5 points. On RoboDojo [18], it leads the compared methods on all four dimensions we
evaluate, and its Precision, evaluated by us, is on par with the leading entries of the public leaderboard. On a real
robot, it lifts π0.5 from 23.3% to 76.7% on stacking three blocks and from 33.3% to 90.0% on putting shoes in a box.
In summary, our contributions are:
• Native LAM, in which a pretrained video world model serves as both IDM and FDM, bridged only by dynamics
registers, without a separate encoder or decoder.
• A training recipe of counterfactual temporal pairs and a text-free FDM mode that makes the world model rely
on its latent, applied to 46 robot, human, and simulation sources.
• PAWlicy, a VLA pretraining paradigm on native latent actions, with evidence that these latents are action-
informative and transferable and improve over π0.5 in simulation and the real world.

Related Work
Latent action models. LAPO [71] and Genie [9] showed that an IDM/FDM pair trained to reconstruct future
observations recovers a latent action space from unlabeled video. Most later LAMs [11, 20, 30, 41, 49, 94, 97, 100]
follow this recipe and train their IDM and FDM from scratch. Nikulin et al. [60] show that, with distractors, the
latents tend to collapse onto pixel change. Many LAMs further constrain the latent during training. LAPA [94] and
Moto [21] quantize it into tokens, RotVLA [45] models it as rotations, ALAM [78] regularizes it for composition and
reversal consistency, and UniVLA [11] conditions it on language. villa-X [20] adds proprioceptive supervision, which
is closest to our kinematic grounding. Latent actions serve world models. AdaWorld [29] and DreamDojo [30] use
them as the control input of world models learned from unlabeled video, and LD4WAM [74] learns latent dynamics
from human video and distills them from generated futures to condition a world action model. In these methods the
latent actions serve a world model but are still extracted by a separate LAM. Native LAM reverses this relation:
the world model itself extracts the latent action, and its own generator is the decoder.
World models for policy learning. Pretrained video generators such as Wan [83] and robot-oriented world models
such as Kairos [37] encode strong dynamics priors, and several lines of work bring them into policy learning.
Video-as-policy methods generate a future and recover actions with an inverse-dynamics model [26, 79]. World
action models train a video generator and an action generator jointly, so that one model predicts both future frames
and actions [8, 12, 13, 15, 40, 44, 46, 47, 53, 74, 108]; Fast-WAM [95] shows that much of their benefit comes from
video co-training rather than test-time imagination; it keeps video generation as a training objective and skips it
at inference. Alignment methods instead supervise the policy, or its future tokens, with representations of future
observations—generated images [103], video-model features [34], or frozen-encoder embeddings [3, 55, 66, 101, 107].
In all three, the policy is tied to dense visual targets that are dominated by appearance, which recent studies
identify as a source of brittleness under visual shift [55, 66]. Native LAM uses the world model differently: it distills
the model’s dynamics into a compact latent action, and PAWlicy aligns to that latent alone, so the policy neither
renders pixels nor carries a video backbone.
Visual representations for action. Self-supervised image and video encoders [4, 75] are widely used as visual
backbones for policies. LARY [59] probes such encoders and LAMs for semantic actions and low-level robot control.
Encoder features are dense, with hundreds of patch tokens per frame, whereas a latent action is a compact bottleneck
of a few tokens per segment. We therefore compare with embodied LAMs under LARY’s protocol and show that a
compact world-model-derived latent action is more action-informative than existing ones.

Method
We first train Native LAM, which turns a pretrained video world model into a latent action model by running it in
two modes linked by dynamics registers (Fig. 2; Secs. 3.1 and 3.2). The trained Native LAM then serves as a frozen
dynamics teacher for PAWlicy: it extracts native latent actions from a mixture of robot, human, and simulated
video, on which a VLA is pretrained and then fine-tuned on target-robot demonstrations (Sec. 3.3).

3.2 Making the world model rely on the latent
Counterfactual temporal pairs. A pretrained world model that sees the first frame can already imagine a plausible
future. Trained directly on video from its own pretraining domain, it suffers from posterior collapse: the FDM
ignores the newly initialized latent pathway and leaves the registers uninformative (Sec. 4.2.4). We remove this
shortcut. For each clip x we draw a time map ϕ and build x′ = x ◦ϕ from the same source video, with x′
0 = x0
(Fig. 2a). The map ϕ holds the first frame (static), pauses mid-clip (pause), changes speed mid-clip (speed), retimes
the motion toward the same endpoint (timing), or plays backward into the preceding frames or faster when the
source allows. Frames are taken at native indices: nothing is interpolated, padded, or extrapolated. Both clips are
read independently in IDM mode; in FDM mode they share the first frame, the context, the noise ϵ, and τ. The
two generation problems are identical except for the latent, so the only way to solve both is for ˆa and ˆa′ to carry
the difference in motion.

several horizons after the segment start: base-frame translation, rotation log(RtargetR⊤
start), and gripper change.

3.3 PAWlicy: pretraining actions from world models
PAWlicy is a pretraining paradigm in which the trained Native LAM acts as a frozen dynamics teacher. The teacher
assigns native latent actions to every video, irrespective of whether actions were recorded, and a VLA is trained to
predict these latents and to condition its actions on them. We instantiate the paradigm with three experts that
share attention (Fig. 3): a vision-language model (VLM) that encodes the observation and instruction, a latent
action DiT that predicts native latent actions, and an action DiT that generates motor commands.
Reconstructing the past to predict the future. The VLM is pretrained for vision-language understanding, and its
representations are not trained to match the latent space of Native LAM. Predicting future latents directly from
them asks the latent action DiT to map into this space and to forecast dynamics that have not yet been observed,
all at once. We ease this by adding a past latent group to the target and a past frame to the input. Native LAM
encodes eight groups per clip; we truncate the target ˆa∗

Experiments
We evaluate what native latent actions capture and how they transfer (Sec. 4.2), then test PAWlicy in simulation
and on a real robot and analyze its compute cost (Sec. 4.3).
4.1 Setup
Implementation of Native LAM. Native LAM is trained on 46 video sources: real-robot datasets (65% sampling
rate), human egocentric and hand-object video (30%), and simulation (5%). We use main, head, or external cameras
only. Starting from the public backbone, we train for 13k iterations on 32 GPUs. Only AgiBot World 2026 and
DROID provide kinematic grounding; all other data are video only, without action or text labels (Tab. 1). All
human video comes from publicly released datasets, used under their licenses and the consent and de-identification
procedures of their creators; we collect no new human data.
Implementation of PAWlicy. PAWlicy is pretrained on 16 datasets: 12 of the Native LAM sources and four further
datasets. The simulation benchmarks RoboTwin 2.0 and RoboDojo are excluded from PAWlicy pretraining. The
frozen Native LAM provides a latent target for every clip, and the action DiT is supervised on the robot datasets
marked in Tab. 1. For robot data, the wrist-camera views of each episode are added to the VLM input. We pretrain
PAWlicy for 50k iterations on 32 GPUs with a global batch size of 512.
Benchmarks. LARY [59] probes representations for the robot actions underlying a video. For closed-loop control,
RoboTwin 2.0 [19] provides 50 bimanual tasks with clean and domain-randomized scenes and tests robustness to
visual variation; RoboDojo [18], a recent benchmark with a public leaderboard, groups 42 simulation tasks into five
capability dimensions; and two tasks on a real AgileX NERO robot, stacking three blocks and putting shoes in a

4.2 Native LAM as a latent action model
4.2.1 Action evaluation on LARY
LARY [59] probes a frozen representation of a video for the actions it depicts, on two tracks: semantic action classi-
fication on composite human and robot sets, and regression of the underlying end-effector actions on CALVIN [56],
VLABench [99], RoboCOIN [88], and AgiBot-Beta [10]. For the regression track we encode the source video segment
of each sample, since Native LAM reads clips rather than frame pairs.
Table 2 shows that Native LAM is the strongest latent action model on both tracks. It reaches 71.77% classification
accuracy and 0.25 regression error on average, against 70.75% and 0.37 for WLA3, the best previous LAM. The
margin holds on every dataset, and is largest on the real-robot sets RoboCOIN (0.36 against 0.55) and AgiBot-Beta
(0.41 against 0.57). None of the four regression datasets is used to train Native LAM: AgiBot World 2026 is collected
on the G2 rather than the G1 of AgiBot-Beta, so the two share no trajectories, and the other three are unseen.
Native LAM stays frozen during probing.
4.2.2 Motion that transfers and interpolates
During training, each latent is decoded only from the first frame of its own video. At test time, we read a latent from
one video in IDM mode and replay it in FDM mode from a different first frame (Fig. 4), changing the embodiment
(a), the scene (b), the agent from a human to a robot hand (c), or the domain from simulation to a photorealistic
scene (d). In every case the generated video performs the source motion with the new agent in the new scene: the
latent captures what is done, largely independently of who performs it and in which scene.
Interpolation tests whether the latent space is organized by motion or by appearance. We replay (1−β)ˆastill +βˆafull,
mixing the latents of a still clip and a full-action clip, from the same first frame (Fig. 5). Each intermediate β
yields one coherent motion that travels part of the way, further as β grows, whereas blending the two videos’ pixels
superimposes static and moving arms. A mixture of latents is thus an intermediate motion, not an intermediate
image.

4.2.4 Counterfactual pairs prevent posterior collapse
Without counterfactual pairs, the pretrained FDM learns to ignore the latent (Sec. 3.2). Gθ is initialized from
Kairos 3.1, pretrained on large-scale robot and human manipulation video, and already predicts canonical trajectories
from v0 alone, while the dynamics registers r, the output MLP, and the adapter MLPin(ˆa) start from random
weights. Optimization therefore suppresses the adapter pathway instead of routing dynamics through r; gradients
into r vanish, and FDM rollouts become insensitive to ˆa (register bypass). The pairs remove this shortcut, since the
two flow-matching targets differ only through ˆa and ˆa′.
The collapse appears early. Figure 7 compares two runs after 2k steps that differ only in whether counterfactual
pairs are used, together with the final model at 13k steps. In the source clip the left arm grasps a tissue. Without
pairs, the FDM sends the arm to the cup whether the latent comes from this clip or from its still counterfactual:
the latent is ignored, and the model hallucinates a reach for the cup from the first frame alone. With counterfactual
pairs, the still latent already yields a still rollout at 2k steps, while the moving latent is not yet followed, and at 13k
steps both rollouts match their sources.

Conclusion and Future Work
We show that a pretrained video world model can serve as a native latent action model. Through dynamics registers,
Native LAM distills motion information entangled with appearance into a compact latent that transfers across scenes
and embodiments and interpolates into coherent intermediate motions. As a frozen teacher, it enables PAWlicy
to learn from robot, human, and simulated video without generating video during policy training or inference,
improving a generalist VLA in simulation and on a real robot.
This study establishes the paradigm on Kairos 3.1. Only two of 46 training sources provide kinematic grounding; the
remainder are video-only, and the evaluation datasets contain no overlapping trajectories, indicating that the learned
action structure is not simply inherited from dense action labels. The transfer experiments preserve initial geometry
while varying embodiment, scene, or domain, providing a controlled qualitative test of motion retention. Future
work will test other video DiTs, isolate the contribution of kinematic grounding, and evaluate broader geometric
changes and additional robots. As video world models improve, their internal dynamics may provide an increasingly
scalable foundation for learning actions from video.
