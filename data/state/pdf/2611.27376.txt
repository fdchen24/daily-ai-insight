RL post-training typically produces domain experts that must then be consolidated into one model, and
as new experts keep arriving, this consolidation becomes a continual process. Weight merging and multi-
teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) must re-consolidate the whole model whenever an expert is added,
and can erode capabilities outside the target domains. We propose Composable Soft-Prompt Distillation
(CSPD), which keeps the pre-RL model frozen, distills each RL expert independently into a soft prompt, and
composes experts by concatenating their prompts without joint training. A single soft prompt suffices to
distill an RL expert, recovering at least 90% of the teacher’s gain in 22 of 23 math, code, and agent settings.
Independently trained prompts compose well. In a multi-expert setting, the average rises with every expert
added, and the full composition matches MOPD (67.83 vs. 67.79). The composition generalizes best across six
held-out benchmarks. Input-space adaptation adds little serving overhead, keeping throughput within 1.5%
of the unadapted backbone and reaching 2.40× that of rank-4 LoRA. These results point toward continual
post-training on a fixed backbone. Code is available at https://github.com/UCSB-AI/CSPD.

Composable Soft-Prompt Distillation of RL Experts
1. Introduction
Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training is typically carried out one domain at a time, since each domain
comes with its own data, verifier, and environment. A common recipe is therefore to train domain experts
separately from a shared base model and then consolidate them into a single model (Wang et al., 2026b, Ma
et al., 2026a). Because new domains and stronger experts keep arriving, this consolidation is not a one-time
step but a continual process. Ideally, each new expert would be absorbed like an installed module, such
that (i) the model gains what the expert learned in its domain, (ii) everything the model could already do,
in the other experts’ domains and in general, is left intact, and (iii) the cost of the update depends only
on the new expert, not on how many experts came before.
Existing approaches fall into two groups. The first consolidates all experts into a single set of weights. Joint
multi-task RL must rerun RL on every domain whenever one is added. Sequential RL (Wang et al., 2026a)
trains only on the new domain, but earlier gains are susceptible to irreversible damage from later stages.
Weight merging (Ilharco et al., 2023, Yadav et al., 2023) combines experts by parameter arithmetic, where
their updates can interfere. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) (Wang et al., 2026b, Ma et al.,
2026a) must retrain the student against all teachers. All four also apply one fixed mixture to every request
and overwrite the base weights, which can degrade performance outside the target domains (Wang et al.,
2026c). The second group keeps experts separate and selects them per request. Expert routing depends on a
router that is hard to train and must be updated whenever an expert is added, cannot combine experts within
a request, and must host a full model per expert. Merging LoRA experts per request (Huang et al., 2023,
Prabhakar et al., 2024) avoids these issues but adds adapter computation to every layer at every decoding step.
Both groups keep experts in model parameters. We instead make each expert part of the model’s input,
and propose Composable Soft-Prompt Distillation (CSPD) (Figure 2). During training, CSPD keeps the
pre-RL model frozen and distills each post-RL expert into its own soft prompt (Lester et al., 2021) of 256
vectors, using only that expert’s verifier-filtered responses (Kim and Rush, 2016). Each soft prompt is trained
independently. To prepare the prompts for later combination, half of the training examples place a random
context prefix before the prompt, so that it works regardless of what precedes it. At inference, the prompts
are composed directly by concatenating them in the system message, without any joint training. Adding an
expert therefore requires training only its prompt, removing an expert requires only dropping its prompt, and
the backbone is never modified. Unlike merged or routed LoRA experts, the prompts are plug-in modules,
with no merging coefficient or router to refit when the set of experts changes.
Merging experts in input space proves surprisingly effective. A single soft prompt suffices to distill an RL
expert. Across 23 math, code, and agent settings, it recovers at least 90% of the teacher’s improvement over
the pre-RL model in 22 of them, and matches or exceeds the teacher in 15. Independently trained prompts
exhibit strong composability. In a multi-expert setting (Wang et al., 2026b), we distill soft prompts from
experts in instruction following, science, code, and math, and add them to the frozen student one at a time,
as in continual post-training. The four-domain average rises with every addition, from 50.41 to 67.83, and
the full composition matches MOPD (67.79) without any joint training. The composition generalizes best
beyond the training domains. Among the compared methods, it attains the highest mean score across six
held-out benchmarks (56.6, versus 53.5 for MOPD). A soft prompt learns behavior rather than words. On a
GPT-OSS math expert, the prompt closes 86.8% of the teacher–student KL gap, mainly by making the student
check its intermediate results, while written instructions with the same intent close at most 2.1% (Section 4).

No joint training is involved. In continual post-training, experts arrive over time. When a new expert arrives,

Prefix-robust training for composition.
A prompt is trained alone but follows other prompts at inference.
To prepare it for this, we use prefix-robust training, which places a 128–256-token prefix, sampled from
other questions in the same domain, before the soft prompt in half of the training examples. The prefix is
excluded from the loss and is not used at inference. This makes composition more robust (Appendix C).
3. Experiments
We first evaluate whether a soft prompt can recover a single RL expert (Section 3.2). We then evaluate the
composition of independently distilled prompts in-domain (Section 3.3) and in a continual post-training
setting (Section 3.4), and evaluate out-of-domain generalization (Section 3.5). Finally, we measure the
cost of serving a distinct prompt per request (Section 3.6).
3.1. Evaluation Metrics

prompted student. Let AS, AT, and AS+P denote the student, teacher, and prompted-student scores, each

3.2. Distilling Individual RL Experts
Before composing experts, we first test whether a single soft prompt can recover an individual RL expert,
using public pre-RL/post-RL model pairs in math, code, and agent tasks. For each pair, we distill the
RL teacher into a 256-vector prompt on its frozen pre-RL checkpoint and compare the prompted student
(Student+SP) with both (Table 1). The table also gives model links and references, and evaluation details
are in Appendices B.3 and B.1.
The 23 settings are deliberately diverse. They span math reasoning, code generation on LiveCodeBench, and
multi-step agent tasks on SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench 2.1. The models range from 7B to 35B
parameters and include both dense and mixture-of-experts architectures from many model families, and their
teachers were trained with different RL recipes and data. Across this range, the prompt recovers at least 90%
of the teacher’s gain in 22 settings, and the prompted student matches or exceeds the teacher in 15 (Table 1).
The trend holds for multi-step agent tasks as well, where the prompted student exceeds the teacher in five of
six settings. For example, DeepSWE’s student improves from 24.90% to 45.65% on SWE-bench Verified,
compared with 44.05% for its teacher. The lowest recovery, 88.08%, is for MiMo-7B on code. Its RL training
data are not publicly available, so we distill on AReaL code questions instead. This suggests that CSPD

Experimental setting.
We follow M2RL (Wang et al., 2026b), which first applies supervised fine-tuning to
Qwen3-4B-Base (Yang et al., 2025) and then trains a separate RL expert from this SFT model in each domain.
We directly use their SFT model, M2RL-SFT, as the frozen student and their RL experts in four domains, math,
code, science, and instruction following, as teachers. We do not retrain the SFT model or the RL experts, and
we distill each soft prompt on the training data of the corresponding RL expert. These domains are evaluated
on AIME24 (MAA, 2024), LiveCodeBench v5 (Jain et al., 2025), GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2024), and
IFBench (Pyatkin et al., 2025), respectively. Evaluation uses temperature 0.6, a maximum generation length
of 32k tokens, and sampling counts of K = 64/8/16/8 across the four benchmarks.
Each expert is distilled independently into a 256-vector prompt with prefix-robust training (Section 2.2),
using the Muon optimizer for 200 updates, and we use the final checkpoint. We evaluate the same four
prompts in two configurations. Single-domain SP attaches only the prompt of the domain being evaluated,
and CSPD attaches all four prompts. We report two concatenation orders, MCSI (math, code, science,
instruction following) and its reverse ISCM, and use MCSI by default. The input templates and training
recipe are provided in Appendix A.
Baselines.
We compare against expert weight merging through Task Arithmetic (TA) (Ilharco et al., 2023)
and TIES (Yadav et al., 2023), and against MOPD (Wang et al., 2026b). We also distill each expert into
a LoRA adapter using the same data as the prompts and combine the adapters with TA or TIES. For TA,
we report the best evaluated coefficient, which is 1.0 for the full-model RL experts and 0.5 for the LoRA
adapters. Coefficient sweeps and baseline training settings are in Appendix D.

backbone and all prompts fixed throughout.
Continual addition.

Comparison with progressive training and merging.
A natural alternative is to train each new prompt
in the context of the earlier ones, as in Progressive Prompts (Razdaibiedina et al., 2023), where each new
prompt is trained with all previous prompts frozen in front of it, so it can adapt to the existing composition.
Because progressive training places each new prompt in front of the previous ones, we compare it with
independent prompts in the same prepended order (I, SI, CSI, MCSI), with the same optimizer and number

3.6. Serving Efficiency
A soft prompt adds parameters only at the input. It is processed once during prefill, and every later decoding
step only attends to its cached keys and values, so relative to the backbone the only cost is a slightly longer
context (Appendix F). A LoRA adapter, by contrast, adds computation to every layer at every decoding step.
Because every expert is an input to the same frozen model, requests that use different experts, or different
combinations of them, can also share one batch. We measure the cost of this by assigning a distinct adapter
to every request, the extreme case of per-user personalization, and comparing throughput and first-token
latency against the same backbone served without adapters.
Experimental setting.
We serve GPT-OSS-20B in BF16 on a single NVIDIA B200 with SGLang. We compare
the unadapted backbone, 256-vector soft prompts, and unmerged rank-4 LoRA adapters served with the
CSGMV backend. Each request has 20,480 text input tokens and generates 512 tokens, simulating an agent
setting. SP adds 256 vectors to the input. We use matched text inputs across configurations and register
the synthetic adapters before timing generation.
We vary the concurrency limit C over 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24, 32. After a warm-up burst of C requests, a closed-
loop client issues 8C measured requests, starting a new one whenever a slot becomes available. All three
configurations use the same KV-cache budget at each concurrency level, with 524,288 tokens through
C = 16 and 1,048,576 at C = 24 and 32. We report aggregate output tokens per second from the first
request submission to the last completion, and the 95th percentile of the time from request submission
to receipt of the first token.
Multi-adapter serving.
SP throughput stays within 1.5% of the unadapted backbone across the seven
concurrency levels (Figure 5). Relative to LoRA, the advantage is largest with one request in flight, where SP
delivers 4.12× the output throughput of LoRA. At 32 concurrent requests, SP serves 715.4 output tokens/s,
compared with 721.3 for the backbone and 298.5 for LoRA. At this operating point, SP delivers 2.40× the
throughput of LoRA and reduces P95 time to first token from 39.21 to 17.26 seconds, a 2.27× reduction,
close to the backbone’s 16.89 seconds.
Speculative decoding.
Soft prompts also remain compatible with draft models trained on the backbone.
Adding a 256-vector prompt reduces the average acceptance length from 2.34 to 2.28 for EAGLE-3 (Li
et al., 2025) (−2.6%) and from 2.96 to 2.82 for DFlash (Chen et al., 2026) (−4.7%). Acceptance length
does not directly measure end-to-end latency.

4. What Does a Soft Prompt Learn?
We analyze one expert from Table 1, the 256-vector soft prompt that distills GPT-OSS-Swallow-20B-RL
into GPT-OSS-Swallow-20B-SFT for math. The teacher, the student, and the prompted student answer 100
held-out AIME and MATH-500 questions. Each model then reads the same answers, and at every response
position we compare its next-token distribution with the teacher’s. See Appendix G for details.
The nearest word of a soft prompt vector does not tell what the vector learned. Across six models,
84–100% of the vectors keep their initialization token as the nearest word, even though in five of them the
vectors move far from that token (median cosine similarity 0.25–0.34).
The soft prompt moves the student most of the way to the teacher, mainly by making it check its
intermediate results. It closes 86.8% of the teacher–student KL gap. The tokens it raises most are ?, But,
and Check, and those it lowers most are paragraph breaks, So, and Thus (Figure 6b). Instead of stating a
result and moving on, the prompted student first questions or checks it, as the teacher does.
Written instructions with the same intent do not reproduce the effect. Instructions asking the model to
check and verify its work close at most 2.1% of the gap, and raising the reasoning effort closes none (Figure 6a).
The soft prompt acts mainly through response tokens attending to it directly, rather than by changing
how the model reads the question. When only response tokens may attend to the soft prompt, 80% of its
improvement remains, but only 33% remains when only input tokens may attend to it (Figure 6c).
Taken together, a soft prompt does not learn a message that can be put into words. It learns the teacher’s

5. Discussion
Why compose in input space?
Weight-space consolidation writes every expert into the same parameters,
so adding an expert means re-consolidating the model, and the experts can interfere with one another and
with the backbone’s general capabilities. CSPD separates acquiring a capability from combining capabilities.
Each prompt is distilled using only its own teacher and data, prompts meet only at inference, and the
backbone never changes. This separation lets experts be trained in parallel by different parties, added or
replaced without revisiting earlier domains, and removed exactly by dropping their prompt. Our results
show that this separation does not cost in-domain performance relative to MOPD, and that it better preserves
out-of-domain behavior. The progressive-training comparison further suggests that composition does not
require each module to be trained with knowledge of the others.
Plug-in experts versus multi-LoRA.
Combining LoRA experts from different domains usually takes one of
two forms. Merging adds their weight updates with tuned coefficients (Ilharco et al., 2023, Yadav et al., 2023,
Huang et al., 2023, Prabhakar et al., 2024), so all experts write into the same weights and can interfere.
With the same distillation data, merged LoRA adapters reach 65.50 with TA and 66.19 with TIES, below the
67.83 of composed soft prompts (Table 2). Mixture-style methods instead learn a router or gate over the
adapters (Buehler and Buehler, 2024, Zadouri et al., 2024), which must be retrained whenever an expert is
added. A soft prompt is a plug-in module. Adding an expert trains only its prompt, removing one drops its
prompt exactly, and several experts act together within a request by concatenation, with no coefficient or
router to fit. Different requests in one batch can also use different sets of prompts on the same unchanged
backbone, which gives 2.40× the output throughput of rank-4 LoRA with 32 distinct adapters (Section 3.6).

Limitations.
Our composition study uses one backbone and four domains. Whether composition continues
to improve as the number of experts grows, and how it depends on the relations between domains, remain
open. Experts do interact, both positively and negatively (Section 3.4). Several reported numbers, including
single-expert checkpoints and TA coefficients, are selected on the evaluation benchmarks, and small teacher
gains make some recovery ratios noisy, so we report absolute scores throughout (Appendix B.3). Each prompt
adds 256 positions to the context, so attention and KV-cache costs grow with the number of composed
experts. Finally, prompts are tied to their backbone and require RL teachers for supervision, so moving
to a new backbone requires re-distillation.
Toward continual post-training.
CSPD suggests a different shape for post-training, with a frozen backbone
and a growing library of compact expert modules, each distilled when a new capability becomes available
and composed as needed. Four directions follow. The first is scale, with more backbones, larger prompt
libraries, and more arrival orders. The second is prompt selection, learning which prompts each request
needs instead of choosing them by hand. The third is mechanism, understanding why prompts transfer
across domains and how several prompts share attention. The fourth is cost, with shorter prompts for large
libraries and a full training-cost comparison with MOPD and LoRA.
6. Related Work
Capacity requirements of post-training.
LoRA can match full fine-tuning in selected post-training
regimes (Schulman and Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). Single-layer training, sparse subnetworks, and
low-dimensional adapters can also recover substantial RL gains (Zhang et al., 2026, Mukherjee et al., 2025,
Morris et al., 2026), while SGD-based RL exhibits sparse updates (Mukherjee et al., 2026). These findings
motivate studying input-space representations of expert capabilities.
Distilling post-training capabilities. Sequence-level distillation transfers teacher-generated responses,
including reasoning capabilities (Kim and Rush, 2016, DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), whereas on-policy methods
supervise student-generated trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024, Gu et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2025, Lu and
Thinking Machines Lab, 2025). Multi-teacher on-policy distillation consolidates several experts into one
student (Wang et al., 2026b, Ma et al., 2026a). Approaching teacher performance in-domain can nevertheless
accompany out-of-domain losses (Wang et al., 2026c), motivating evaluation beyond the distilled capabilities.
Representations of adaptation. Adaptation can reside in weights, continuous inputs, layerwise prefixes, or
hidden-state interventions (Hu et al., 2021, Lester et al., 2021, Li and Liang, 2021, Wu et al., 2024). PromptKD
and soft-prompt teachers use learned prompts to guide distillation into student weights (Kim et al., 2024, Ma
et al., 2026b). DDPT instead places demonstration-induced reasoning in dynamically selected prompts (Teng
et al., 2026). Soft prompts can also receive distilled safety behavior (Alfarra et al., 2026). Cartridges train
key–value cache prefixes to compress long contexts through self-study (Eyuboglu et al., 2025). Concurrent
with our work, Hypertune trains key–value cache prefixes directly with RL or SFT, adding co-residence
training so that several prefixes can be loaded together (Hyperstition, 2026). CSPD instead learns prompts
in the input embedding space by distilling existing RL experts, trains each prompt in isolation, and composes
them by concatenation. An input-space prompt can also be prefilled once into a key–value prefix for serving.
Composition, continual learning, and efficient serving. Model soups, Task Arithmetic, and interference-
aware merging combine weight updates (Wortsman et al., 2022, Ilharco et al., 2023, Yadav et al., 2023, Yu
13
Composable Soft-Prompt Distillation of RL Experts
et al., 2024), with mixed results for RL experts (Wu et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026b). Adapter composition
combines updates or learns gates (Pfeiffer et al., 2021, Huang et al., 2023, Prabhakar et al., 2024, Buehler
and Buehler, 2024, Zadouri et al., 2024), while BAR composes experts through a routed mixture-of-experts
architecture (Morrison et al., 2026). In input space, APT learns composable prompts and ATTEMPT mixes
source prompts (Bowman et al., 2023, Asai et al., 2022). Progressive Prompts freezes earlier prompts during
continual learning (Razdaibiedina et al., 2023), and Skill Neologisms composes independently learned skill
embeddings (Berthon et al., 2026). Deployment work optimizes concurrent adapters (Chen et al., 2023,
Sheng et al., 2024), prefill-only adaptation (Lanpouthakoun et al., 2026), and speculative decoding (Li
et al., 2025, Chen et al., 2026). CSPD connects these research directions by independently distilling RL
experts into prompts on a shared frozen backbone, then evaluating their concatenation, out-of-domain
behavior, and serving efficiency.
7. Conclusion
We presented CSPD, which distills each post-RL expert into an independently trained soft prompt on a
frozen pre-RL backbone and composes experts by concatenating their prompts. A single prompt recovers
at least 90% of the teacher’s gain in 22 of 23 math, code, and agent settings. Four prompts from different
domains, trained without access to each other, compose to match MOPD in-domain, and the average rises
with every expert added one at a time, as in continual post-training. The composition also attains the highest
mean score on held-out benchmarks among the compared methods. Because the experts live in the input,
serving them costs little more than serving the backbone alone. These results suggest input-space modules
as a practical foundation for continual post-training.
Impact Statement
This work studies modular transfer of post-trained behavior. Compact, attachable modules may make
specialized behaviors easier to distribute and serve, including behaviors that require safety evaluation.
Freezing the backbone does not guarantee that an attached prompt, or a combination of prompts, preserves
safety properties, and our XSTest evaluation covers only refusal behavior on the tested requests. Release
decisions should consider model and dataset terms, generated training material, and the behavior of the
resulting modules alone and in combination.
