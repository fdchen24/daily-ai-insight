Ulm University

Abstract
It has been demonstrated that, in certain settings, large lan-
guage models can perform weight-space programming to
directly modify the weights of neural networks. However, it
is unknown how dedicated training of these networks can
contribute to the successes of weight-space programming.
We present a benchmark of LLM-driven weight-space pro-
gramming on vision models, in which five LLMs edit convo-
lutional networks and vision transformers on relabeling and
error-correction tasks on Waterbirds, CIFAR-100, PACS and
Entity-30. We report more than 2,600 such edit processes,
whereby each edit is scored on held-out images for success,
side effects and accuracy loss. We compare standard train-
ing with label smoothing, gradient-based editable training,
and our Edit-Ready Training (ERT), a novel method that
rehearses the direct, closed-form weight edits LLMs perform.
We show that preparing networks for editing raises the suc-
cess of LLM edits substantially where standard networks are
hard to edit. ERT further achieves the highest mean score
for GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 across all evaluated
baselines. We will release our LLM testing framework along-
side all tasks, editing trajectories and scores. Project page:
https://viscom.uni-ulm.de/wsp
1. Introduction
Deployed image classifiers often need small, specific
changes: a fine-grained concept has to move to another class,
a recurring confusion between two classes has to be repaired,
or a rare subgroup is misclassified. Collecting data and fine-
tuning is costly and can change behavior far beyond the
requested fix. Model editing methods instead compute a tar-
geted weight change for one request [22, 24, 31]. Recently,
general-purpose large language models (LLMs) have begun
to edit networks directly, a practice called weight-space pro-
gramming [39]. Here, an LLM agent inspects activations
and weights, finds the units that respond to a concept, writes
a targeted change and checks its effect with forward passes,
all without gradient descent.
The networks such agents edit are, however, trained for
accuracy alone. Nothing in their training asks that a concept
be represented in a form that a short, targeted edit can isolate.
It is therefore unknown how well LLM agents edit standard
vision models, and whether training can make networks
easier for them to edit.
We study both questions for image classification (see
Fig. 1). Blinded LLM agents receive an edit request with a
few support images, edit any weight of the network through a
tool interface, without gradients, under a fixed budget of for-
ward passes, weight reads and weight edits, and are scored
on held-out test images for success and side effects. Dur-
ing these edit processes, they relabel a fine-grained concept,
fix a confusion between two classes, or fix a misclassified
subgroup, on Waterbirds [30], CIFAR-100 [19], PACS [20]
and Entity-30 [32], with ResNet-18, ResNet-50 [12] and
ViT-B/16 [6] classifiers. Five LLMs act as editors, led by
GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5, to edit networks that are
trained in four ways: standard empirical risk minimization
(ERM), label smoothing (LS), gradient-based editable train-
ing (ENN) [36], and Edit-Ready Training (ERT), which we
introduce. Our pre-study revealed that agents mostly change
the classifier head along the contrast between support and
other images, and ERT rehearses such edits during training,
so that the features become easy to edit this way.
On four of the nine tested settings formed by dataset, task
and backbone, standard networks are already easy to edit,
and dedicated training adds little on average. Where they are
hard to edit, ERT helps substantially for both main editors.
On the Entity-30 confusion fix it raises the net score from
0.46 to 0.79 for Astra and from 0.44 to 0.74 for Opus, and
on Waterbirds with ViT-B/16 from 0.50 to 0.74 and from
0.33 to 0.76. While none of the compared training methods
is best across the board, ERT has the highest mean net score
for both leading editors. Thus, our main contributions are as
follows:
• We present a systematic study of how well LLM agents
edit trained vision classifiers, and how the classifier train-
ing affects edit quality.
• We propose Edit-Ready Training (ERT), which delivers
the highest mean net edit score for GPT-6 Astra and Opus
5.5 among all tested baselines.

2. Related Work
Weight-space analysis. Weight-space analysis treats the
weights of trained networks as data. Test accuracy or train-
ing hyperparameters can be predicted from the weights
alone [7, 40], representations learned on model zoos cap-
ture model characteristics without labels [33, 34], architec-
tures for learning on weights respect the permutation sym-
metries of neurons [26, 42], and hypernetworks generate
weights [11]. Interpretability research can assist the anal-
ysis of networks by assigning visual concepts to units [2],
combining weights between units into circuits [28], and by
describing units in natural language [13, 27].
Language models have recently taken over parts of this
analysis. Agents with interpretability tools run experiments
on vision models and explain their units [35], and language
models have been trained to read weights [16]. Frontier
LLMs identify small networks from their raw weights with-
out such training [39], although their readings of small image
networks break under label permutation [1].
Neural network editing. Network editing changes the be-
havior of a trained network on a specific input or concept
while leaving the rest intact. Most methods compute the
weight change with an algorithm: fine-tuning, learned hyper-
networks that turn a gradient into an edit [4, 24], or closed-
form low-rank updates at located layers [22, 23]. In vision,
prediction rules are rewritten at a chosen layer of a classi-
fier [31] or a generative model [3], and concepts are erased
from diffusion models [10]. Task arithmetic adds and sub-
tracts weight differences [15], linear projections remove a
concept direction from the features [29], and retraining only
the last layer corrects reliance on spurious features [17],
which makes the classifier a natural place for small edits.
Weight-space programming [39] replaces the editing al-
gorithm with an LLM that decides itself what to inspect and
what to write. This offloads editing to LLM agents that carry
out technical work autonomously [14]. Existing work [39]
judges an edit only by its effect on the target behavior. Our
benchmark also scores side effects and accuracy cost on held-
out images, blinds the agent to the origin of the network, and
compares several agents under one protocol.
Few works target editability during training. Editable
Neural Networks (ENN) [36] meta-train a network, in the
spirit of MAML [8], so that a few gradient steps on new
examples correct its predictions without harming others. We
keep this structure but rehearse the edits that LLM agents
perform. Apart from dedicated techniques, ordinary training
choices also affect editability [21]. Label smoothing [25, 38]
produces less extreme logits, and sparse final layers make a
classifier easier to inspect and debug [41]. To our knowledge,
none of these methods has been evaluated with LLM editors.
3. Method
Our approach has two parts, as shown in Fig. 1. First, Edit-
Ready Training (ERT) prepares a network for later edits,
by simulating head weight edits, computing the errors of
the edited network, and backpropagating them through the
edit into all weights. Second, Iterative Weight-Space Pro-
gramming (WSP) edits the trained network afterwards in
an iterative process. An LLM receives an edit request and
a few images, and uses our WeightLab sandbox server to
2
inspect the network, modify its native weights and check
the result. We describe WSP first, because the rules of its
editing protocol determine which edits ERT performs during
training.
3.1. Iterative Weight-Space Programming
To systematically investigate weight-space programming
of vision models, an LLM edits a trained network through
WeightLab, our sandbox environment that exposes the net-
work and enforces a fixed editing protocol (Fig. 1, center).
The protocol serves five purposes. It keeps the edits direct,
such that the agent has no gradients, cannot train the network
and cannot add layers or adapters, so it cannot fall back on
fine-tuning, which would make it a gradient-based editor. It
further makes editors and training methods comparable, as
every editor uses the same tools, budget and system prompt
(Sec. A). Blinding keeps an editor from recognizing how a
network was trained. We record every edit by logging all
calls and changes, which makes the edits analyzable (see
Sec. 5.3). Finally, a budget limits trial and error on the few
support images, which would otherwise lead to overfitting.
For our setup, we consider a classifier with a back-
bone that computes features f(x) ∈Rd and a linear head
with weights W and bias b, so that the logits are z(x) =
Wf(x) + b. An edit request asks to move a concept s, cur-
rently assigned to class y, to a target class t, while all other
predictions stay as they are. More generally, an edit request
consists of a few support images labeled with the class they
should receive and a set of reference images labeled with
the network’s current predictions, which should not change.
Besides relabeling a concept, we consider two kinds of re-
quests that correct existing errors: fixing a confusion, where
images of one class are predicted as another class, and fixing
a subgroup, where images of one class from a particular
domain, such as sketches, are misclassified (Fig. 2).
Each edit process addresses a single edit request, such as
“move this concept to class t”, and starts with a fresh agent.
Each request provides a few support images labeled with
the desired class (five for relabeling, eight for the fix tasks)
and 64 reference images, labeled with the network’s original
predictions to indicate what should remain unchanged. The
agent never sees the images themselves, and it observes only
how the network responds to them.
Within each edit process, the agent can iteratively inspect,
modify, and evaluate the network. It inspects predictions,
layer activations, feature contributions to logits, and parame-
ter values. It then modifies weights by setting, adding to, or
scaling individual entries, rows, channels, or whole tensors,
anywhere in the classification head or the backbone. The
protocol does not restrict which parameters are changed or
how, the only limits are the number of calls and the size of a
single edit call. During the process, the agent can probe the
modified network on the provided images, revise its changes,
undo the last edit, or reset the weights.
The agent submits these tool calls through model re-
sponses, which can contain several of these calls. Each
edit process is limited to 20 forward probes, 30 weight reads,
and 8 edit calls. A single edit call may change up to 65,536
parameters, and a single read returns at most 512 values.
This write parameter budget is enough to rewrite the entire
head of every network in one call (the largest, ResNet-50 on
Entity-30, has 61,470 parameters) and any row, channel, bias
or normalization layer of the backbone. At the same time,
the read parameter budget, stops the agent from exfiltrating
and retraining the network. Undo and reset do not count
towards the edit budget, and do not restore it either. During
editing, the agent further sees only an anonymous request
ID, never receives images from the test set, and has no in-
formation about how the network was trained. Its system
prompt encourages targeted edits that generalize beyond the
provided examples (full text in Sec. A.3). An edit process
ends when the agent submits its modified weights. If the
agent stops without submitting or reaches the limit of 150
model responses, the original weights are evaluated.
3.2. Edit-Ready Training
Standard training minimizes the cross-entropy LCE on the
training labels and does not enforce editability. Edit-Ready
Training (ERT) in contrast rehearses during training the edits
that LLM agents make. To enable an agent to edit any param-
eter of the head or the backbone, our editing protocol does
not constrain these edits during weight-space programming.
However, during training the rehearsed edits are meant to
simulate real Opus edits. Therefore, during a small pre-
study, we have tasked Opus to edit ERM-trained networks.
We found that Opus mainly (>90%) changes head weights,
an observation which we could also confirm later during our
full edit analysis (see Sec. 5.3). Furthermore, the performed
edits were mostly in a rank-one manner, i.e., confined to the
rows of the source and the target class, and concentrated on
the few features that differ most between the support and
the reference images. To simulate such edits during training,
each training step draws a few edit episodes that simulate an
edit request to move a concept s of class y to a target class t.
During these episodes, four support images of s define the
edit, four unseen query images of s check that it generalizes,
and reference images of other concepts from the batch check
that their predictions stay unchanged. Edit episodes draw
s only from training concepts, concepts that editors later
changed during testing are never used. During the training
process, all edit episodes pass through the three ERT steps
(see Fig. 1 (a)), which we describe below.

Edit backpropagation.
The simulated edits are a fixed,
closed-form function of the current network, so both post-
edit losses are differentiable with respect to all weights, in-
cluding the backbone. The selection of the top-8 units is held
fixed in the backward pass, while β and the feature direction
pass gradients. The update is applied to the original weights
and the temporary W ′ is discarded, so no edit is ever kept.
Training therefore does not make one particular edit work.
Instead, it is meant to shape the features so that edits also
work for concepts and images not seen during training, with
the goal that each concept is kept in a few distinctive units or
a clean direction. The extra cost per training step are the for-
ward and backward passes over the episode images, which
are appended to the training batch, and one closed-form edit
per episode.
4. Experimental Setup
4.1. Network Training
Vision model training.
All networks to be edited are
initialized with ImageNet weights and are classification-
fine-tuned on the dataset’s classes: ResNet-18 [12] and
ViT-B/16 [6] on Waterbirds (100 epochs), CIFAR-100 (30
epochs) and PACS (50 epochs), and ResNet-50 on Entity-30
(20 epochs).
Since training compute and frontier model requests are
limited, we train a single network per training method and
setting, with the exception of multiple trained networks for
the seed ablation in Sec. 5.2. The intervals we report there-
fore reflect variation across edit processes, conditional on the
trained networks. The seed ablation in Sec. 5.2 supports this
design: the variation between networks trained with different
seeds is negligible compared to the variation between edit
processes on the same network.
Baselines.
We compare our ERT against three baselines.
Standard empirical risk minimization (ERM) minimizes
cross-entropy only. Label smoothing (LS) replaces the one-
hot target with 0.8 on the true class and 0.2 spread over all
classes [38], to obtain less peaked logits [25] such that a
small change can move a decision. ENN denotes Editable
Neural Networks [36]. ENN is analogous to ERT, but simu-
lates the edit with one or five gradient steps on the support

the CIFAR-100 tuned hyperparameters, across all methods.
Search spaces and selected values can be found in Sec. B.1.
4.2. Weight-Space Programming
Editing tasks.
We evaluate on three kinds of edit requests
(see Fig. 2). Relabel a concept: a fine-grained concept
moves to another class. The concept is a bird species on Wa-
terbirds [30] (two classes) or a fine class on CIFAR-100 [19]
(20 superclasses). Fix a confusion (CIFAR-100, and Entity-
30 [32], a 30-class subset of ImageNet [5]): superclass A is
wrongly predicted as B, which should be corrected. Fix a
subgroup (PACS [20]): misclassified sketches of one class
should be corrected.
LLM editors and protocol.
The two main editors are GPT-
6 Astra (high reasoning effort) and Claude Opus 5.5 (default
effort), each restricted to the WeightLab tools. Both cover all
nine settings with all training methods. Three open-weight
editors test how far the results carry over to weaker models:
GLM-5.3-Flash, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and Gemma-4-26B
(detailed numbers in Sec. E).
Each editor handles eight edit requests per setting and
training method, each with its own images, on one trained
network, or split over two networks on PACS and three
on the Entity-30 confusion fix (three requests each), which
the seed ablation in Sec. 5.2 uses. We pair results of the
same editor for different training methods and compare two
training methods with an exact two-sided sign-flip test on
the paired differences. The main grid contains 292 scored
edit processes of Astra, 308 of Opus and 757 of the three
open-weight editors. Including ablations and pilots, the five
editors made more than 2,600 scored edit processes with
more than 75,000 tool calls, and the median edit process
takes about three minutes for Astra and Opus (Sec. H).
Metrics.
The reported scores are computed on held-out
test images that no editor sees. For relabeling, success is the
fraction of test images of the concept predicted as t, and the
side effect is the fraction of test images of all other concepts
whose prediction flips. For the two fix tasks, success is the
fraction of the targeted test errors that the edit corrects, and
the side effect is the fraction of originally correct test images
outside the target group that become wrong. The net score
as our primary measure reports success minus side effect, as
also reported by Sinitsin et al. [36].

5.2. Ablations
We have run four main ablations to investigate ERT’s depen-
dence on type, location and number of edits, as well as seed
used during training.
Edit rule ablation.
To obtain insights on the effect of edit
rules used during training, we retrained ERT on Waterbirds
with ResNet-18 with seven edit variants: more units, larger
steps, a bias shift or sparse edits of the size of Opus’s own
(see Sec. 5.3), ENN’s gradient step as additional rule, and
stronger rehearsal. No variant differs reliably from the ref-
erence for Opus (differences from −0.06 to +0.06, none
significant), and stronger rehearsal, trained for eight of the
nine settings (all but the Entity-30 confusion fix), is ahead in
three and behind in five. The benefit of ERT thus does not
seem to depend on matching the editor’s edit closely (details
in Sec. F.1).
Backbone edit ablation.
All of the tested rules edit only
the final layer, whereas Opus sometimes also edits the back-
bone (see Fig. 3). To investigate if ERT should facilitate also
backbone edits, we retrained a modified ERT on Waterbirds
with ResNet-18, where most backbone edits occur. During
this training we introduced a third rule: lower the input of
the last ReLU on the selected channels, the backbone edit
Opus makes most often on ERM trained networks. While the
network keeps its accuracy (92.6% against 92.9%) under this
edit, Opus does not profit: its net score is 0.61 against 0.73
for default ERT on the same eight species (difference −0.12,
ahead on 2 of 8, p = 0.13). Although, in this scenario Opus
edits the backbone in 3 of 8 edit processes against 1 of 8,
this indicates no added benefits.
Training seed ablation.
As most cells of Tab. 1 rest on
a single trained network per method, it is crucial that the
training methods have a low training seed variance. To check
this, we use the 26 cells of Tab. 1 that have several trained
networks per method (PACS, the Entity-30 confusion fix,
and ENN and ERT on Waterbirds ResNet-18 with Opus), in
which every edit request is run on each network. The mean
net score of a network varies between networks by a standard
deviation of 0.00 (95% CI upper bound 0.03), whereas edit
processes on the same network vary by 0.17.
Edit budget ablation.
Our weight-space programming
protocol allows a maximum of 8 edit calls (see Sec. 3.1),
and the editor knows this limit from the start. To investigate
the impact of the maximum number of allowed edits, we
let Opus repeat the Waterbirds relabeling requests on ERM
and ERT trained ResNet-18 with budgets of 1, 4, 8 and 12
edits (Fig. 4). Against 8 calls in the same pilot, neither 4 nor
12 calls change the net score detectably (pooled over both
networks −0.01, p = 0.86, and +0.03, p = 0.43; 16 pairs
each). Only a single call is lower (−0.10, p = 0.08). Even
with 12 calls allowed, Opus uses a median of 5 to 6. ERT
is ahead of ERM at every budget, by 0.18 with one call and
0.24 to 0.29 with 4 to 12, and this gap does not depend on
the budget (all interactions p ≥0.36).

Edit shapes.
In every task, dataset and architecture, Opus’s
change to the final layer is rank one (median share of its en-
ergy in the first singular value above 99% in every setting),
confined to the rows of the source and the target class, and
sparse (median 11 to 64 feature columns, 2–8% of the fea-
tures). The columns are chosen by contrast. Of the eight
features that differ most between the concept and the refer-
ence images, the edit contains 3.2 to 4.5, against 0.1 to 1.4
by chance. The training method does not seem to change
this shape.
Among closed-form edits computed from the same sup-
port images, the closest to Opus’s edits, on the entries it
changed, is a rank-one re-route along the contrast, ERT’s
direction rule without removal (mean cosine 0.72 to 0.93 on
eight of the nine settings), followed by a gradient step, the
edit ENN rehearses (0.51 to 0.87); on the Entity-30 confu-
sion fix, neither is close (0.38 and 0.40). An LLM that edits
by hand thus arrives at a sparse version of the edits that both
edit-aware methods rehearse, and how gradient-like an edit
is does not seem to predict which method wins.
Method differences.
What differs between training meth-
ods is the size of the edit. On Waterbirds with ResNet-18,
Opus’s change to the classifier weights is about five times
larger on ERM-trained networks than on prepared ones (me-
dian ℓ2 norm 7.5, against 1.4 for ERT and 1.6 for ENN), with
a similar number of changed units. Preparation thus does not
change what kind of edit Opus makes, as it makes a small, or-
dinary edit sufficient where an ERM-trained network needs
a large one or an edit inside the backbone.
6. Conclusion and Future Work
We presented the first systematic study of weight-space pro-
gramming for vision models, with a benchmark of LLM
agents that edit trained classifiers and with Edit-Ready Train-
ing (ERT), which rehearses the head edits these agents write.
Over more than 2,600 scored edit processes, ERT has the
highest mean net score for both GPT-6 Astra and Claude
Opus 5.5. Further, standard networks already support tar-
geted edits in easy settings, and preparation pays off where
they resist.
Our study also leaves several directions open. It covers
image classification with three backbones and four datasets.
Other vision tasks, larger backbones and generative mod-
els remain to be tested. With 8 to 16 paired edit processes
per cell, only large differences between methods are de-
tectable. Hyperparameters were tuned and baselines selected
with scripted editors on ResNet-18 rather than with agents.
Finally, with every training method, edits still lower test ac-
curacy. Preparation that also preserves accuracy, rehearsal of

edits inside the backbone or tailored to a specific editor, and
sequences of edits to one network are natural next steps. We
release all tasks, trajectories and scores so that new methods
and editors can be compared directly.
