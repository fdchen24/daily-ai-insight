Abstract
Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) scale model capacity by in-
creasing both parameter count and computation. Mixture-
of-Experts (MoE) variants expand parameter capacity while
keeping per-token computation roughly fixed, but still require
substantial parameter storage. In this paper, we propose
Dynamic Looped MoE DiT (DyLoMo-DiT), which reuses
parameters rather than adding them: a weight-shared loop
repeatedly applies the middle layers, while sparse experts
allocate parameter capacity across tokens. This design deep-
ens the network without adding parameters, freeing the pa-
rameter budget for other uses, such as more experts or more
distinct layers. It also allocates compute dynamically, vary-
ing the number of passes across noise levels. Experiments
show that DyLoMo-DiT makes more effective use of parame-
ter and compute budgets, with further gains from assigning
more passes to low-noise steps. On ImageNet 256 × 256,
DyLoMo-DiT reduces FID by 11–24% relative to DiT and
36–56% relative to DiT-MoE at matched parameter and
compute budgets. For text-to-image generation, a scaled
DyLoMo-DiT achieves a higher GenEval score than FLUX.1
(0.714 vs. 0.673), with less than one tenth of its active pa-
rameters (1.05B vs. 12B).
1. Introduction
As diffusion models grow in size, scaling them to improve
generation quality also increases computational and memory
costs [6, 21, 68]. More efficient scaling therefore requires
making better use of model parameters and computation,
rather than simply increasing both.

dently parameterized layers increases both depth and pa-
rameter count. Although timestep conditioning changes the
network’s activations, it does not change the layers or com-
putation used at each step. MoE-DiTs such as DiT-MoE [23]
use sparse experts [61] to expand parameter capacity while
keeping per-token computation roughly fixed, but still re-
quire substantial parameter storage. Both designs maintain
uniform depth across noise levels, even though high-noise
steps primarily establish global structure and low-noise steps
refine detail [5].
Depth offers another way to use model parameters more
efficiently. Recurrently applying a group of weight-shared
layers increases effective depth while storing only one set
of parameters for the repeated layers. This reduces the pa-
rameter cost of added depth and frees budget for experts.
Recurrent depth has been explored in language models and,
more recently, in diffusion Transformers [12, 19, 28, 73].
However, recurrence alone does not determine where the
additional computation is most useful: a fixed pass count ap-
plies the same extra computation at every noise level, while
dense feed-forward networks lack MoE’s token-dependent
parameter capacity. In our matched-budget ablation, a uni-
form two-pass loop does not improve FID at the DiT-MoE-B
budget (Sec. 5.4). These observations motivate combining
parameter-efficient recurrent depth with sparse experts and
adapting computation across the denoising trajectory.
In this paper, we propose Dynamic Looped MoE DiT
(DyLoMo-DiT), an architecture that jointly allocates param-
eter capacity, computation, and depth under a target param-
eter and compute budget. Its feed-forward networks use
multi-head sparse experts to route each token to a small
subset of experts, expanding parameter capacity across to-
kens while keeping per-token computation nearly fixed. A
group of middle Transformer layers shares weights across
recurrent passes, adding effective depth without storing a
separate copy of the layer parameters. The resulting parame-
ter savings can be reinvested in more experts. DyLoMo-DiT
also uses a dynamic loop schedule that varies the number
of passes with the noise level. Assigning more passes to
low-noise inputs, rather than applying the same number of
passes throughout denoising, brings further gains. This de-
sign differs from DiT, which uses a fixed dense layer stack,
and MoE-DiT, which expands token-dependent parameter
capacity but retains uniform depth across noise levels. The
expert configuration, looped segment, routing heads, and
pass schedule are jointly selected for the target budget.
We evaluate DyLoMo-DiT on ImageNet 256 × 256 and
in text-to-image generation. At matched parameter and
forward-compute budgets, DyLoMo-DiT reduces FID by
11%, 14%, and 24% relative to DiT in the B, L, and XL
tiers, respectively, and by 36%, 56%, and 56% relative to
DiT-MoE in the S, B, and L tiers under the same training
protocol. The advantage persists against scaled-up baselines:
a DiT with three times the parameters and compute still has
13% higher FID, while the original DiT-MoE-XL configu-
ration, with six times the parameters, has 63% higher FID.
Further analyses show how the joint design contributes to
these gains: at the DiT-MoE-B budget, progressively adding
the design choices lowers FID from 16.06 to 5.28 (Sec. 5.4).
Extra passes improve results primarily at low noise, while
routers without explicit timestep inputs learn to specialize
by noise level. Finally, a scaled text-to-image variant scores
higher than FLUX.1 on GenEval at 10242 (0.714, compared
with 0.673 for FLUX.1-schnell and 0.671 for FLUX.1-dev)
with less than one tenth as many active parameters (1.05B
vs. 12B).

• Parameter-efficient recurrent depth for diffusion

2. Related Work
Parameters without compute: sparse experts in Trans-
formers. DiT-MoE [23] introduces per-token sparse ex-
perts into DiT. Subsequent work improves expert rout-
ing or extends sparse experts to other backbones and
tasks [1, 36, 62, 64, 74, 79]. DiffMoE and ProMoE re-
port that token-choice sparse experts often underperform
dense models when active parameter counts are matched.
Another line of work assigns experts explicitly to different
noise stages [5, 11, 22, 24, 49, 50, 69]. Our method does not
use such an assignment, but Sec. 5.7 shows that its routing
learns a similar split. Per-head routing has been explored
in Multi-Head Mixture-of-Experts [75], Multi-Head Latent-
MoE [14], and MAGI-2 [59]. None of these methods shares
weights across depth, so depth remains tied to parameter
count.
Depth without parameters: weight-shared Transform-
ers. The Universal Transformer [17] applies the same trans-
formation recurrently across depth. SMELT [72] compares
looped and non-looped MoE language models at matched
compute, parameter count, and KV cache, and recom-
mends looping through roughly half of the layers—located
in the middle of the network—for two passes, with resid-
ual updates scaled by 1/r. For visual generation, Fixed
Point Diffusion Models [4] incorporate fixed-point solver
layers into the denoiser.
ELT, Looped DiT, LiFT, and
LoopREG [12, 19, 28, 73], three of which are concurrent
with this work, apply loops to some or all layers of a DiT.
However, they retain dense feed-forward networks, leaving
parameter count tied to computation.
Removing both couplings. Several language-model stud-
ies combine sparse experts with recurrence [8, 13, 25, 65, 72].
Lee et al. [38] find that looped MoE models benefit more
from scaling than the standard baseline, whereas looped
dense models do not. Subsequent work explores using more
passes, the roles of the two visits, scaling laws with respect
to pass count, experts shared across layers, and per-token

3. Method
3.1. Three Resources and the Design Space
DiT ties parameter count, forward compute, and depth (the
number of layers each token traverses) to its width and layer
count, using the same configuration for every token and
noise level. DyLoMo-DiT makes these choices separately
configurable (Fig. 3): width d; L physical layers, including
a middle segment of m weight-shared layers repeated ac-
cording to the noise-dependent schedule r(t); and H routing
heads per feed-forward network, each with E experts and
k selected per token. Weight sharing adds effective depth
without duplicating parameters, freeing budget for experts.
DiT, MoE-DiT, and looped DiT correspond to one expert and
one pass, one pass, and one expert, respectively (Table 1).
Otherwise, DyLoMo-DiT follows DiT [52]: patchified
VAE latents are conditioned through adaLN-Zero using
timestep and class embeddings, and the output layer pre-
dicts noise and variance. Training uses the DiT objective
without auxiliary losses, loop-specific objectives, or pass
embeddings. Further implementation details are in App. H.

perts

is a multi-head sparse mixture of experts: a d×d linear layer
transforms the sublayer input x and splits it into H heads
of d/H dimensions, each with its own router and E experts.
Each router gives its experts sigmoid scores, selects the top k
after adding a load-balancing bias, and weights them by the
scores L1-normalized over the k and multiplied by a fixed
constant α (App. H). The head outputs are concatenated,
passed through a linear layer, and added to the output of a

3.3. Depth from a Weight-Shared Loop
The L physical layers form a front, a middle and a back
segment, the middle one of m consecutive layers: a forward
pass executes the front segment, the middle segment r times,
and the back segment, with the r passes sharing one set of pa-
rameters (attention, experts, routers and adaLN modulation),
so depth grows with r while the parameter count does not.
Each sublayer of the middle segment writes to the residual

Routing is recomputed in every pass, so a token may select
different experts in different passes. The recipe follows
SMELT [72], whose ablation favors looping about half of
the layers, in the middle, over the full stack, and the factor
1/r follows Loop-µP [71], keeping the updates of the shared
weights from accumulating across visits; under the ImageNet
protocol the middle segment is also the best among front,
back, middle and all layers (App. G).
3.4. Depth by Noise Level: Allocating the Passes
The number of passes is a function of the noise level: the
recommended schedule runs r = 3 for t < 500 and r = 1
for t ≥500, two passes on average over uniformly spaced
sampling steps, so the mean compute equals that of two
passes at every timestep while a token passes through 30
layers at low noise and 16 at high noise (XL tier). The
schedule is part of the model: the timestep of each training
sample decides its number of passes, sampling uses the
same schedule, and a model trained under one schedule must
be run under that schedule; when the number of passes is
instead drawn from {1, 2, 3} at every training step, one set
of weights can be run under several schedules, so depth can
be chosen at inference. Section 5.6 compares schedules;
the return on extra passes (Sec. 5.5) is also observed on an
in-house video generation model (App. G).
3.5. Configuring a Model for a Budget
Because the three resources share one budget, a member is
configured as a whole: the width and the number of layer
visits set the forward compute, matched within 1%; the
loop decides how many of these visits are distinct layers
4
(a shared middle segment of a third to a half of the layers
leaves fewer physical layers than a loop-free model has at the
same compute); and the number of experts fills the parameter
budget, matched within about 2%, so the parameters that
the loop saves go to the experts. At the budget of DiT-XL/2
(28 layers, 0.68B parameters, 118.6 GMAC), DyLoMo-DiT-
XL has 16 physical layers and 23 layer visits on average;
removing the loop and spending the saved parameters on
independent layers gives the single-pass member at the same
budget (23 layers), which Secs. 5.1 and 5.4 use to isolate the
loop (all configurations: App. H).
4. Experimental Setup
To ensure a fair comparison with DiT and DiT-MoE, we
train all class-conditional models on ImageNet [18] using
the same training setup. We also train a single text-to-image
model, without ablations, to assess whether DyLoMo-DiT
extends to text-conditioned generation. This model scales
up the architecture in Sec. 3: it has width 1024, 32 physi-
cal layers with the middle 16 looped, 4 routing heads with
128 experts each, 15.48B total parameters, and 1.05B ac-
tive parameters per image token. It differs from the class-
conditional models in three respects: text and image tokens
form a single sequence, with conditioning written into each
token’s input rather than injected through adaLN; it uses a
4-stream residual, which trains normally without adaLN and
improves performance (App. I); and it is trained with the
rectified-flow objective [43].
Training details. All ImageNet models follow a com-
mon pipeline based on the DiT recipe: VAE latents [56],
the DDPM objective [33, 47], a global batch size of 256,
400k training steps, AdamW [44] with a constant learning
rate of 10−4, and EMA with decay 0.9999 (App. H). We
train the DiT baselines from scratch using the official model
definitions; their unguided FID agrees with Table 4 of the
DiT paper (App. H). We implement DiT-MoE [23] based on
its public code (Sec. 5.2, App. J).
ImageNet-256. We evaluate class-conditional generation
on ImageNet at 256 × 256, following the DiT protocol [52].
We use EMA weights and sample with 250-step DDPM and
classifier-free guidance [32] at scale 1.5. Every 100k training
steps, we generate 50k images per checkpoint and evaluate
FID, sFID [46], IS [58], Precision, and Recall [37] using the
ADM evaluator [20].
Compute matching. Because both model capacity and
inference cost matter, we match each DyLoMo-DiT model
to its baseline in total parameter count and forward compute,
unless stated otherwise. We measure forward compute as the
number of multiply-accumulates per image in one forward
pass. GMAC denotes 109 multiply-accumulates, counted in
the same way as the Gflops reported in the DiT paper. For
models with noise-dependent loop schedules, we average
compute over the 250 uniformly spaced sampling steps. Pa-
rameter counts include the adaLN modulation layers, which
account for about one third of DiT’s parameters while adding
negligible compute; these layers are shared within the looped
segment. Excluding adaLN, DyLoMo-DiT has 1.2–1.3× the
parameters of the DiT in the same tier (App. B). All reported
results are means over seeds, and differences and percentages
are computed from unrounded values.
Text-to-image evaluation.
We report GenEval [27];
DPG-Bench [34] and COCO FID [41] are reported in App. A.
We rerun and rescore public models using the same evalua-
tion pipeline at their native resolutions. Differences smaller
than 0.03 are treated as not meaningful (App. A).
5. Results
The experiments have four stages.
We first compare
DyLoMo-DiT with DiT and DiT-MoE on ImageNet-256
at matched total parameters and forward compute (Secs. 5.1
and 5.2), and then against scaled-up baselines (Sec. 5.3).
Next, we ablate the design choices and analyze the loop’s
parameter–compute trade-off, the noise levels that bene-
fit from extra passes, and the learned routing (Secs. 5.4
to 5.7). Finally, we test a text-to-image model at a larger
scale (Sec. 5.8).
5.1. Comparison with DiT at Equal Parameters and
Compute
At matched parameter and compute budgets, DyLoMo-DiT
reduces FID relative to DiT by 11%, 14%, and 24% in the B,
L, and XL tiers, respectively (Table 2). Its advantage grows
during training: the FID ratio of DyLoMo-DiT to DiT de-
clines from 100k to 400k steps in every tier (B: 1.05 →0.89;
L: 0.98 →0.86; XL: 0.93 →0.76), as shown in Figure 2.
The no-loop variant accounts for part of the improvement;
adding the shared loop with noise-dependent pass allocation
further reduces FID by 3%, 8%, and 16% in the B, L, and
XL tiers, with larger gains at larger scales. The advantage
persists with longer training: at 1M steps, DyLoMo-DiT-XL
reaches an FID of 2.46 versus 3.08 for DiT-XL/2 (Fig. 4);
at 1.6M steps, it reaches 2.18, below the 2.27 reported for
DiT-XL/2 after 7M steps [52]. Figure 5 compares samples
generated from the same class label and noise.

5.4. Ablating the Design at the DiT-MoE-B Budget
To isolate each design choice, Table 4 adds them incremen-
tally at the DiT-MoE-B budget. The single-pass model re-
duces FID from 16.06 to 7.93. A uniform two-pass loop
leaves FID essentially unchanged (7.94), showing that recur-
rence alone does not improve this sparse-expert model at the
matched budget. Noise-dependent pass allocation then low-
ers FID by 12%, with three routing heads and finer-grained
experts reducing it further to 5.28. The final model’s FID
is 67% lower than DiT-MoE-B; other layouts (layer count,
width, and routing heads) are in App. B.
5.5. The Loop’s Parameter–Compute Trade-off
We next examine the loop’s two resource trade-offs (Fig. 6).
At fixed compute (63 GMAC), reducing parameters to
0.72×, 0.58×, and 0.31× of the two-pass model raises FID
by 14%, 27%, and 41%, respectively. Even with less than
one third of its parameters, the smallest model achieves
13% and 30% lower FID than the two DiT-MoE configura-
tions at the same compute. Conversely, at fixed parameters
(796M), increasing passes from one to four reduces FID by
21%, 15%, and 2.7% across successive increases. Allocating
passes by noise level is worth roughly one extra pass: at
two-pass compute, it approaches three passes throughout
(7.01 vs. 6.72 FID), and at three-pass compute, it matches
four passes throughout (6.47 vs. 6.54).
5.6. Allocating Passes by Noise Level
We next examine where additional loop computation is use-
ful along the denoising trajectory. Compared with two passes
at every timestep, allocated-pass DyLoMo-DiT-XL has lower
noise-prediction error at every t < 500 (by up to 0.6%
around t = 150–200), slightly higher error just above 500,
and similar error beyond 600 (Fig. 7a). At the DiT budget,
noise-dependent allocation lowers FID by 4.2%, 4.7%, and
3.4% in the B, L, and XL tiers, using all available seeds; at
the DiT-MoE budget, it improves the single-head B model
by 12% (App. B). Reversing the allocation worsens the B-
tier FID by 33% (to 26.14), while a three-band schedule
performs similarly to the two-band schedule (App. G).
The pass schedule can also be chosen after training
(App. G). A DyLoMo-DiT-XL trained with pass counts sam-
pled from {1, 2, 3} can run under different schedules; any
schedule with at least two passes at low noise reaches nearly
the same FID (within 3%). When run with two passes, this
model reaches 4.68 FID, 7% worse than the model trained
with two passes throughout (4.36), reflecting the cost of
choosing depth at inference.

5.8. Text-to-Image Generation
Scored with the same pipeline at their native resolutions,

token. DyLoMo-DiT uses 24% and 22% as much forward-
pass compute as FLUX.1-schnell and FLUX.1-dev, respec-
tively. Sample generations are shown in Figure 1.
6. Conclusion
DyLoMo-DiT improves matched-budget efficiency by com-
bining sparse experts with weight-shared recurrence, with
further gains from dynamically assigning more passes to
low-noise steps. Routers also learn noise-level specialization
without explicit timestep inputs. On ImageNet, DyLoMo-
DiT reduces FID by 11–24% versus DiT and 36–56% versus
DiT-MoE at matched budgets; at equal compute, it outper-
forms DiT-MoE-B with less than one third of its parameters,
and a DiT with three times the parameters and compute
8
remains 13% behind; at 1.6M steps, it surpasses the FID re-
ported for DiT-XL/2 after 7M steps. Scaled up to 15B param-
eters, it becomes a production-scale text-to-image model that
scores above FLUX.1 on GenEval with less than one tenth
of its active parameters, and it transfers to video (App. C).

A. Sampling and Evaluation Details of the Text-
to-Image Experiments
This appendix gives the details behind Sec. 4 (text-to-image
setup) and Sec. 5.8, and lists the prompts of the head figure
(Fig. 1). The experiment asks whether the same architecture
carries over from class-conditional generation on ImageNet
to text-to-image; only one model is trained, with no ablation.
Images are encoded with the Wan2.2 VAE [69] and text with
a frozen Qwen2.5-VL-7B [3]; sampling uses 50 Euler steps
with APG [57] at guidance scale 5.
Model.
The first and last layers use dense feed-forward
networks, and the 30 layers in between use sparse-expert
feed-forward networks; each token selects 6 experts per head,
and there is one always-on shared expert (width 1280). The
looped segment is the middle 16 layers, with 8 layers before
it and 8 after it, so one forward pass executes 8+16×2+8 =
48 layers. Active parameters are counted as follows: the
layers of the looped segment are counted once; the routers,
which score all experts, are counted in full; the image input
and output adapter layers are counted once. The timestep
is written into the first 64 channels of the input as a 64-
dimensional sinusoidal encoding.
Encoders.
The VAE has 48 channels and downsamples
spatially by 16×; there is no further patching, so one latent
position is one token. Text features are taken from the output
of the third-to-last layer of the text encoder, before its final
normalization layer, with at most 1,000 tokens.
Checkpoints.
Table 5 uses the checkpoint evaluated at
1024 × 1024 (the checkpoint of the head figure and of the
gallery below). Earlier checkpoints score 0.705 at 448×256
and 0.700 at 784 × 448; the samples in Fig. A1 come from
the latter.
Sampling.
Euler method with 50 steps; u is taken at
equally spaced points from 1 to 0 and then passed through
the shift transform σ = s · u/(1 + (s −1) · u) with s = 1.6.
APG [57] acts on the predicted clean image, with guid-
ance scale 5, momentum −0.5, parallel-component weight
η = 0.15 and no rescaling; the unconditional branch uses
the empty text, and no negative prompt is used. All three
metrics use this one set of settings.
Metrics.
GenEval [27] has 553 prompts with 4 images
each, 2,212 images in total; we use the official implemen-
tation, with Mask2Former (Swin-S) [10] as the detector
and colors judged by CLIP [55]. DPG-Bench has 1,065
prompts with 4 images each, scored with the script released
by ELLA [34] and mPLUG-large [39]; the official script
reads a 2 × 2 grid and splits it, whereas we feed the 4 single
images directly; we also fixed one issue in the upstream pars-
ing script, which skipped the first question of every prompt.
COCO FID uses the first 10,000 captions of the commonly
used 30,000-caption MS-COCO [41] val2014 list, one image
per caption, with the original images of these captions as
the reference set; generated and reference images are both
resized directly to 256 × 256 (no cropping, so the aspect
ratio changes), and FID is computed with clean-fid [51].
DyLoMo-DiT scores 83.6 on DPG-Bench at 448 × 256 and
85.2 at 784 × 448, and its COCO FID is 20.5 at 448 × 256.

B. Parameters, Compute and Results of All
Models and Ablations
This appendix is the listing that Sec. 4 refers to: parame-
ters, forward compute and FID over training of every model
and ablation of the ImageNet experiments (Tables B1, B3
and B5). It also gives the numbers that Secs. 5.1 to 5.3,
5.5 and 5.6 summarize: the continuation to 1M steps, the
sub-figures on parameters added at equal compute and on
the XL tier, the trade between layers and passes, the arms
with fewer parameters and more compute, and the remaining
layouts at the budget of DiT-MoE-B.
Compute accounting.
The numbers are measured on the
instantiated models and include the router scoring (0.07% of
the total for DyLoMo-DiT-XL) but not the score nonlinearity
and the top-k selection. Equal compute does not mean an
equal number of layers: DyLoMo-DiT-XL has hidden width
1024 (1152 for DiT-XL/2) and 23 layer visits on average (28
layers for DiT-XL/2), so each visit costs on average about
1.2 times one layer of DiT-XL/2, with the excess coming
mainly from the sparse experts. Active parameters are the
parameters that one token uses, counted per layer visit (a
token that selects different experts in different passes of the
looped segment uses more than this count): the layers of
the looped segment are counted once, the routed experts in
proportion to the number selected, and everything else in
full.
Models at the budget of DiT.
Table B1 lists the nine rows
of Table 2 together with the models that share their budget. In
Table 2, against the DiT of each tier, sFID, IS and Precision
move in the same direction as FID, and Recall differs by at
most 0.01; in the B tier DyLoMo-DiT is still behind DiT-B/2
at 100k steps and ahead from 200k steps on. A second B-tier
layout at equal parameters and compute, with 16 physical
layers and a looped middle segment, reaches 17.30 (−18%
against DiT-B/2); the main text keeps the 12-layer layout,
which has the depth of DiT-B/2. A second layout without
the loop at the XL tier (28 layers, 5 experts per head with 4
selected) gives 5.13, against 5.02 for the 23-layer layout of
Table 2.

Layers against passes.
With both parameters and compute
fixed, layers and passes are interchangeable between two and
four passes: in the B tier (single routing head, budget of DiT-
MoE-B), 12 layers with three passes and 24 experts per layer
give 7.89 against 7.94 for 16 layers with two passes; in the
XL tier, 11 layers with four passes give 4.44 against 4.36 for
16 layers with two, while eight passes over 9 layers (5.87)
are worse (Tables B1 and B5).
Fewer parameters with more compute; five low-noise
passes.

E. Scaling Curve of the Text-to-Image Architec-
ture
This appendix supports the statement of Sec. 1 that the design
scales in the text-to-image setting (Sec. 5.8). It tests whether
the same architecture keeps improving with more parameters
across scales. All three scales use the architecture of the text-
to-image model (the middle half of the layers executed in
two passes, sparse experts routed per head, and a multi-
stream residual with 4 streams), changing only the width,
the number of layers and the number of experts (Table E1).
All are trained from random initialization for 6,000 steps
with the same data, batch and learning-rate schedule; the
validation set is fixed, and the timestep and noise of every
image are fixed and identical across the three scales sample
by sample. For each scale a control without multi-stream
residual is also trained. We report the validation loss at 6,000
steps.
Validation loss decreases monotonically with scale: 2.6%
lower from 2.87B to 15.48B, and a further 1.1% lower at
34.73B (Fig. E1a); the training curves keep the same order at
all 12 evaluation points (Fig. E1b). The group without multi-
stream residual also decreases monotonically with scale. At
all three scales the version with multi-stream residual has the
lower validation loss, by 0.8%, 0.7% and 0.6% respectively.
These differences are reported for description only.

is part of the model and of its training, and total parameters
and mean compute stay equal to those of the baseline.

The method places the

Return on passes in a video model.
The return on extra
passes at fixed parameters (Sec. 5.5) is also observed on a
separate in-house video generation model: image-to-video,
24 layers, 64 experts per head, with the multi-stream residual
of App. I, 2.87B parameters in total, looping layers 7 to
18. At every training step the number of passes is drawn
uniformly at random from {1, 2, 3, 4}, for 10B tokens in
total. The same weights are then evaluated with one to four
passes by the validation loss on 2,000 held-out video clips
(the flow-matching [42] mean squared error, with fixed noise,
so the four settings are comparable per sample; Table G5).
With each additional pass the gain falls to about three tenths
of the previous one: the third pass executes 33% more layers
and lowers the validation loss by 0.0036; the fourth adds
another 25% and lowers it by 0.0011.
Two points bear on reading Table G5. During training
each of the four settings received only a quarter of the steps,
and the single pass is diluted the most, so the decrease at
the second pass is inflated (when the same architecture is
trained with the pass count drawn only from {1, 2}, this
term is 0.0074); the increments at the third and fourth passes
are diluted about equally on both sides of the comparison
and can be compared with each other. The differences in
the table are obtained by spending more compute on the
same weights and are not a comparison at equal compute;
the ImageNet models trained separately at a fixed number of
passes (Fig. 6b, Fig. B4) show the same diminishing return.
In addition, LOOM [30], with a separate router and residual
scaling per pass, still gains from pushing the loop beyond
five passes in language models, so the diminishing return at
the third and fourth passes may depend on the recipe and
need not be a limit of looping itself.

H. Architecture and Protocol Details
This appendix completes Sec. 3 and Sec. 4: the architecture
with all values (Fig. H1), the configurations of all tiers, the
remaining details of the layers (position embedding, nor-
malization, initialization, routing weights, activation), the
training and evaluation protocol, the agreement of the DiT
baselines with the DiT paper, and the number of seeds behind
every number.
Configurations.
Table H1 lists the configurations at the
budget of DiT and Table H2 the single-head configurations
at the budget of DiT-MoE; the layout of every other arm is
given with its results in App. B. The middle segment is about
half of the layers: at the XL tier L = 16 with layers 5 to 11
looped; at the B and L tiers L = 12 with layers 4 to 9 looped,
so that a token passes through 24 layers at low noise and 12
at high noise. At the XL tier H = 4, E = 16 and k = 6, the
intermediate width of both the experts and the shared expert
is 640, and each token activates 24 routed experts per layer.
In the single-head B model the experts have intermediate
width 1256 and the shared expert 760; the three-head model
of Table 4 keeps the expert width and has a shared expert of
width 776.
Input, conditioning and normalization.
The input is the
VAE latent, split into 2×2 patches and linearly projected into
tokens; the timestep embedding and the class embedding are
summed into a conditioning vector c, which every sublayer
injects through adaLN-Zero; positional information is sup-
plied by rotary position embeddings [63] inside attention;
the output layer predicts the noise and the variance. All nor-
malization in the network is RMSNorm, applied once each
to q and k; the DiT baselines keep the official implemen-
tation. The rotary position encoding is a two-dimensional
Fourier RoPE with 16 frequency bands, rotating only the
first 3/4 of the dimensions of each attention head; the sig-
moid gate of each attention head reads the normalized input.
Normalization is computed in FP32 with eps = 10−6.
Initialization.
Router weights are drawn from a normal
distribution with standard deviation 0.02. The experts’ up-
projection has standard deviation 256−1/2 and the down-
projection f −1/2. The remaining linear layers have standard
deviation equal to the input dimension to the power −1/2,
none with bias; the exceptions are the adaLN modulation
layers (with bias, zero-initialized) and the patch embedding
layer (PyTorch default initialization, with bias). The load-
balancing biases start at zero. The RMSNorm w, the at-
tention sink and the output layer are also zero-initialized.
The timestep and class embeddings use the PyTorch default
initialization.
Routing weights.
The constant α = 4.9 that multiplies
the normalized routing weights in Sec. 3.2 is the default of
the implementation and was not tuned.
Activation function.
The experts, the shared expert and
the dense feed-forward networks of the first and last layers
all use a clamped SwiGLU: the gate branch g is clipped to
at most 7, the other branch u is clipped to [−7, 7], and the
output is g ·σ(1.702·g)·(u+1). This SwiGLU [60] variant
with clipping and a residual term appears in gpt-oss [48].
Protocol details.
Images are encoded with sd-vae-ft-
ema [56] with scaling factor 0.18215 into 32 × 32 × 4 la-
tents. Training uses a global batch of 256 for 400k steps,
AdamW [44] with β = (0.9, 0.999), a constant learning
rate of 10−4, no weight decay, and an EMA coefficient of
0.9999. DDPM [33] uses a 1000-step linear noise schedule,
predicting the noise and learning the variance [47]; evalua-
tion follows Sec. 4, using ADM’s [20] TensorFlow evaluator
and reference statistics.

I. Full Results for the Multi-Stream Residual
This appendix gives the evaluation of the multi-stream resid-
ual that Sec. 2 and Sec. 4 refer to: on ImageNet it is on
par when training completes normally but makes training
unstable together with adaLN, so it is not part of the method;
without adaLN it trains normally and brings a gain, so the
text-to-image model keeps it.
Multi-stream residual.
Hyper-Connections [82] widen
the residual stream into several streams and learn the con-
nections between them; its appendix reports on DiT-XL/2
that static HC (SHC×2) improves FID from 2.36 to 2.18
(a baseline with FP16 and QK normalization, 1400 epochs,
guidance scale 1.5). mHC [77] constrains the mixing matrix
between streams to be doubly stochastic; oHC [29] con-
strains it to the rotation group and parameterizes it with a
pair of unit quaternions; other models fix it to the identity
(iHC), such as the language model Hy4-preview [67] and
Chimera [26]. The experiments of mHC and oHC are only
on language models. In diffusion models, MAGI-2 [59] uses
mHC; Chimera uses iHC together with sparse experts and
reports that both improve the compute efficiency of pretrain-
ing (measured by the compute needed to reach the same
training loss); Liang et al. [40] compare single-stream and
four-stream mHC on ImageNet-256 and find that the gain
depends on the prediction target: at the B tier in pixel space,
FID goes from 10.19 to 9.78 with x-prediction and from
139.83 to 25.36 with v-prediction. We evaluate oHC sep-
arately as an optional component on top of DyLoMo-DiT,
with the results below.
The DyLoMo-DiT of this paper has no multi-stream resid-
ual. We additionally trained versions with oHC, in which the
residual stream is widened to 4 streams mixed by an SO(4)
rotation, with the training and evaluation protocol unchanged
and 1.2% more compute. These experiments were run on the
XL family with 32 experts per head and two passes at every
timestep (1.13B parameters; App. B) and on its ablations.
When training completes normally, the version with oHC
is on par with the version without: the final FID is 4.20,
against 4.21 without. Under this recipe, however, training
with oHC is unstable: of the 9 runs across the arms with
oHC, 7 have FID rising rather than falling at later evaluation
points (back up to tens, at most over three hundred); in the
11 runs of these arms without oHC (9 at the XL tier, 2 at the
L tier), FID decreases monotonically with training steps. In
these abnormal runs the model itself degrades in the same
way: the quality of individual images drops while diversity
does not. Evaluating the abnormal checkpoints directly with
the raw (non-EMA) weights gives FID from 23.96 to 46.24,
Precision from 0.43 to 0.58 and Recall from 0.62 to 0.69;
the normally completed checkpoints give 7.03 to 10.50, 0.70
to 0.74 and 0.55 to 0.59 respectively. In 2 of these runs
(300.19 and 255.67) the EMA weights collapse further on
top of this, with FID in the hundreds and Recall close to
0. The phenomenon also appears in the arms with dense
feed-forward and no routing, so it does not depend on the
routing of the sparse experts. We therefore did not include
oHC in the method proper.

J. Sparse-Expert Baselines
This appendix documents the DiT-MoE baseline of Sec. 4
and Secs. 5.2 and 5.3: its two configurations (both in the
S, B and L tiers; the original one also at XL), how the
implementation was checked against the released code, how
our training differs from the recipe of the DiT-MoE paper,
and what two independent works report for the same kind of
routing.
Implementation and the two configurations.
DiT-MoE
is implemented from the code released by its authors [23].
A model keeps the width, depth and attention heads of the
DiT of the same name; the feed-forward network of every
layer is replaced by routed experts plus one shared expert,
with SwiGLU experts. The router takes a softmax over
the experts, selects those with the highest probability and
weights them by the probabilities without renormalization;
the shared expert is computed for every token with weight
1. Load balancing uses an expert-level auxiliary loss with
coefficient 0.005, the value in the text of the DiT-MoE paper
(the default of the released code is 0.01), with statistics
computed locally in each data-parallel process. The training
protocol, optimizer, EMA, sampling and evaluation are those
of all other models (Sec. 4). We train two configurations
(Table J1). The original configuration has 8 experts per layer
with 2 selected, each of intermediate width 4d, and a shared
expert of width 2d (the released code implements the two
shared experts of the paper as one feed-forward network
of this width). The second configuration cuts every expert
in two: 16 experts per layer of width 2d with 4 selected
and the same shared expert, so that total parameters, active
parameters and compute are unchanged up to the router; it
has the number of experts per layer of the DyLoMo-DiT
it is compared with in Table 3. At the B tier, the original
configuration (12.91) is 20% below the configuration with
16 experts (16.06), and DyLoMo-DiT (7.01) is 46% and
56% below the two. At the S tier the two configurations
are level: the original one gives 30.80 and the one with 16
experts 30.83, and DyLoMo-DiT (19.79) is 36% below both,
so the advantage of the original configuration at the B tier
does not carry over to the S tier. At the L tier the original
configuration is at 34.23, 16.38, 10.33 and 7.50 after 100k,
200k, 300k and 400k steps, against 35.64, 17.27, 10.94 and
7.92 for the configuration with 16 experts, so it is 5% below

Differences from the recipe of the DiT-MoE paper.
The
DiT-MoE paper trains with a batch size of 1024 for 500k,
1M and 7M iterations and reports, with guidance and after
long training, an FID of 1.72 for its XL configuration against
2.27 for DiT-XL/2. Our runs use the protocol of all other
models in this paper (batch 256, 400k steps, DDPM, 250
sampling steps, guidance scale 1.5). The DiT-MoE numbers
of this paper therefore characterize its recipe under this pro-
tocol and say nothing about the results in its paper. Training
every model under one protocol is also what ProMoE [74]
and DiffMoE [62] do when they compare with this kind of
routing; both in addition reconfigure the sparse-expert base-
lines to the active parameters of the dense model, whereas
Sec. 5.2 keeps the width, depth, routing, shared expert and
auxiliary loss of DiT-MoE and configures our model for its
budget.
Verification against the released code.
The checks were
made on the two eight-expert models at the compute of
DiT-XL/2. Given the same inputs and weights, our imple-
mentation and the released code agree bitwise in BF16 on the
forward output of the training branch and on the gradients of
all parameters; both expert widths were checked (the check
at expert width 1232 used a shared expert of width 608; the
shared expert is an ordinary dense feed-forward network
and does not go through the expert kernel). The remaining
checks were done on the model with experts of width 768: a
full-model FP32 forward pass on CPU with the 100k-step
EMA weights gives a relative L2 error of 2.4 × 10−5 to
3.5 × 10−4, and the gradient injected by the auxiliary loss
agrees bitwise with explicit differentiation (CPU, FP32). A
control trained with the original implementation of the re-
leased code (without kernel fusion) reaches FID 36.14 at
100k steps, comparable to the 37.20 of our implementation.
The ordering of the training loss across evaluation points
agrees with that of FID. FID evaluated with the raw (non-
EMA) weights at 100k steps is 41.36, worse than the 37.20
with EMA weights, so the gap to DiT does not come from a
mismatch between EMA weights and routing. The routing
of the two models stays close to uniform over training: the
normalized entropy, averaged over layers and positions, is
between 0.96 and 0.98 at every checkpoint, and no expert is
ever unused; the share of selections that goes to one expert
(12.5% when uniform) ranges from 7% to 18% in the model
with experts of width 1232 and from 1.3% to 29.4% at 400k
steps in the model with experts of width 768.
Independent evidence.
Two independent works that re-
train this kind of routing next to a dense DiT under one
protocol find the same direction as Secs. 5.2 and 5.3. Pro-
MoE (Table 10 of [74]; DDPM, L tier, 500k steps) reports
FID 23.12 without guidance and 7.55 at guidance scale 1.5
for its DiT-MoE-L, which has 8 experts with 1 selected and
no shared expert, against 20.81 and 6.29 for the dense DiT-L
(its Table 4). DiffMoE (Table 11 of [62]; DDPM, L tier, 700k
steps) reports 20.81 for token-choice routing with 16 experts
against 17.87 for the dense DiT-L. In both, token-choice
sparse experts are worse than the dense model at equal active
parameters.

DiT-MoE is the
baseline of the main text because the later models start from
its design: ProMoE [74], DiffMoE and EC-DiT keep a DiT
backbone with a single pass over distinct layers and change
how tokens are routed to the experts, a different axis from the
budgeting of depth, parameters and compute studied here.
We also compare with ProMoE at equal total parameters
and compute. Its model code is ported from the authors’

K. Flow-Matching Objective
The ImageNet experiments of the main paper use the DDPM
objective of the DiT protocol (Sec. 4). To test whether
the comparison with DiT depends on this objective, we
retrain the L-tier and XL-tier models of Table 2, DiT-L/2
and DiT-XL/2 and the DyLoMo-DiT of each tier, with a
flow-matching objective [42, 43, 45]: the input is the linear
interpolation xσ = (1 −σ) · x0 + σ · ϵ with σ drawn uni-
formly from [0, 1], the network predicts the velocity ϵ −x0,
and the time embedding receives 1000 σ. The architectures,
their parameters and compute, and all other training settings
are those of the DDPM runs. DyLoMo-DiT keeps its pass
schedule with the boundary at σ = 0.5: three passes for
σ < 0.5 and one otherwise, two passes on average. All
models are sampled from the EMA weights with 250 Euler
steps uniformly spaced in σ and evaluated as in Sec. 4.
At guidance scale 1.5, DyLoMo-DiT is 11% below DiT in
the L tier and 15% below it in the XL tier; without guidance
it is 14% and 17% below (Table K1). Under the DDPM
objective the gaps are 14% and 24% with guidance and 20%
and 25% without (Table 2, App. H). The L-tier entries are
means over two seeds. At guidance scale 1.5, the two DiT-
L/2 runs reach 6.75 and 6.57 and the two DyLoMo-DiT-L
runs 5.90 and 6.00: both DyLoMo-DiT-L runs are below
both DiT-L/2 runs, and the two seeds of a model differ by at
most 0.19, against 0.71 between the means of the two models.
The XL tier has one seed. The gain therefore carries over to
a flow-matching objective; with this few seeds, its direction
is better established than its size. The two objectives are
sampled differently, so FID values are compared only within
an objective.
