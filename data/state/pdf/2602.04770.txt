Abstract
Generative modeling can be formulated as learning a mapping f such that its
pushforward distribution matches the data distribution. The pushforward behavior
can be carried out iteratively at inference time, e.g., in diffusion/flow-based models.
In this paper, we propose Drifting Models, a new generative modeling paradigm
that evolves the pushforward distribution during training and naturally admits
one-step inference. We introduce a drifting field that governs the sample movement
and achieves equilibrium when the distributions match. This leads to a training
objective that allows the neural network optimizer to evolve the distribution. In
experiments, our one-step generator achieves state-of-the-art one-step results on
ImageNet 256×256, with FID 1.54 in latent space and 1.61 in pixel space. We hope
that our work opens up new opportunities for high-quality one-step generation.

Introduction
Generative models are commonly regarded as more challenging than discriminative models. While
discriminative modeling typically focuses on mapping individual samples to their corresponding
labels, generative modeling concerns mapping from one distribution to another. This can be expressed
as learning a mapping f such that the pushforward of a prior distribution pprior matches the data
distribution, namely, f#pprior ≈pdata. Conceptually, generative modeling learns a functional (here,
f#) that maps from one function (here, a distribution) to another.
The “pushforward” behavior can be realized iteratively at inference time, e.g., in prevailing paradigms
such as Diffusion [65] and Flow Matching [49]. When generating, these models map noisier samples
to slightly cleaner ones, progressively evolving the sample distribution toward the data distribution.
This modeling philosophy can be viewed as decomposing a complex pushforward map (i.e., f#) into
a chain of more feasible transformations, applied at inference time.
In this paper, we propose Drifting Models, a new generative modeling paradigm that learns a
pushforward map by evolving it during training time, thereby removing the need for an iterative
inference procedure. The mapping f is represented by a single-pass, non-iterative network. As the
training process is inherently iterative in deep learning optimization, it can be naturally viewed as
evolving the pushforward distribution, f#pprior, through the update of f. See Fig. 1.

the sample movement. This field depends on the generated distribution and the data distribution. By
definition, this field becomes zero when the two distributions match, thereby reaching an equilibrium
in which the samples no longer drift.
Building on this formulation, we propose a simple training objective that minimizes the drift of the
generated samples. This objective induces sample movements and thereby evolves the underlying
pushforward distribution through iterative optimization (e.g., SGD). We further introduce the designs
of the drifting field, the neural network model, and the training algorithm.
Drifting Models naturally perform single-step (“1-NFE”) generation and achieve strong empirical
performance. On ImageNet 256×256, we obtain a 1-NFE FID of 1.54 under the standard latent-space
generation protocol, achieving a new state-of-the-art among single-step methods. This result remains
competitive even when compared with multi-step diffusion-/flow-based models. Further, under the
more challenging pixel-space generation protocol (i.e., without latents), we reach a 1-NFE FID of
1.61, substantially outperforming previous pixel-space methods. These results suggest that Drifting
Models offer a promising framework for high-quality, efficient generative modeling.

Related Work
Diffusion-/Flow-based Models.
Diffusion models (e.g., [65, 31, 67]) and their flow-based coun-
terparts (e.g., [49, 50, 2]) formulate noise-to-data mappings through differential equations (SDEs
or ODEs). At the core of their inference-time computation is an iterative update, e.g., of the form
xi+1 = xi + ∆xi, such as with an Euler solver. The update ∆xi depends on the neural network f,
and as a result, generation involves multiple steps of network evaluations.
A growing body of work has focused on reducing the steps of diffusion-/flow-based models.
Distillation-based methods (e.g., [62, 53, 79, 91]) distill a pretrained multi-step model into a single-
step one. Another line of research aims to train one-step diffusion/flow models from scratch (e.g.,
[68, 19, 3, 20]). To achieve this, these methods utilize the SDE/ODE dynamics in training by approxi-
mating the induced trajectories. In contrast, our work presents a conceptually different framework and
does not rely on SDE/ODE formulations in diffusion/flow models (more discussions in Appendix C).
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs).
GANs [22] are a classical family of models that train
a generator by discriminating generated samples from real data. Like GANs, our method involves
a single-pass network f that maps noise to data, whose “goodness” is evaluated by a loss function;
however, unlike GANs, our method does not rely on adversarial optimization. Implicit Maximum
Likelihood Estimation (IMLE) [45] trains a generator without a discriminator, by pulling the nearest
generated sample toward each data sample; it has no repulsion between generated samples.
Variational Autoencoders (VAEs).
VAEs [38] optimize the ELBO, consisting of reconstruction
and KL terms. Classical VAEs are one-step generators with Gaussian priors, while modern uses often
treat VAEs as tokenizers with learned priors from diffusion or autoregressive models [60, 18].
Normalizing Flows (NFs).
NFs [59, 12, 83] learn data-to-noise mappings by maximizing sample
log-likelihood. They require invertible architectures with tractable Jacobians. Conceptually, NFs are
one-step generators at inference, with computation performed by the inverse of the network.
Moment Matching.
Moment-matching methods [17, 47] seek to minimize the Maximum Mean
Discrepancy (MMD) between the generated and data distributions. Moment Matching has recently
been extended to one-/few-step diffusion [89]. Related to MMD, our approach also leverages the
concepts of kernel functions and positive/negative samples. However, our approach focuses on a
drifting field that explicitly governs the sample drifts at training time; MMD relates to a special case
of this field. Further discussion is in Appendix C.4 and D.2.
Contrastive Learning.
Our drifting field is driven by positive samples from the data distribution
and negative samples from the generated distribution. This is conceptually related to the positive
and negative samples and softmax-normalized weights in contrastive representation learning, e.g.,
[23, 56, 25, 5]. Contrastive learning has also been extended to generative models, e.g., in energy-
based models trained with contrastive divergence [29, 41, 77, 55, 15, 16], in GANs [72, 35], or in
Flow Matching [70]; we discuss the relation to Coulomb GAN in Appendix C.5.
Drifting Fields in Existing Generative Models.
For one-step generative methods that minimize a
loss between two distributions p and q, our framework implies the existence of an underlying drifting
field V. In particular, one may define V∝−∂L

Drifting Models for Generation
We propose Drifting Models, which frame generative modeling as a training-time evolution of the
pushforward distribution via a drifting field. This naturally allows one-step generation at inference.
3.1
Pushforward at Training Time
Consider a neural network f : RC 7→RD with input ϵ ∼pϵ (e.g., noise in dimension C) and output
x = f(ϵ) ∈RD. In general, the input and output dimensions need not be equal.
We denote the distribution of the network output by q, i.e., x = f(ϵ) ∼q. In probability theory, q is
referred to as the pushforward distribution of pϵ under f, denoted by:
q = f#pϵ.
(1)
Here, “f#” denotes the pushforward induced by f. Intuitively, it means that f transforms a distribution
pϵ into another distribution q. The goal of generative modeling is to find f such that f#pϵ ≈pdata.
Since neural network training is inherently iterative (e.g., SGD), the training process produces a
sequence of models {fi}, where i denotes the training iteration. This corresponds to a sequence of
pushforward distributions {qi} during training, where qi = [fi]#pϵ for each i. The training process
progressively evolves qi to match pdata.
When the network f is updated, a sample at training iteration i is implicitly “drifted” as: xi+1 =
xi + ∆xi, where ∆xi := fi+1(ϵ) −fi(ϵ) arises from parameter updates to f. This implies that the
update of f determines the “residual” of x, which we refer to as the “drift”.
3.2
Drifting Field for Training
Next, we define a drifting field to govern the training-time evolution of the samples x and, conse-
quently, the pushforward distribution q. A drifting field is a function that computes ∆x given x.
Formally, denoting this field by Vp,q(·): Rd →Rd, we have:
xi+1 = xi + Vp,qi(xi),
(2)
Here, xi = fi(ϵ) ∼qi and after drifting we denote xi+1 ∼qi+1. The subscripts p, q denote that this
field depends on p (e.g., p = pdata) and the current distribution q.
Ideally, when p = q, we want all x to stop drifting i.e., V = 0. We consider the following proposition:
Proposition 3.1. Consider an anti-symmetric drifting field:
Vp,q(x) = −Vq,p(x),
∀x.

We note that the converse implication, i.e., Vp,q = 0 ⇒q = p, is false in general for arbitrary
choices of V. For our kernelized formulation (Sec. 3.3), we give sufficient conditions under which
Vp,q ≈0 implies q ≈p (Appendix D.1).

Equilibrium and Matched Distributions. Since our training loss in Eq. (6) encourages minimizing
∥V∥2, we hope that V ≈0 leads to q ≈p. While this implication does not hold for arbitrary choices
of V, we empirically observe that decreasing the value of ∥V∥2 correlates with improved generation

for our kernelized construction, zero drift imposes many bilinear constraints on (p, q), which under
mild non-degeneracy assumptions force approximate matching of p and q.

the negative samples in the same batch, i.e., y−∼q. On the other hand, we sample Npos data points
y+ ∼pdata. The drifting field V is computed in this batch of positive and negative samples. Alg. 1

3.5
Classifier-Free Guidance
Classifier-free guidance (CFG) [30] improves generation quality by extrapolating between class-
conditional and unconditional distributions. Our method naturally supports a related form of guidance.
In our model, given a class label c as the condition, the underlying target distribution p now becomes
pdata(·|c), from which we can draw positive samples: y+ ∼pdata(·|c). To achieve guidance, we draw
negative samples either from generated samples or real samples from different classes. Formally, the
negative sample distribution is now:
˜q(·|c) ≜(1 −γ) qθ(·|c) + γ pdata(·|∅).
(15)
Here, γ ∈[0, 1) is a mixing rate, and pdata(·|∅) denotes the unconditional data distribution2. The
goal of learning is to find ˜q(·|c) = pdata(·|c). Substituting it into (15), we obtain:
qθ(·|c) = α pdata(·|c) −(α −1) pdata(·|∅).
(16)
where α =
1
1−γ ≥1. This implies that qθ(·|c) is to approximate a linear combination of conditional
and unconditional data distributions. This follows the spirit of original CFG.
In practice, Eq. (15) means that we sample extra negative examples from the data in pdata(·|∅), in
addition to the generated data. The distribution qθ(·|c) corresponds to a class-conditional network
fθ(·|c), similar to common practice [30]. We note that, in our method, CFG is a training-time
behavior by design: the one-step (1-NFE) property is preserved at inference time.

Implementation for Image Generation
We describe our implementation for image generation on ImageNet [10] at resolution 256×256. Full
implementation details are provided in Appendix A.
Tokenizer and Architecture.
By default, we perform generation in latent space [60]. We adopt
the standard SD-VAE tokenizer, which produces a 32×32×4 latent space. Our generator (fθ) has a
DiT-like [57] architecture. Its input is 32×32×4-dim Gaussian noise ϵ, and its output is the generated
latent x of the same dimension. We use a patch size of 2, i.e., like DiT/2. Our model uses adaLN-zero
[57] for processing class-conditioning or other extra conditioning.
CFG conditioning.
We follow [21] and adopt CFG-conditioning. At training time, a CFG scale
α (Eq. (16)) is randomly sampled. Negative samples are prepared based on α (Eq. (15)), and the
network is conditioned on this value. At inference time, α can be freely specified. Details are in A.7.
Batching.
The pseudo-code in Alg. 1 describes a batch of N = Nneg generated samples. In
practice, when class labels are involved, we sample a batch of Nc class labels. For each label, we
perform Alg. 1 independently. Accordingly, the effective batch size is B = Nc×N, which consists
of Nc×N negatives and Nc×Npos positives. We define a “training epoch” based on the number of
generated samples x. In particular, each iteration generates B samples, and one epoch corresponds to
Ndata/B iterations for a dataset of size Ndata.
Feature Extractor.
Our model is trained with drifting loss in a feature space (Sec. 3.4). The feature
extractor ϕ is an image encoder. We mainly consider a ResNet-style [24] encoder, pre-trained by
self-supervised learning, e.g., MoCo [25] and SimCLR [5]. When these pre-trained models operate in
pixel space, we apply the VAE decoder to map our generator’s latent-space output back to pixel space
for feature extraction. Gradients are backpropagated through the feature encoder and VAE decoder.
We also study an MAE [26] pre-trained in latent space (detailed in A.3). For all ResNet-style models,
features are extracted from multiple stages (i.e., multi-scale feature maps). The drifting loss in (13) is
computed per scale and then combined. We discuss details in A.5.
Pixel-space Generation.
While our experiments primarily focus on latent-space generation, our
models support pixel-space generation. In this case, ϵ and x are both 256×256×3. We use a patch
size of 16 (i.e., DiT/16). The feature extractor ϕ is directly on the pixel space.

Experiments
5.1
Toy Experiments
Evolution of the generated distribution.
Figure 3 visualizes a 2D toy case, where q evolves
toward a bimodal distribution p at training time, under three initializations. In this toy example, our
method approximates the target distribution without exhibiting mode collapse. This holds even when
q is initialized in a collapsed single-mode state (bottom). This provides intuition into why our method
is robust to mode collapse: if q collapses onto one mode, other modes of p will attract the samples,
allowing them to continue moving and pushing q to continue evolving.
Evolution of the samples.
Figure 4 shows the training process on two 2D cases. A small MLP
generator is trained. The loss, whose value equals ∥V∥2, decreases as the generated distribution
converges to the target. This is in line with our motivation that reducing the drift and pushing towards
the equilibrium will approximately yield p = q.
5.2
ImageNet Experiments
We evaluate our models on ImageNet 256×256. Ablation studies use a B/2 model on the SD-VAE
latent space, trained for 100 epochs. The drifting loss is in a feature space computed by a latent-MAE
encoder. We report FID [28] on 50K generated images. We analyze the results as follows.
Equilibrium.
Our method relies on the equilibrium of the drifting field, i.e., V=0 when p=q,
which is ensured by anti-symmetry; see Eq. (3). In Table 1, we conduct a destructive study that
intentionally breaks this equilibrium, by breaking the anti-symmetry. The anti-symmetric case (our
ablation default) works well, while other cases fail catastrophically.
Intuitively, for a sample x, we want attraction from p to be canceled by repulsion from q when p and
q match. This equilibrium is not achieved in the destructive cases. Anti-symmetry is sufficient for
this equilibrium, but not necessary: a field that is not anti-symmetric but still vanishes when p=q
performs on par with our default (Appendix B.6).

Discussion and Conclusion
We present Drifting Models, a V-centric framework for one-step generative modeling. At the core of
our model is the idea of modeling the evolution of pushforward distributions during training, governed
by an explicit drifting field V. This allows us to focus on the update rule, i.e., xi+1 = xi + ∆xi,
during the iterative training process. This is in contrast with diffusion-/flow-based models, which
perform the iterative update at inference time. Our method naturally performs one-step inference.
Given that our methodology is substantially different, many open questions remain. For example,
although we show that q = p ⇒V = 0, the converse implication does not generally hold in theory.
While our designed V performs well empirically and we provide some identifiability arguments in
Appendix D.1, a fully general statement of Vp,q ≡0 ⇒p = q remains open.
From a practical standpoint, although our paper presents an effective instantiation of drifting modeling,
many of our design decisions may remain sub-optimal. For example, the design of the drifting field
and its kernels, the feature encoder, and the generator architecture remain open for future exploration.
Our method also has limitations: it currently relies on a feature encoder for high-dimensional data, and
our image-generation experiments are confined to class-conditional ImageNet (see Appendix E.1).
From a broader perspective, our work reframes iterative neural network training as a mechanism
for distribution evolution, in contrast to the differential equations underlying diffusion-/flow-based
models. We hope this perspective inspires future realizations of this mechanism.

A.1
Pseudo-code for Computing Drifting Field V
Alg. 2 provides the pseudo-code for computing V. The computation is based on taking empirical
means in Eq. (11) and (12), which are implemented as softmax over y-sample axis. In practice, we
further normalize over the x-sample axis, also implemented as softmax on the same logit matrix. We
ablate its influence in B.4.
It is worth noting that this implementation preserves the desired property of V. In principle, this
implementation can be viewed as a Monte Carlo estimation of a drifting field:
Vp,q(x) = EB,p,q[ ˜KB(x, y+) ˜KB(x, y−)(y+ −y−)],

def compute_V(x, y_pos, y_neg, T):
# x: [N, D]
# y_pos: [N_pos, D]
# y_neg: [N_neg, D]
# T: temperature
# compute pairwise distance
dist_pos = cdist(x, y_pos) # [N, N_pos]
dist_neg = cdist(x, y_neg) # [N, N_neg]
# ignore self (if y_neg is x)
dist_neg += eye(N) * 1e6
# compute logits
logit_pos = -dist_pos / T
logit_neg = -dist_neg / T
# concat for normalization
logit = cat([logit_pos, logit_neg], dim=1)
# normalize along both dimensions
A_row = logit.softmax(dim=-1)
A_col = logit.softmax(dim=-2)
A = sqrt(A_row * A_col)
# back to [N, N_pos] and [N, N_neg]
A_pos, A_neg = split(A, [N_pos,], dim=1)
# compute the weights
W_pos = A_pos # [N, N_pos]
W_neg = A_neg # [N, N_neg]
W_pos *= A_neg.sum(dim=1,keepdim=True)
W_neg *= A_pos.sum(dim=1,keepdim=True)
drift_pos = W_pos @ y_pos # [N_x, D]
drift_neg = W_neg @ y_neg # [N_x, D]
V = drift_pos - drift_neg
return V

on statistics within B.

A.2
Generator Architecture
Input and output. The input to the generator consists of random noise along with conditioning:
fθ : (ϵ, c, α) 7→x
where ϵ denotes random variables, c is a class label, and α is the CFG strength. ϵ may consist of
both continuous random variables (e.g., Gaussian noise) and discrete ones (e.g., uniformly distributed
integers; see random style embeddings). For latent-space models, the output x ∈R32×32×4 is in the
SD-VAE latent space. For pixel-space models, the output x ∈R256×256×3 is directly an image.
Transformer. We adopt a DiT-style Transformer [57]. Following [78], we use SwiGLU [64],
RoPE [71], RMSNorm [84], and QK-Norm [27]. The input Gaussian noise is patchified into
256=16×16 tokens (patch size 2×2 for latent, 16×16 for pixel). Conditioning (c, α) is processed by
adaLN, as well as by in-context conditioning tokens. The output tokens are unpatchified back to the
target shape.

conditioning [57]. These tokens are formed by summing the projected conditioning vector with
positional embeddings.
Random style embeddings. Our framework allows arbitrary noise distributions beyond Gaussians.
Inspired by StyleGAN [63], we introduce an additional 32 “style tokens”: each of which is a random
index into a codebook of 64 learnable embeddings. These are summed and added to the conditioning
vector. This does not change the sequence length and introduces negligible overhead in terms of

A.3
Implementation of ResNet-style MAE
In addition to standard self-supervised learning models (MoCo [25], SimCLR[5]), we develop a
customized ResNet-style MAE model as the feature encoder for drifting loss.
Overview. Unlike standard MAE [26], which is based on ViT [14], our MAE trains a convolutional
ResNet that provides multi-scale features. For latent-space models, the input and output have
dimension 32×32×4; for pixel-space models, the input and output have dimension 256×256×3.
Our MAE consists of a ResNet-style encoder paired with a deconvolutional decoder in a U-Net-style
[61] encoder-decoder architecture. We only use the ResNet-style encoder for feature extraction when
computing the drifting loss.
MAE Encoder. The encoder follows a classical ResNet [24] design. It maps an input to multi-scale
feature maps (4 scales in ResNet):
Encoder : x 7→{f1, f2, f3, f4}
Here, a feature map fi has dimension Hi×Wi×Ci, with Hi×Wi ∈{322, 162, 82, 42} and Ci ∈
{C, 2C, 4C, 8C} for a base width C.
The architecture follows standard ResNet [24] design, with GroupNorm (GN) [76] used in place
of BatchNorm (BN) [34]. All residual blocks are “basic” blocks (i.e., each consisting of two 3×3
convolutions). Following the standard ResNet-34 [24]: the encoder has a 3×3 convolution (without
downsampling) and 4 stages with [3, 4, 6, 3] blocks; downsampling (stride 2) happens at the first
block of stages 2 to 4.
For latent-space (i.e., latent-MAE), the input of this ResNet is 32×32×4; for pixel-space, the
256×256×3 input is first patchified (by a 8×8 patch) into 32×32×192. The ResNet operates on the
input with H×W = 32×32.
MAE Decoder. The decoder returns to the input shape via deconvolutions and skip connections:
Decoder : {f4, f3, f2, f1} 7→ˆx.
It starts with a 3×3 convolutional block on f4, followed by 4 upsampling blocks. Each upsampling
block performs: bilinear 2×2 upsampling →concatenating with encoder’s skip connection →GN
→two 3×3 convolutions with GN and ReLU. A final 1×1 convolution produces the output channels.
For the pixel-space, the decoder unpatchifies back to the original resolution after the last layer.
Masking. The MAE is trained to reconstruct randomly masked inputs. Unlike the ViT-based
MAE [26], which removes the masked tokens from the sequence, we simply zero out masked patches.
For the input of a shape H×W = 32×32 (in either the latent- or pixel-based case), we mask 2×2
patches by zeroing. Each patch is independently masked with 50% probability.
MAE training. We minimize the ℓ2 reconstruction loss on the masked regions. We use AdamW [51]
with learning rate 4×10−3 and a batch size of 8192. EMA with decay 0.9995 is used. Following [26],
17
we apply random resized crop augmentation to the input (for the latent setting, images are augmented
before being passed through the VAE encoder).
Classification fine-tuning. For our best feature encoder (last row of Table 3), we fine-tune the MAE
model with a linear classifier head. The loss is λLcls + (1 −λ)Lrecon. We fine-tune all parameters in
this MAE for 3k iterations, where λ follows a linear warmup schedule, increasing from 0 to 0.1 over
the first 1k iterations and remaining constant at 0.1 for the rest of the training.
A.4
Other Pretrained Feature Encoders
In addition to our customized MAE, we also evaluate other feature encoders for computing the
drifting loss.
MoCo and SimCLR. We evaluate publicly available self-supervised encoders trained on ImageNet
in pixel space: MoCo [25, 7] SimCLR [5]. We use the ResNet-50 variant. For latent-space generation,
we apply the VAE decoder to map generator outputs from latent space (32×32×4) to pixel space
(256×256×3) before feature extraction. Gradients are backpropagated through both the feature
extractor and the VAE decoder.
MAE with ConvNeXt-V2. In our pixel-space generator, we also investigate ConvNeXt-V2 [75] as
the feature encoder. We note that ConvNeXt-V2 is a self-supervised pre-trained model using the
MAE objective, followed by classification fine-tuning. Like ResNet, ConvNeXt-V2 is a multi-stage
architecture.

A.8
Sample Queue
Our method requires access to randomly sampled real (positive/unconditional) data. This can be
implemented using a specialized data loader. Instead, we adopt a sample queue of cached data, similar
to the queue used in MoCo [25]. This implementation samples data in a statistically similar way to a
specialized data loader. For completeness, we describe our implementation as follows, while noting
that a data loader would be a more principled solution.
For each class label, we keep a queue of size 128; for unconditional samples (used in CFG), we
maintain a separate global queue of size 1000. At each training step, we push the latest 64 new real
(positive/unconditional) samples, alongside their labels, into the corresponding queues; the earliest
ones are dequeued. When sampling, positive samples are drawn from the queue of the corresponding
class, and unconditional samples are drawn from the global queue. We sample without replacement.

B.1
Ablations on Pixel-Space Generation
We provide more ablations on pixel-space generation in Table 8 and 9. Table 8 compares the effect of
the feature encoder on the pixel-space generator. It shows that the choice of feature encoder plays
a more significant role in pixel-space generation quality. A weaker MAE encoder yields an FID of

another feature encoder, ConvNeXt-V2 [75], which is also pre-trained with the MAE objective. This

Table 9 reports the results of training longer and using a larger model. Due to limited time, we train

result in the main paper (Table 5).

Framework. By treating V as the central designable object rather than as the gradient of a fixed
divergence, our framework expresses several extensions as instances of a single zero-drift condition
V = 0, none of which has a natural counterpart in the DMD framework, where the update is tied to
the score difference. (i) Aggregation across temperatures and feature spaces. Multiple drifting fields,
computed at different kernel bandwidths and in different feature encoders, can be combined under
one shared zero-drift condition. (ii) Kernel and V-normalization. Because V is a vector field rather
than a loss, it admits normalizations (kernel-bandwidth-aware and V-magnitude-aware) that are hard
to express purely as divergence minimization. (iii) Modified equilibria. The zero-drift condition
V = 0 can be deliberately shifted to a non-pθ = pdata fixed point (e.g. to encode CFG-style guidance),
while keeping the same training principle.
Practical instantiation. We compute V on clean samples x, often in feature spaces and with
non-Gaussian kernels, whereas DMD uses score differences on noisy samples xt, amortized over
diffusion time t, directly in raw space. Our ImageNet instantiation estimates V directly from data
and model samples, whereas DMD estimates score differences with parametric networks that are
typically initialized from pretrained diffusion teachers and trained alternately with the generator. As
a consequence, our generator can be trained from scratch, without a pretrained diffusion teacher and
without alternating optimization between an auxiliary score network and the generator. Our realized
field additionally supports batch-level distance normalization, V-normalization, and aggregation
across multiple feature spaces, making the update meaningfully different from the DMD construction.
C.2
Relation to Mean Flow/Consistency Methods.
Consistency models and related flow-map methods learn one-/few-step generators by matching long
jumps along diffusion or flow trajectories [68, 66, 3, 20]. Methods such as Consistency Models [68],
Mean Flows [20], Terminal Velocity Matching (TVM) [90], and Self-Evaluation (Self-E) [81] have
a related stop-gradient target structure, which can be abstractly written as regressing to sg(x + ∆).
In these methods, ∆is tied to an underlying flow-matching trajectory: Consistency Models / Mean
Flows / TVM build ∆by perturbing the flow-matching endpoint, while Self-E builds ∆using implicit
classification scores. Our method also trains with a stop-gradient target, but the update is conceptually
different: ∆comes from an explicit drifting field computed directly between data and generated
samples, and does not rely on a prescribed FM trajectory.
25
C.3
Connection to CACR
CACR [88] is part of the broader family of softmax-normalized contrastive representation-learning
objectives. It explicitly decomposes the contrastive objective into an attraction term and a repulsion
term. In CACR, the attraction weights are normalized over the positive set, while the repulsion
weights are normalized over the negative set. This separate positive/negative normalization is similar
in spirit to our mean-shift instantiation in Eqs. (8)–(10), where positives from p and negatives from q
each have their own kernel normalizer.
However, the role of the interaction is different.
CACR uses attraction and repulsion as a
representation-learning objective: the optimized variables are encoder parameters, and the goal
is to shape an embedding space. In our framework, the interaction defines a vector-valued sample-
update field for generated samples. The optimized variables are generator parameters, and the central
condition is not contrastive separability but zero drift of the generated distribution relative to the data
distribution. Thus the attraction–repulsion form is repurposed from representation learning into a
generative matching principle.
C.4
Relation to Kernel-Based Generators
Kernel-based generators, such as GMMN [47, 17], train a generator by minimizing the MMD between
the generated and data distributions. GMMN computes the MMD in pixel space or in autoencoder
codes, and was demonstrated on small datasets such as MNIST; MMD-GAN [44] learns the kernel
adversarially. In our ImageNet setting, an MMD generator in the latent space does not produce
reasonable results (214.6 FID; Table 17, row (a)).
Our method is not derived from a discrepancy between two distributions. It is formulated around
the drifting field V, and it only requires V = 0 when p = q. From this view, MMD relates to one
special case: a drifting field with an unnormalized kernel (Appendix D.2). The designs that our
results rely on follow from this formulation: computing the field in feature spaces at multiple scales
and locations (Sec. 3.4), normalized kernels, drift normalization, and guidance. Table 17 shows the
effect of each design. Rows (b)–(d) of that table are ablations of our method, rather than existing
baselines. In particular, to our knowledge, training a generator from scratch with a kernel-based
objective, computed in the feature space of a frozen encoder at multiple scales, first appears in this
paper.
Pretrained features have also been used to train generators in other ways. GFMN [13] matches the
mean and covariance of the features of a pretrained network, rather than a kernel-based objective.
MMD-DDM [1] fine-tunes a pretrained diffusion model with MMD in a pretrained feature space.
C.5
Relation to Coulomb GAN
Coulomb GAN [72] is a GAN whose discriminator learns a kernel-based potential field; the gen-
erator moves its samples along the gradient of this potential. Its ideal field is Ey∼p[∇xk(x, y)] −
Ey∼q[∇xk(x, y)] with the Plummer kernel k, which resembles the unnormalized-kernel drifting field
of MMD (Eq. (48)) and vanishes when p = q. Our method differs in three respects. (i) Estimation.
Coulomb GAN estimates the field with a discriminator network trained alongside the generator. This
learned estimate has low variance, but it is biased when the discriminator has not been trained to
match the current q. Our field is computed in closed form from the current batch at every step, with
no learned approximation: it has no such bias, at the cost of a higher variance from the finite batch.
(ii) Aggregation. A learned potential requires one network per feature space, whereas our fields from
many features, scales, and temperatures are aggregated at little cost (Appendix A.5 and A.6). (iii)
Form of the field. Coulomb GAN requires the field to be the gradient of a potential. Our formulation
only requires V = 0 when p = q, and thus admits fields that do not correspond to any potential, such
as our mean-shift field with normalized kernels (Eq. (11)).

Applicability to our practical objective. The analysis above considers a single drifting field. Our
practical objective sums the drifting losses of multiple fields: one per feature, in addition to the vanilla
drifting loss in the raw space (Appendix A.5). The analysis applies to this objective as follows.
Equilibrium. Every field vanishes when p = q, so p = q is an equilibrium of the full objective. This
also holds for our mini-batch implementation, which is a Monte Carlo estimate of a drifting field that
satisfies Vp,p = 0 (Appendix A.1).
Identifiability. Conversely, suppose that the drift is zero for the full objective. Then every field
vanishes, including the field in the raw space. If a vanishing raw-space field implies p = q, we obtain
p = q without any assumption on the feature encoder. In other words, the feature-space fields provide
richer training signals (Sec. 3.4), while the guarantee comes from the raw-space field and depends
only on its kernel. Our low-dimensional experiments use the raw space only.
Kernel. What remains is whether a vanishing raw-space field implies p = q, which depends on the
kernel. For the Gaussian kernel, this holds for arbitrary distributions (Proposition D.1). For the
Laplacian kernel used in our experiments, we show it when p and q are both Gaussian distributions

degeneracy assumption, but a proof remains open. Our analysis also does not cover fields aggregated
over multiple temperatures.

E.1
Limitations
Reliance on a feature encoder. On ImageNet, our method relies on a feature encoder (Sec. 5.2). We
attribute this to the variance of the drifting field when it is estimated from a batch of samples: as
with other kernel-based estimates, this variance grows rapidly with the dimension of the space. The
feature encoder maps the data to a space in which a batch of practical size gives a reliable estimate.
This may be a drawback for modalities that lack strong pretrained encoders. There are two potential
mitigations. First, our encoder is pre-trained on the same data with a standard self-supervised method
(MAE; classification fine-tuning is optional, Appendix B.2), and generation quality improves with a
larger encoder and longer pre-training (Table 3). The same pre-training could therefore be run on the
data of a new modality. Second, in low-dimensional settings (robotics control, 2D), drifting operates
without any encoder.
Frozen encoder. Our feature encoder is frozen during generator training. This keeps training
non-adversarial, but, unlike a discriminator, a frozen encoder cannot adapt to the generator. We
discuss two potential failure modes. (i) Exploiting the encoder. A generator could differ from the
data in ways that the encoder does not capture. To reduce this risk, we compute the drifting loss on
multi-scale, multi-location features, in addition to the raw space (Appendix A.5). Our model also
remains competitive under DINO-FD (Table 12), whose backbone is independent of our encoder.
An adaptive encoder could detect such behavior, but would reintroduce adversarial training. (ii)
Sensitivity to the encoder architecture. Our method works with several encoders (SimCLR, MoCo-v2,
and MAE in Table 3; ConvNeXt-V2 in Table 8), while its quality depends on the encoder. Other
architectures, such as ViT, are left for future work.
Scope of experiments. Our image-generation experiments are confined to class-conditional ImageNet
256×256; text-conditional generation and other modalities remain to be explored.

E.3
Broader Impacts
This work studies one-step generative modeling for images and robotic control. Potential positive
impacts include reducing inference-time sampling cost for high-quality generation, making generative-
model evaluation and iteration faster, and enabling lower-latency policy generation in simulated
robotics settings. Because the method targets efficient generation, these benefits may be especially
relevant in settings where repeated multi-step sampling is costly.
At the same time, improvements in fast image generation can also amplify existing risks of synthetic
media, including misinformation, fake profiles, spam, or other deceptive uses. In robotics, lower-
latency policy generation should not be interpreted as sufficient for safe real-world deployment without
task-specific safety validation. To support reproducibility, we release code and pretrained weights for
our class-conditional ImageNet 256×256 models at https://github.com/lambertae/drifting.
These models are comparable in scale and capability to widely available public ImageNet generators.
We do not release new datasets or generated-data assets.
