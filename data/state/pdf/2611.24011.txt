Abstract
Arm-free quadrupeds can perform loco-manipulation,
walking on three legs while manipulating with a front leg,
yet no existing policy for it follows language instructions.
World action models (WAMs) have emerged as effective
language-conditioned manipulation policies, but are pre-
trained for robot arms and sample slowly.
WAM-Policy
turns one WAM into a quadrupedal loco-manipulation pol-
icy via two stages.
The first, Cross-Morphology Pol-
icy Adaptation and Acceleration, adapts the arm-centric
WAM to quadrupeds and distills the transferred policy into
a student with one video and two action steps by on-
policy self-distillation (OPSD). This student, however, is
bounded by its teacher, which only imitates demonstrations.
The second stage, Task-Specific Improvement and Merg-
ing, therefore tunes the teacher by reinforcement learning
(RL) and merges the slow specialists into one few-step stu-
dent by multi-teacher on-policy distillation (M-OPD). On
QLM-Bench, ten simulated tasks with 1,000 demonstrations
synthesized without teleoperation, the transferred policy
reaches 77.0%, and the OPSD student keeps 76.7% while
sampling 14 times faster.
Task-specific RL and M-OPD
lift it to 85.0%. We also deploy the OPSD student on a
real Unitree Go2 Air. WAM-Policy thus achieves a stronger
quadrupedal policy at a fraction of the inference cost. Code
& Website.
1. Introduction
Quadrupeds reach terrain that wheeled and fixed-base
robots cannot, and language is a natural way to instruct
them.
To manipulate, most quadrupeds carry an arm or
a gripper [15, 32, 59], but an arm-free quadruped can
also walk on three legs and use a front leg as the end-
effector [3, 9, 10].
Manipulating with a leg spares the
robot the weight and mechanical complexity of an added
arm [3].
Existing visual planners for such leg manipu-
lation learn from point clouds and cannot map language
instructions to actions end to end [16, 17], and, to our
knowledge, no demonstrations with language instructions
exist for this setting.
Meanwhile, world action models
(WAMs), which jointly predict future observations and ac-
tions, have emerged as effective language-conditioned ma-
nipulation policies [6, 14, 21, 24, 57, 61, 74]. This paper
brings a WAM to arm-free quadrupedal loco-manipulation
(Fig. 1).
This raises two challenges. First, pretrained WAMs are
arm-centric, and their multi-step sampling is slow.
Our
base WAM, LingBot-VA [24], sees head and wrist cameras
and emits a 30-dimensional dual-arm command, whereas
the cameras of a quadruped move with its body and its ma-
nipulating leg also supports it. Its video and action streams
are each sampled over many steps at one network function
evaluation (NFE) per step, so our transferred policy needs
2.226 s for a chunk that covers 0.32 s of robot time, and sam-
arXiv:2611.24011v1  [cs.RO]  18 Nov 2026
pling with one video and two action steps lowers its success.
On the real robot, the body drifts while the whole-body con-
troller [9] balances it during sampling, so actions computed
from the state before sampling no longer fit the state after it.
Second, the distilled policy remains bounded by imitation.
A distilled student is bounded by its teacher, which only
imitates demonstrations and still fails on some hard tasks.
Reinforcement learning with task-specific rewards can go
beyond the demonstrations, but it tunes the slow high-NFE
policy one task at a time, so it yields several slow specialists
that must be merged into one policy and accelerated again.
WAM-Policy turns one WAM into a quadrupedal loco-
manipulation policy via two stages, one for each chal-
lenge: Cross-Morphology Policy Adaptation and Accelera-
tion (Stage 1) and Task-Specific Improvement and Merging
(Stage 2). Both stages end by distilling a high-NFE teacher,
the policy itself in Stage 1 and the task-routed teachers in
Stage 2, on-policy into a 1/2-NFE student (one video and
two action steps), so the final policy is stronger than the
transferred one at a fraction of its inference cost. Our con-
tributions are:
• Cross-morphology
adaptation
and
acceleration.
Stage 1 replaces only the visual and action interfaces
of a WAM, fine-tunes it on quadruped demonstrations,
and accelerates it by a few-step form of on-policy self-
distillation (OPSD) [69], in which the same network with
more sampling steps labels the student’s own samples.
The transferred policy reaches 77.0%, and the OPSD
student keeps 76.7% (69.3% without distillation) while
sampling 14 times faster.
• Task-specific improvement and merging. Stage 2 tunes
specialist teachers by per-task reinforcement learning and
merges them by multi-teacher on-policy distillation (M-
OPD) [40], which routes each context to the teacher of its
task. On pushing a box and opening a door, the specialists
succeed in every episode, against 47% and 53% for the
OPSD teacher, and the M-OPD student reaches 90% and
87%, raising the ten-task success from 76.7% to 85.0%,
above the OPSD teacher (77.0%).
• QLM-Bench, ten simulated loco-manipulation tasks with
1,000 language-annotated demonstrations synthesized
without teleoperation, and evaluation in simulation and
on a real Unitree Go2 Air, where the OPSD student runs
without real-robot data.
2. Related Work
Quadrupedal loco-manipulation.
Quadrupeds that fol-
low language mostly pair a VLA/VLM with a controller for
velocity, gait, or navigation [8, 11, 53, 68], while manip-
ulation systems attach an arm or gripper [15, 32, 39, 59],
sequence skills via an LLM [43], or track foot targets with
leg controllers [3, 9, 10]. Visual leg-manipulation planners
learn from point clouds by reinforcement learning [16] or
imitation (Leg-Manip [17]) without language; WAM-Policy
is a single language-conditioned RGB policy.
World action models.
World action models (WAMs)
jointly predict future scenes and actions using video-
pretrained priors [6, 14, 21, 57, 61, 74], including our
base model LingBot-VA [24]. Prior WAMs target fixed-
base arms, humanoids [26, 27, 45, 65, 70], or an armed
wheeled-legged quadruped [37], and quadruped world mod-
els address locomotion or navigation [4, 22, 58]; none ma-
nipulates with a load-bearing leg. Rather than training a
cross-embodiment policy [12, 42, 54], we adapt one arm-
centric WAM—to our knowledge the first WAM for arm-
free quadrupedal loco-manipulation.
Few-step WAMs and on-policy distillation.
Multi-step
joint denoising makes WAMs strong but slow [21, 24, 61];
unlike methods that skip full future prediction [25, 36,
38, 51, 64, 66, 70], we accelerate the latent plan so that
the teacher can supervise it. Few-step distillation [34, 35,
44, 48, 50, 56, 62, 63] has been applied offline to WAMs
by Flash-WAM [2] and concurrent DirectWAM [72] and
AnyStep-WAM [55], whereas on-policy training supervises
a student on its own samples [1, 5, 20, 47]. Existing on-
policy WAM/VLA distillation either labels a single rollout
batch [60], preserves the step count [33, 40], or runs on-
policy only along the denoising chain [41]. Whereas prior
on-policy self-distillation grants the teacher privileged con-
text [30, 69], our OPSD teacher uses the same closed-loop
context with more sampling steps, distilling the student to
one video and two action steps along its own denoising
chain.
Reinforcement learning and policy merging.
Rein-
forcement learning fine-tunes diffusion and flow mod-
els [31, 71] and policies [46, 67], including VLAs [7, 23],
and lets a policy go beyond its demonstrations; it is best
used to adjust an already competent policy [19]. Tuning one
task at a time, however, yields several specialists; Multi-
Teacher OPD [40] distills several teachers into one student
but keeps their step count, whereas M-OPD merges the spe-
cialists into one few-step student.

3.1. Stage 1: Cross-Morphology Policy Adaptation
and Acceleration
3.1.1. Cross-Morphology Policy Transfer
LingBot-VA is pretrained for arms: its video stream sees
the head and wrist cameras of a manipulator, and its action
stream emits a 30-dimensional dual-arm command [24].
We replace only these two interfaces and keep the rest
of the model, including the frozen VAE and text encoder
(Stage 1.1 in Fig. 2). We call this transfer cross-morphology
rather than cross-embodiment, which in manipulation often
means a different arm or gripper: our target is an arm-free
quadruped that manipulates with one of its legs. Fine-tuning
both streams with the new interfaces on the QLM-Bench
demonstrations (Sec. 4) with flow matching gives the trans-
ferred policy πθ0, one policy for all ten tasks, still with the
high-NFE sampler.
Visual adaptation.
The robot carries two body-mounted
RGB cameras: a wide-angle ego camera at the head looks
forward, and a top-down camera, raised above the trunk and
pitched down, looks at the workspace. The two views are
encoded by the frozen VAE and concatenated spatially, as
LingBot-VA does for its views, so zk and Zk cover both.
Because both cameras move with the body, predicting the
next latent requires modeling how the robot’s own motion
changes both views.
Action adaptation.
We replace the arm action space with
the command interface of RAMBO [9], a whole-body con-
troller C for a quadruped that walks on three legs and manip-
ulates with its front-left leg. A command a ∈R9 specifies
the planar base velocity and yaw rate, the target position of

3.2.1. Task-Specific Reinforcement Learning
The OPSD student is bounded by its teacher, which only im-
itates the demonstrations, so Stage 2.1 tunes the transferred
policy itself by reinforcement learning, one task at a time,
adjusting a policy that imitation has already made compe-
tent rather than learning one from scratch [19]. For a task g,
only a LoRA update ∆g on top of θ0 is trained, which gives
a specialist teacher πg
T with the original high-NFE sampler.
We tune two tasks, pushing the box and opening the door,
with a window-level variant of DiffusionNFT [71].
From states of the teacher’s own closed-loop rollouts, a
slowly updated copy of the policy runs groups of candidate
windows of several chunks, each with one noise pair kept
over the window. Each window receives one reward com-
puted only from the trajectory of the task object or of the
foot relative to it: how far the box advances without de-
viating from a straight push, and how far toward the door
the foot rests on the handle after contact. The Diffusion-
NFT loss pulls the policy toward the candidates rewarded
above the mean of their start and away from those below it
(Sec. B.2).

4. QLM-Bench
QLM-Bench
(Quadrupedal
Loco-Manipulation
Bench-
mark) is a MuJoCo benchmark for language-conditioned
loco-manipulation in which a Unitree Go2 manipulates with
its front-left leg, with the two cameras, the commands, and
the whole-body controller C of Sec. 3.1.1. The benchmark
has ten tasks (Fig. 4): close a fridge door, close an oven
door, turn a valve, pull open a drawer, open a room door,
press a button, lift a basket, kick a ball through a goal, push
a box into a target area, and sweep a block into a target area.
Demonstration synthesis.
For each task, a large multi-
modal model drafts a reference trajectory of commands
from a short description of the task (Fig. 2, bottom left). Af-
ter human review, the reference is retargeted to randomized
scenes and executed by C, and the successful episodes are
kept as demonstrations, with neither teleoperation nor hand-
designed trajectories. This gives 1,000 demonstrations, 100
per task, each recording the two camera streams, the exe-
cuted commands, and a language instruction. We will re-
lease the benchmark and the demonstrations with our code.
5. Experiments
The experiments test that Stage 1 yields a competent
quadruped policy (Sec. 5.2.1) that samples faster than real
time without losing this competence (Sec. 5.2.2). They then
test that Stage 2.1 yields specialist teachers that outperform
the OPSD teacher on their own tasks, that Stage 2.2 merges
them into one 1/2-NFE student that keeps most of their
gains (Sec. 5.3), and that the OPSD student runs on a real
robot (Sec. 5.5). Section 5.4 ablates OPSD and the inputs of
the teacher. Because OPSD does not depend on the embod-
iment, we also apply it to an arm WAM and ablate it there
(Sec. D).
5.1. Setup
Evaluation
protocol.
We
evaluate
on
QLM-Bench
(Sec. 4) with its Standard100 protocol: ten held-out scene
configurations (cases) per task, a time limit of 24 to 60 s per
task, and policy seeds 42, 43, and 44 on each case, for 300
episodes per policy. A policy seed only sets the initial noise
of the policy, while the case fixes the scene, its reset, and
the physics. Simulation waits for the policy at every chunk,
so success rates do not depend on inference speed. Paired
McNemar tests between the policies are in Sec. I.
Policies.
Teacher is the transferred policy πθ0 of Stage 1.1
(Sec. 3.1.1) with its original high-NFE sampler, which we
call the OPSD teacher where it appears next to the specialist
teachers. Student w/o distillation samples the same param-
eters (∆= 0) with Nv/Na = 1/2. Student (Ours), which
we call the OPSD student, is the 1/2-NFE policy θ0 + ∆
that OPSD trains on equal shares of streaming and teacher-

5.2.2. On-Policy Self-Distillation
Speed.
The teacher takes 2.226 s to generate a chunk that
covers 0.32 s of robot time, about seven times slower than
real time (Tab. 1). With 1/2 NFE, the same parameters take
0.156 s, 14 times faster and about half the duration of a
chunk; Sec. G gives the measurement.
Cost of reducing steps.
Without distillation, this speed
lowers success from 77.0% to 69.3% (Tab. 1). The loss
comes from pulling the drawer open and turning the valve,
which together cost 15 points of the ten-task mean, and is
partly offset by opening the door and pressing the button.
Distillation.
OPSD recovers the success of the teacher at
1/2 NFE, with 76.7% against 77.0% (Tab. 1). On the same
cases and seeds, it gains 53 episodes and loses 31 against
the student without distillation, and restores the two tasks
that fewer steps break: pulling the drawer open rises from 0
to 29 of 30 episodes and turning the valve from 14 to 30.
5.3. Stage 2:
Task-Specific Improvement and
Merging
The OPSD student matches but does not exceed its teacher,
so Stage 2.1 tunes the transferred policy on two tasks on
which it often fails, pushing the box (47%) and opening
the door (53%). Each specialist teacher succeeds in all 30
episodes of its own task (Tab. 1); its training curves are in
Fig. S1. Stage 2.2 merges both by M-OPD into one 1/2-
NFE student, which learns the other eight tasks from the
OPSD teacher (Sec. 3.2.2). The M-OPD student pushes the
box in 90% and opens the door in 87% of the episodes,
against 50% and 37% for the OPSD student, so it keeps
most of the gain of its specialist teachers; on each of the
other eight tasks it differs from the OPSD student by at
most one episode, and it still never sweeps the block, which
the router does not send to the open-door specialist (53%;
Sec. F). Through these two tasks alone, its ten-task success
rises to 85.0%, above the OPSD student (76.7%) and the
transferred policy, the OPSD teacher (77.0%), while it sam-
ples a chunk in 0.159 s instead of 2.226 s and replaces two
specialist teachers at about 2.7 s each (Tab. 1).
5.4. Ablation
Training contexts.
We vary the source of the training
contexts through η (Tab. 2): the teacher cache alone already
supervises the student on its own samples, and streaming
contexts add those that it reaches in closed loop (Sec. 3.1.2).
All three students recover the teacher at 1/2 NFE, with
76.3% to 77.0% against 77.0%, so OPSD is not sensitive to
the source of its contexts: the recovery comes from the ob-
jective that the three share, the same-noise regression along
the student’s own sampling chain. We use the equal mixture
by default.
Number of updates.
We train beyond the default 300 up-
dates to test whether the student keeps improving (Tab. 3).
With the teacher cache only, 2,000 updates give 77 suc-
cesses in 100 episodes against 79 after 300, and with the
default mixture, 900 updates give 79 against 82. The dis-
tillation loss on held-out contexts keeps falling slowly over
these updates (Fig. S2), but the closed-loop success does
not follow it, so we keep the default 300 updates.
Distillation objective.
We replace the regression of
OPSD by distribution matching [62, 63], which trains the
student against the difference between the scores of the
teacher and of a second model fitted to the student’s own
outputs, on streaming contexts (Tab. 4, Sec. C). After 900
updates, this student succeeds in 59 of 100 episodes, below
the 68 of the student without distillation, whereas OPSD

Both cameras are mounted on the body,

a proprioceptive state reduces the noise that this shaking
adds to the video stream. We add a 10-dimensional propri-

10 episodes but neither turns the valve nor opens the door,
which the RGB-only teacher does in all 10. Predicting the
state jointly with the video and the action thus lowers suc-
cess, so WAM-Policy observes RGB only.
5.5. Real-Robot Deployment
We deploy the OPSD student on a Unitree Go2 Air with
the two cameras of Sec. 3.1.1: the ego view comes from
the Go2’s built-in camera and the top-down view from a
RealSense D435i. The command interface and the whole-
body controller C are those of the simulation, and the stu-
dent runs off-board, with the VAE, on a workstation with
a single RTX 5070 Ti that connects to the robot by wired
Ethernet. The policy is trained only on simulated data from
QLM-Bench and sees no real-robot data.
We test three tasks with ten trials each, and the OPSD
student succeeds in 7 of 30 trials overall (Tab. 7). Figure 5
compares key frames of real-robot and simulated rollouts
of these tasks, and Fig. 1 (top) shows time-lapse compos-
ites of real-robot rollouts. On this workstation, sampling
takes 0.159 s per chunk, but the closed loop takes 0.66 s
with the encoding of the observations; since this exceeds
the 0.32 s of a chunk, the robot holds its last command be-
tween chunks.
6. Conclusion
We presented WAM-Policy, which turns an arm-centric
world action model into a language-conditioned policy for
quadrupedal loco-manipulation. Stage 1 adapts the WAM to
quadrupeds and distills it into a few-step student by OPSD,
which is bounded by its teacher. Stage 2 therefore tunes the
transferred policy into specialist teachers by reinforcement
learning and merges them by M-OPD into one 1/2-NFE
student that is stronger than the transferred policy (85.0%
against 77.0%) at a fraction of its inference cost. We fur-
ther build QLM-Bench, ten quadrupedal loco-manipulation
tasks with demonstrations synthesized without teleopera-
tion, and evaluate WAM-Policy in simulation and on a real
quadruped.

A. Preliminaries
A.1. World Action Models
A world action model acts in chunks.
Given a lan-
guage instruction ℓ, it conditions on the context Hk =
(ℓ, z≤k, A<k), where zk is the latent of the RGB obser-
vation xk at the start of chunk k under a frozen causal VAE,
and A<k are the action chunks executed so far. We build on
LingBot-VA [24], a transformer with parameters θ that pro-
cesses video and action tokens in two streams. Each stream
has its own input embedding and output projection, and the
two streams interact through joint self-attention at every
layer. The transformer is initialized from the Wan2.2-5B
video generator [52]. The video stream predicts the latent of
the next observation, the latent plan Zk. The action stream
acts as an inverse dynamics model and infers the chunk Ak
from this plan. We write vZ
θ and vA

Training.
Both streams are trained with flow match-
ing [28, 34]. A clean sample y is noised to yσ = (1 −
σ) y + σϵ with ϵ ∼N(0, I), so σ = 1 is pure noise, and the
velocity field regresses ϵ −y. On demonstrations, the video
stream learns to predict the latent zk+1 of the next obser-
vation, and the action stream learns to recover the executed
chunk Ak from this real latent (teacher forcing).

D.3. Ablations
We ablate two choices on LIBERO-Pro2.0 against the stu-
dents of Tab. S2 (Tab. S3).
Repeated collection.
The collected-once variant collects
its contexts once with the initial student, about as many
as the teacher cache, and trains only on this fixed set, as
in the one-time collection of Yang et al. [60].
Like the
streaming-only variant, it uses no teacher cache, so compar-
ing the two tests whether the contexts must follow the stu-
dent as it changes. It draws 48 contexts per update from its
fixed set, whereas the streaming-only variant draws 24 new
ones. It reaches 70.2% and 72.3% under static and runtime
perturbations, nearly the same as the 70.2% and 72.4% of
the streaming-only variant, and the two differ by 0.1 points
overall (Tab. S6). Overall, it is 5.0 points above the student
without distillation, 1.2 points above the teacher-cache-only
variant, and 1.1 points above OPSD (Tab. S6).
Across
OPSD and its three variants, overall success on LIBERO-
Pro2.0 differs by at most 1.2 points: as on QLM-Bench
(Sec. 5.4), the gain comes from the shared distillation ob-
jective, whatever the source of the training contexts.
Teacher.
SFT only (no teacher) fine-tunes a LoRA of
the same rank on the LingBot-VA LIBERO demonstrations
with the official flow-matching recipe, and samples with 1/2
NFE. Despite its larger budget, it reaches only 65.9% and
69.7%, below the distilled students, so the gain comes from
matching the teacher rather than from further fine-tuning.
Overall, it is 2.6 points below the teacher-cache-only vari-
ant and 2.7 points below OPSD, and only 1.2 points above
the student without distillation (Tab. S6).

F. Implementation Details
This section completes the implementation details of
Sec. 5.1 for QLM-Bench.
Fine-tuning.
We fine-tune for 5,000 updates on 80
demonstrations per task and hold out the other 20.
The
re-initialized input embedding and output projection of the
action stream take the 9-dimensional command in place of
the 30-dimensional dual-arm vector of LingBot-VA, which
consists of end-effector poses, joint angles, and gripper
commands. Figure S4 shows the losses of this fine-tuning:
the validation loss falls from 0.209 to 0.053 for the video
stream and from 1.37 to 0.0012 for the action stream, and
most of each decrease, 72% and 83% of the drop in log loss,
occurs within the first 1,000 updates.
Distillation.
∆has rank 256, and the teacher cache holds
about 5,000 contexts. OPSD draws 24 streaming and 24
cached contexts per update (η = 0.5 in Eq. (3)). M-OPD
starts from the EMA weights of the OPSD student after 300
updates and trains for another 300 updates with the settings
of OPSD; the optimizer state, the warm-up, and the EMA
start anew. As in OPSD, streaming environments are bal-
anced over tasks; specialist contexts replace the cache en-
tries of their tasks one for one. Neither the teacher nor the
student uses classifier-free guidance, and every student is
evaluated with EMA weights after 300 updates unless stated
otherwise.
Hyperparameters.
All steps use AdamW with a linear
warm-up to a constant learning rate. Stage 1.1 fine-tunes
the transformer with a learning rate of 1 × 10−5. OPSD
and M-OPD train the LoRA update with a learning rate of
3 × 10−5, with c = 10−3 and λA = 1 in Eq. (2), and keep
an EMA of the trained weights with decay 0.99. Each spe-
cialist of Stage 2.1 trains a LoRA update of rank 32 with
a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, for 15 rounds of 24 updates.
Each camera renders 640 × 360 RGB at 50 Hz; the model
takes every fourth frame, resized to 320 × 180 per view,
and the two views are stacked vertically after the VAE. A
chunk holds one latent, which covers four such frames, and
16 commands at 50 Hz. A command consists of the planar
base velocity (m/s) and yaw rate (rad/s), the target position
of the front-left foot (m), and its target force (N); the veloc-
ity and position dimensions are normalized with their 1st
and 99th percentiles over the training demonstrations. All
samplers use Euler steps on a uniform grid of noise levels
with a flow shift of 5 for the video stream and 1 for the
action stream, and attend to a window of 8 past video and
action units.
Leg-Manip.
The per-task planners of Leg-Manip use 4 to
80 successful demonstrations per task, only 4 for pushing
the box and 5 for sweeping the block; opening the door has
none. Over the nine tasks with demonstrations, its mean
success is 68.6%.
Specialist teachers.
Each specialist of Tab. 1 is applied
on all ten tasks. The push-box specialist is the update af-
ter the last round of Stage 2.1, chosen without looking at
the evaluation; the open-door specialist is the update after
round 4, chosen on 32 held-out episodes of training, not on
Standard100. Outside its tuned task, the push-box special-
ist pulls the drawer open in 21 of 30 episodes against 29
for the OPSD teacher, and the open-door specialist sweeps
the block in 16 of 30 against none. Both differences re-
main when the OPSD teacher is evaluated again on the sec-
ond workstation with batched inference (Sec. G): it pulls
the drawer open in 29 of 30 episodes and never sweeps the
block.
G. Compute Resources
Training time.
Each training run uses one node with eight
GPUs. The fine-tuning of Stage 1.1 takes about 14 hours,
OPSD about 8 hours for the 300 updates of the student in
Tab. 1, the two specialist teachers of Stage 2.1 about 5 and
9 hours, and M-OPD about 5 hours, shorter than OPSD
because the collection of its streaming contexts was opti-
mized.

Timing.
Time in Tab. 1 is measured on an RTX 5070
Ti and counts only the sampling of the video and action

of the teacher and of the 1/2-NFE sampler are measured
on the teacher’s parameters, with the high-NFE sampler (25
video and 50 action steps) and with the 1/2-NFE sampler,
and each is the mean duration of one inference call, which
generates one chunk. The students use the same 1/2-NFE
sampler and are timed with their LoRA updates merged
into the weights, which gives 0.159 s per chunk. The spe-
cialist teachers are timed with the high-NFE sampler and
their LoRA updates applied without merging them into the
weights, which gives 2.746 s and 2.740 s per chunk, against
2.226 s for the OPSD teacher.
Evaluation configurations.
The Teacher, Student w/o
distillation, and OPSD student rows of Tab. 1 and all rows
of the ablations are evaluated under one configuration, on a
workstation with an RTX 5070 Ti and the VAE on the CPU.
Of the 300 episodes of the push-box specialist teacher, 60
run under this configuration, and the other 240 on a sec-
ond workstation with the VAE on the GPU, 220 of them
with batched inference. Of those of the open-door special-
ist teacher, 53 run under this configuration, including all 30
of opening the door, and the others on the second work-
station. Of those of the M-OPD student, 72 run under this
configuration, including all 30 of pushing the box and all
30 of opening the door, and the other 228 on the second
workstation, where one resident model serves several con-
current clients with the VAE on the GPU. The parameters
and the sampler are the same in both configurations, but the
arithmetic is not bit-identical, so outside their tuned tasks
the per-task differences of these three rows from the OPSD
teacher are indicative only.
