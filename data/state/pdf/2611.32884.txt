Lazy Agents Answer Against Their Own Beliefs:
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs Teaches
Them to Act on What They Believe
Richmond Sin Jing Xuan∗, Peilin Feng∗, Wenbin Wang, Soujanya Poria
DeCLaRe Lab, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore
∗Equal contribution
Abstract
AI agents working on long tasks often stop too soon and commit to an answer before they have enough
evidence, a behavior often called laziness. In our evaluations, they frequently commit even while their
stated belief still names several possible answers. At every turn, the agent decides whether to take a
further step or to commit to an answer. An outcome reward at the end of the episode scores only the
final answer. It does not show which turns helped, and it cannot teach the agent when to commit,
because a lucky guess receives the same reward as an answer backed by evidence. During training,
however, the simulator knows the rules linking the hidden answer to the evidence, so it can compute
the exact posterior over the answer at every step and tell whether the evidence is already enough.
We propose Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs (RLVB), which takes this posterior as the
belief supported by the evidence. RLVB rewards the agent for taking further steps while this belief still
allows several answers and for committing once the evidence is sufficient. The reward depends only
on the evidence the agent has gathered and needs no judge, critic or reward model. It never reads the
belief line the agent writes before each action, although the update trains this line together with the
action. We also introduce Pro-Worlds, a benchmark of visual puzzles where the agent views a large
world through a small window and must decide when it has enough visual evidence to commit. We
evaluate three vision-language models on three benchmarks, two of which are never seen in training.
RLVB improves the base model in all nine settings, by 17.1% on average and by 10.4% with expectation
maximization (EM) as the learner. Under the outcome reward, GRPO and PPO fall below the base
model on average. RLVB also outperforms PPO with a process reward from an LLM judge by 9.3%
on average while using 1.6 times fewer GPU-hours. To find where this gain comes from, we group
the answers by the evidence the agent has when it answers. The trained agent answers with sufficient
evidence in 51%, 12% and 74% of the episodes on the three benchmarks, against 8%, 0.4% and 36%
for the base model. For every method, 66 to 92% of the answers given with sufficient evidence are
correct. The accuracy gain of the trained agent therefore comes from answering more often at high
levels of evidence, while at the same level of evidence its answers are no more accurate than those of
the base model. The belief line shows how the trained agent reaches this evidence: it acts on its own
belief. It commits at only 0.1 to 2.2% of the turns at which the evidence is insufficient and its line
still names several answers, against 3 to 68% for the base model with the same line. Over all turns,
its line is not consistently more exact than that of the base model, and each of its steps adds about
as much evidence as a step of the base model. Because the trained agent acts on its belief, we expect
better reading of the evidence to translate into better answers.

Introduction
Agents that work over long horizons are now used in games, embodied settings, and other interactive envi-
ronments (Yao et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a,b). Such an agent must keep working until
it has gathered sufficient evidence to act (Bajcsy, 1988; Das et al., 2018). However, current agents often stop
too early (Laban et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Cuadron et al., 2025) and answer with a guess. This behavior
is called laziness (Ma and Liu, 2025). In the cups task in figure 1, the agent lifts one cup at a time to find a
hidden object. After two lifts, the evidence is not yet sufficient because the object could still be under either
of two cups. The next action should therefore lift another cup instead of guessing. In our experiments, the
base models often guess even after they write that several cups remain possible.

At every turn, an evidence-seeking agent should keep a belief that matches the evidence. It should take
steps aimed at what this belief leaves open. It should commit only when the belief identifies the answer.
Knowing how much one knows and acting on it is called metacognition (Flavell, 1979). Metacognition has
two parts: monitoring what one knows and controlling what one does with it (Nelson and Narens, 1990).
Language models can often recognize what they know (Kadavath et al., 2022). However, the base models
in our evaluations commit too early, even though our prompts tell them to commit only once the evidence
identifies the answer. When they write a belief line before each action, they commit at 3 to 68% of the turns
at which the evidence is insufficient and this belief line still names several answers. They often state that
the answer remains open and commit anyway. These models do not act on what their own belief line states.
A check in the harness that rejects the first answer does not solve this problem, because the harness cannot
read the observations, as section 4.6 shows. Whether the evidence determines the answer depends on what
the agent has observed. Only the agent reads these observations. The agent’s own belief can therefore guide
each decision once the agent learns to act on it. We call an agent reliable when its answers are both correct
and backed by sufficient evidence, because a user who receives an answer usually cannot check how the agent
reached it. Training an agent to make this decision reliably over a long horizon is a central problem in agentic
reinforcement learning (RL).

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is a common way to train large language models on math (Shao et al., 2024;
Zeng et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025) and code (Le et al., 2022; Gehring et al., 2025; Wei et al.,
2025) tasks. A verifier checks the final answer and rewards the model when the answer is correct (Lambert
et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Kimi Team, 2025). Recent work trains multi-turn agents with the same kind
of framework (Zhou et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b,a; Feng et al., 2025).
Over a long horizon, however, a reward on the final answer cannot tell which steps helped and which steps
caused a failure (Minsky, 1961; Sutton and Barto, 2018). A lucky guess is reinforced as fully as an answer
supported by the evidence. A careful search is penalized whenever the episode is lost. Agents also tend to
answer before the evidence supports the final answer (Laban et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026) or fall back on
fixed patterns that ignore the evidence (Wang et al., 2025b, 2026b). As table 2 shows, the base models on
HiddenGames give 64 to 97% of their answers before their evidence is sufficient, whether or not they write a
belief line. RL with the outcome reward even lowers the accuracy of these models on average.
To credit single steps, recent work returns to the PPO framework (Schulman et al., 2017) and rewards every
step with a process reward (Uesato et al., 2022; Lightman et al., 2024). This reward is often given by a
process reward model or an LLM judge (Cui et al., 2026; Khalifa et al., 2026). Yet the value of a step is hard
to estimate, because the critic of PPO is fitted to outcomes that depend on the hidden target. As figure 2(b)
shows, an LLM judge that sees the target may reward a lucky guess and penalize a correct but still uncertain
belief. The critic and the judge also make training more expensive, because the critic is a second model
trained next to the policy and the judge is called at every step.
Agentic RL usually trains in a sandbox simulator (Wang et al., 2025b,a; Feng et al., 2025). This simulator
knows much more than whether the final answer is correct. It knows the hidden target and what each action
will reveal about it. Prior work passes this privileged information to a teacher or a critic (Pinto et al., 2018;
Chen et al., 2019; Choudhury and Sodhi, 2025). In tasks where the agent gathers evidence, this information
lets the simulator compute the exact posterior over the hidden answer after every step. This posterior is the
belief state (Åström, 1965; Kaelbling et al., 1998). The final answer is then no longer the only thing the
simulator can verify. The posterior is the belief supported by the evidence. The simulator can verify it at
every step, just as RLVR verifies the answer at the end. This belief shows whether the agent should take
further steps or answer. This moves us from reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards to Reinforcement
Learning with Verifiable Beliefs (RLVB).
At each turn, we give every method its full history of actions and observations. As a result, no method has
to remember what it has explored. From the exact posterior, the agent learns when to commit and when
to keep working. The reward also scores which step the agent chooses next, but the trained agent does not
choose more informative steps than the base model, as section 4.6 shows. Before each action, the agent states
the answers it still sees as possible, such as Possible: cups 2, 4. This belief line shows whether the agent
thinks it has sufficient evidence. The reward never reads this line. Because the update raises the likelihood
of the whole turn, it still trains the line along with the action. As section 4.6 shows, the trained agent then
learns to act on the line. The turn’s reward compares the agent’s next action to the action a teacher chooses
by looking ahead on the exact posterior (Howard, 1966). The reward can also score the stated belief against
the posterior, but scoring the action is enough, as section 4.4 shows. The reward never pays for a guess. It
depends on the evidence the agent has gathered. It does not depend on which answer happens to be correct
in the episode. In figure 1, cups 2 and 4 are still possible. Lifting another cup gets a reward close to zero.
Answering cup 4 gets about −7, whether or not the object is under cup 4. At each history, the agent samples
several of its own turns and makes the higher-reward turns more likely. The learner uses either a policy
gradient that compares turns within a group, as in GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), or expectation maximization
(EM) (Dempster et al., 1977). RLVB needs no judge, critic or reward model because the posterior is exact.
Our contributions are:
• RLVB. We use the simulator’s privileged information to compute the exact posterior after every step. We
turn this posterior into a process reward. This reward scores each action against a teacher acting on the
exact posterior. The reward never reads the agent’s belief line, but the update still trains the line along
with the action. After training, the agent rarely commits while it has insufficient evidence and its belief
line still names several answers. The policy learns from this reward using policy gradient or EM. It needs
no judge, critic or reward model.
3
• Experimental results. On three vision-language models and three benchmarks, RLVB improves the base
model in all nine settings, by 17.1% on average and by 10.4% with EM as the learner. It outperforms SFT
and RL with the outcome reward in every setting. It also outperforms PPO with a process reward from
an LLM judge on average and uses 1.6 times fewer GPU-hours, or 4 times fewer with EM. Along with
this gain, the share of answers given too early falls from 76–87% to 44–46%.
• Why the training helps. RLVB answers with sufficient evidence in 51%, 12% and 74% of episodes on the
three benchmarks. The base model does so in 8%, 0.4% and 36% of episodes. Using a belief line and the
exact posterior, we find that the trained agent acts on its own belief. It commits at only 0.1 to 2.2% of
the turns with insufficient evidence at which its belief line still names several answers. The base model
with the same line commits on 3 to 68% of those turns. When it commits, the trained agent gives the
answer its line names, just like the base model. Across all turns, its belief line is not consistently more
exact than the base model’s. The single answers it names are often wrong. Its steps add roughly as much
evidence as the base model’s. In metacognitive terms, training mainly links decision control to the agent’s
own monitoring. Monitoring improves only at the states that both agents reach, where the line names a
single answer too early less often. We expect a better reading of the evidence to lead to better answers.
• Pro-Worlds Benchmark. We introduce Pro-Worlds, a new benchmark of visual puzzles. The agent sees a
large world only through a small window. It moves this window and must decide when it has seen enough
to answer. Pro-Worlds tests this decision in visual worlds never seen in training.

Related Work
RL for language and vision-language agents.
RL with outcome rewards has improved the reasoning
of language models (Guo et al., 2025; Kimi Team, 2025; Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). Self-training
methods follow a similar idea, as they sample the model’s own rationales and keep those that reach a correct
answer (Zelikman et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2024). For multi-turn agents, RAGEN (Wang et al., 2025b)
finds that sparse and delayed rewards fail to reinforce the reasoning of each step. GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025)
obtains a finer credit by grouping the steps that share a state. VAGEN (Wang et al., 2025a) adds a turn-level
reward for the agent’s estimate of the world state. These rewards still come from the outcome or from a
learned estimate, while RLVB computes its rewards from the exact posterior.
Process rewards.
Process reward models score each step of a solution instead of only the final an-
swer (Lightman et al., 2024). Most are trained on human or Monte Carlo labels, and their quality depends on
how these labels are made (Zhang et al., 2025). Others learn from outcomes alone (Cui et al., 2026), reason
before they judge (Khalifa et al., 2026), or derive the step reward from a regularized RL objective (Yao et al.,
2026). Closer to our setting, PRInTS (Lee et al., 2026) learns a reward model for long-horizon information
seeking. Our process reward is computed exactly by the environment, and it needs no reward model, no
judge and no step labels.
Belief states and information gathering.
The belief state is the posterior over the hidden state. It
is a sufficient statistic for acting under partial observability (Åström, 1965; Kaelbling et al., 1998). The
value of an evidence-gathering action is the information gained from it (Howard, 1966; Lindley, 1956). BED-
LLM (Choudhury et al., 2026) asks the questions with the highest expected information gain at test time.
Bayesian teaching (Qiu et al., 2026) fine-tunes a model to imitate a Bayesian assistant. CURIO (Wan et al.,
2025) and ECHO (Nath et al., 2026) reward each turn in a multi-turn interaction by how much it improves
a belief over a hidden variable. Unlike these methods, RLVB uses a lookahead on the exact posterior to
value the decision to stop. It also compares several turns at the same history. Agent-BRACE (Singh et al.,
2026) is closest to our work. It trains an agent in text environments to state its belief as claims about the
environment, each with a certainty word. Its policy acts on this belief instead of the history. An LLM judge
grades these claims against the true hidden state rather than the posterior. When two answers are equally
likely, the judge rewards a claim that names only the true answer, just as an outcome reward rewards a lucky
guess. In RLVB, the agent is visual and states the set of answers it still sees as possible. The exact posterior
scores its action and can also score this belief. As a result, the reward is verifiable and needs no judge.

3.1 Problem formulation
Exact posterior.
A simulator knows more than the agent trained in it. Prior work passes this privileged
information to a teacher or a critic as the true state (Pinto et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2019; Choudhury and
Sodhi, 2025), while we use it to compute the exact posterior over what the agent cannot see. Each task has
a hidden target θ ∈Θ drawn from a prior p0, a correct answer y∗(θ) ∈Y, and evidence actions at ∈A with
observations ot ∼p(· | θ, a1:t). In the cups task of figure 2, θ is the cup hiding the red cylinder, an evidence
action lifts a cup, and an answer names a cup. The simulators of our tasks in section 4.1 enumerate Θ, and
before every step t they compute the exact posterior

Agent.
The agent is a vision-language model πϕ that reasons before it acts (Wei et al., 2022; Guo et al.,
2025). At step t, it reads the task instruction and its history ht and writes a turn xt = (zt, βt, ut) ∼πϕ(· | ht).
The history holds the past actions and observations and the last belief line of the agent. The turn consists
of a reasoning zt in the agent’s thinking block, a belief line βt and an action ut ∈A ∪Y. The belief line
names the set Bt of answers still possible in the agent’s view. It is exact if it names exactly the answers
allowed by the evidence, too narrow if it rules out one of them, and too wide if it keeps an answer ruled out

The action term penalizes the two possible mistakes very differently: answering before the evidence is sufficient
receives a very low reward (−7.42 in figure 1), while taking one additional step after the evidence is sufficient

answer too early.

belief line says. This is the main idea of RLVB.

k, a fixed copy πk of the model plays episodes, and at every history h of these episodes we sample N turns

4
Experiments
4.1 Benchmarks
We evaluate on three benchmarks: HiddenGames, Pro-Worlds and RoboQuest (Liu et al., 2026).
In all
benchmarks, the answer is hidden. The agent must take actions to collect evidence and decide when it has
seen enough to answer.
HiddenGames.
It consists of seven tasks from existing agent benchmarks: MineSweeper (Wang et al.,
2026a), MiniGrid KeyCorridor (Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2023), FrozenLake and Sokoban (Wang et al., 2025a),
and three Tabletop tasks rendered in ManiSkill3 (Tao et al., 2025). We train on these tasks and test on 756
of their games with seeds, maps and colors never seen in training.
Pro-Worlds.
To assess transfer to out-of-domain visual tasks, we introduce Pro-Worlds, a benchmark that
tests how visual agents gather, retain and combine evidence before they commit to an answer. In each game,
the agent sees a larger world only through a small window, moves the window to collect evidence, and must
decide when it has seen enough to answer. The window shows one site of the world at a time. The agent can
make at most 35 moves before it names one of eight candidates (ten in Jigsaw) as its answer. We compute
the exact posterior of every game from its game engine. A teacher acting on this posterior solves every game
in 14.7 moves on average. We test on 15 games (Face, Jigsaw, Kaleido, Bird, Fish, Recipe, Turtle, Balloon,
Rocket, Bell, Bee, Candle, Sailboat, Barn and Lantern), with 20 plays of each.
8
RoboQuest.
To test transfer to tasks without an exact posterior, we use the ten mobile manipulation
tasks of RoboQuest (Liu et al., 2026). They take place in simulated kitchens and require search, inspection
and interactive testing, such as finding the odd parcel by weighing. We test on 50 games of each task. The
supplementary material gives further details of all three benchmarks.
4.2 Experiment Setup
Models.
We train three open-source vision-language models: Qwen3-VL-4B-Thinking and Qwen3-VL-8B-
Thinking (Bai et al., 2025), and Qwen3.5-4B (Qwen Team, 2026).
Baselines.
Base is the untrained model and outputs an action only. Base (w/ belief) is the same model
with a belief line before the action. We consider two groups of trained baselines. The first group acts without
a belief and learns from whole episodes. SFT fine-tunes the base model on the teacher’s actions in winning
games. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) train on the outcome reward of each
episode. PPO also learns a critic. The second group learns from a process reward. In PPO (belief + action),
the model writes its belief as short claims with certainty words and then an action. PPO trains it with the
outcome reward of the episode. A learned value function carries this reward back to every turn. At each turn,
an LLM judge (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (DeepSeek-AI, 2026)) rewards the belief by checking its claims against
the newest observation and the true hidden state. Only the belief gets a per-turn reward. The decision to
continue or to answer is rewarded only by the outcome of the episode. Both rewards of this baseline are based
on the truth. The reward of RLVB is instead based on the evidence. The outcome reward pays a correct
answer even when it was a guess. The judge rewards a true claim even when the evidence does not support it.
PPO (belief + action) also samples whole episodes and learns from one outcome per episode. RLVB instead
samples multiple turns at each history and rewards each of them against the exact posterior. All methods
train LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022) for up to 50 updates on the same tasks. At every turn, every method
receives the full history of its actions and observations but not its earlier reasoning.
Metrics.
We test the adapter after the last update of each run. Each test runs three times with different
sampling seeds. We report the mean accuracy over the three runs1. Accuracy is the share of episodes ending
with the correct answer. The supplementary material lists the settings of RLVB.

Stating a belief.

HiddenGames. However, stating a belief does not stop early answers. Base (w/ belief) still commits on 3 to

Comparison with the baselines.
RLVB has the highest accuracy. RLVB or RLVB (EM) is the best method

4.4 Ablation
The sample set and the two terms of the reward.
Table 3 reports runs that change one setting of
RLVB at a time on Qwen3-VL-4B-Thinking. In every run, the agent still writes a belief line before each action
and receives it at the next turn. Only the training changes. RLVB trains with the action term of equation (5).
We first reduce the sample set to 300 histories and use 200 of them. The accuracy then stays about the same
on HiddenGames, at 35.3% against 36.2%, but falls from 28.7% to 23.9% on Pro-Worlds and from 61.2% to
55.5% on RoboQuest. The larger sample set therefore helps on the two benchmarks never seen in training.
Next, we keep the small sample set and add the belief term. The accuracy does not rise with this term. It
stays about the same on HiddenGames, at 35.9% against 35.3%, and falls from 23.9% to 21.7% on Pro-Worlds
and from 55.5% to 45.8% on RoboQuest.
Finally, we keep the belief term and remove the action term. The reward of a turn is then its belief term
alone. We also turn off the acceptance rule of section 3.4, because it uses the teacher’s probability of the
action. The accuracy is then the same as that of Base (w/ belief) on HiddenGames, at 24.6% against 24.5%.
It is lower on Pro-Worlds, at 14.6% against 18.2%, and on RoboQuest, at 19.7% against 34.0%. The gain of
RLVB therefore comes from the action term. A reward on the belief line alone does not teach the agent when
to continue and when to answer.
Adding a process reward.
PPO (action-only) and PPO (belief + action) use the same learner and the
same outcome reward. Only PPO (belief + action) adds a process reward from an LLM judge that scores
the belief at every turn. In table 1, this process reward raises accuracy in eight of the nine settings, by 13.0%
on average. The two runs also differ in the turn format, because only PPO (belief + action) states a belief.

Fewer premature answers.
Because the reward of RLVB comes from the exact posterior, it penalizes a
guess even when the guess is right and rewards further steps while the evidence is not enough. We pool
HiddenGames, Pro-Worlds and RoboQuest. Each range below spans the three models. Before the evidence
is enough, Base answers at 11 to 20% of the turns and RLVB at only 3%. PPO (belief + action) also writes
a belief, but its reward checks the belief against the true answer instead of the evidence. This reward can
therefore still pay a lucky guess. PPO (belief + action) gives 56 to 83% of its answers too early. In table 6,
the share of premature answers falls from 76–87% for Base to 44–46% for RLVB. Section 4.6 examines where
the gain comes from.
How much evidence the agents gather.
Figure 4 shows how the evidence grows with the number of
steps. On HiddenGames and Pro-Worlds, it plots the exact posterior of the correct answer after each step.
RoboQuest has no posterior. On RoboQuest, the figure plots the share of episodes where the feedback meets
every task requirement. Each curve rises while that method’s agents still take steps. It flattens once they
stop. As a result, the curves separate where the methods stop. On Pro-Worlds, the curves overlap over the
first steps. This happens because every method takes steps for a while, and each step gives about the same
evidence for every method. On HiddenGames, RLVB’s curve closely follows the dash-dot line of random steps.
On Pro-Worlds, the curves for all methods lie above it by about the same amount. RLVB takes the most
steps for all three models on all three benchmarks. Its evidence keeps growing after the other methods stop.
At the end of the episodes, RLVB has more evidence than Base on all three benchmarks. On HiddenGames,
the posterior of the correct answer is 59 to 64% for RLVB, compared to 22 to 37% for Base. On Pro-Worlds,
it is 38 to 42% for RLVB, compared to 17 to 25% for Base. On RoboQuest, the feedback meets every task
requirement in 68 to 77% of RLVB’s episodes, compared to 31 to 45% for Base (table 23). Section 4.6 shows
that RLVB takes these extra steps because it does not commit while its belief line still names several answers.

4.6 Why RLVB works
Summary. RLVB gains because it answers with more evidence. It gets this evidence by acting on its own
belief line. When the evidence is insufficient and its belief line still names several answers, RLVB commits
at 0.1 to 2.2% of turns, against 3 to 68% for Base (w/ belief). As a result, its answers come mostly after
its belief line names a single answer (RQ3). The belief line itself is not consistently more accurate. Over
all turns, compared to Base (w/ belief), it is exact more often in some settings and less often in others,
and the single answers it names are often wrong (RQ1). RLVB’s steps add about as much evidence as
the base model’s steps (RQ2).
There are three possible explanations for RLVB’s gain. The trained agent could read its evidence better
(RQ1), take steps that bring more evidence (RQ2), or act on what its belief line says (RQ3). In terms of
metacognition, RQ1 and RQ2 look at monitoring the evidence. RQ3 looks at whether controlling the decision
follows this monitoring. At every turn, the exact posterior measures how strongly the evidence gathered so
far settles the answer. This lets us test each explanation.
We call each evidence-gathering action a step. We call the evidence sufficient when the exact posterior of the
top answer is at least 0.95. RoboQuest has no posterior, so there the evidence is sufficient once all evidence
requirements of the task are met. We define the evidence at the answer as the top answer’s posterior on the
turn when the agent commits. On RoboQuest, it is the share of requirements met on that turn. A belief line
is exact when it names exactly the answers that are still possible. Base is the base model without a belief
line. Base (w/ belief) is the same model with a belief line. In this section, HiddenGames means its five tasks
with an answer, and RoboQuest means its eight tasks with countable requirements. The accuracies therefore
differ from those in table 1. Section K gives details of these measures and further tests.
The evidence at the answer.
Figure 5 groups each method’s episodes by the evidence at the answer.
HiddenGames and Pro-Worlds use four levels because the posterior takes only a few values there. RoboQuest
uses six levels. Across all methods, answers given with sufficient evidence are correct in 66 to 92% of cases,
against 0 to 13% at the lowest level. The base model gives most of its answers at low evidence levels. It
answers with a posterior below 40% in 79% of its HiddenGames episodes and 96% of its Pro-Worlds episodes.
In contrast, RLVB commits at much higher evidence levels. It answers with sufficient evidence in 51% of
HiddenGames episodes, 12% on Pro-Worlds and 74% on RoboQuest, against 8%, 0.4% and 36% for Base and
20%, 3% and 57% for PPO (belief + action). As a result, the mean evidence at the answer is higher for RLVB
than for Base by 47.4% in HiddenGames (95% interval from 45.4 to 49.4%), 20.6% on Pro-Worlds (from
19.4 to 21.8%), and 34.8% on RoboQuest (from 32.5 to 37.0%). Across individual models, this difference is
between 18 and 51%, as figure 10 shows.

RQ2: Does RLVB judge what further steps can bring?
If RLVB had learned to anticipate upcoming
evidence, it would choose steps that add more evidence than those of Base and recognize when its own steps
stop adding evidence. We measure the value of a step by how much it raises the exact posterior of the correct
answer, or by the share of requirements met on RoboQuest. By this measure, steps from RLVB add about
as much evidence as those from Base. From the states reached by Base, PPO (belief + action), and RLVB,
the first step raises the posterior by 15.6, 14.9, and 14.8% in HiddenGames and by 1.5, 1.9, and 1.5% on
Pro-Worlds, while meeting 19.1, 19.3, and 19.8% of the requirements on RoboQuest, as section K shows.
Later steps have similar value across every window of steps from the start of the game, as figure 7 shows.
Across states reached by both Base and RLVB, about 20% of each agent’s actions meet one more requirement
on RoboQuest, and 4–6% of their moves add evidence on Pro-Worlds, as table 9 shows. Only in MiniGrid
and MineSweeper does the first step of RLVB add less evidence, raising the posterior by 0.14%, against 1.24%
for Base.
RLVB also does not choose better actions. Measured against the actions ranked best by the posterior teacher,
RLVB and Base choose a best action about equally often, in 40 versus 37% of HiddenGames states and 53
versus 52% of Pro-Worlds states. A better belief line does not lead to better steps either. From the same
state and with an exact belief line, a step from RLVB raises the posterior of the correct answer by 0.9% more
than a step from Base (w/ belief) on the Tabletop tasks, where a step adds about 30%, and by 0.02% less on
Pro-Worlds, where a step adds about 0.9%. The 95% intervals for both differences include zero. The same
holds when the posterior teacher continues playing to the end after each step. With an exact belief line, a
step from RLVB leaves the teacher with as many remaining steps as one from Base (w/ belief) on Pro-Worlds
and in the Tabletop tasks, and 0.53 more in MiniGrid and MineSweeper, as section K shows.
RLVB also does not recognize when its own steps stop adding evidence. In MineSweeper, the posterior teacher
reaches sufficient evidence in 83% of scenes, as table 32 shows. RLVB’s steps rarely reach sufficient evidence,
and the agent answers in only 3 to 16% of episodes. On Qwen3-VL-4B, 44 of its 81 MineSweeper episodes
without an answer add no evidence across their last ten turns, as table 37 shows.

A likely reason for this behavior lies in the reward. The reward never reads the belief line, and it scores every
action against the exact posterior. A commit gets a low reward when the evidence leaves several answers

open, even if the guess happens to be correct. In contrast, an outcome reward gives a lucky guess the full
reward. At these states, the belief line usually names several answers. The update raises the likelihood of the
whole turn, including the line. As a result, the agent learns to keep working when its own line names several
answers. Taking one more step after the evidence is sufficient costs only that step’s small time discount in
the action term, as section 3.3 describes. This makes the extra check cheap. Adding a reward on the belief
line itself does not raise accuracy, as table 5 shows.
The on-policy variant of RLVB doubts its belief line more and keeps exploring. On Qwen3-VL-4B, after it
takes a step while its belief line names a single answer, its next line keeps that answer in 61% of cases, against
74% for RLVB. It commits on the next turn in 71%, against 88%. Of these steps, 20% are rejected as invalid,
against 5%. On the Tabletop tasks, its belief line still names several answers on 62% of turns with sufficient
evidence, against 46% for RLVB. It commits at only 9% of these turns, against 37% for RLVB. It ends half
of its HiddenGames episodes without an answer, against 18% for RLVB. It is less accurate than RLVB on all
three benchmarks, as section L shows.
Answer to RQ3. Yes. The base model often commits while its own belief line still names several answers,
and RLVB rarely does. This accounts for 53 to 83% of the drop in early commits on the Tabletop tasks
and nearly all of it on Pro-Worlds. It is the source of the accuracy gain.
A check in the harness.
Such a check cannot replace an agent that acts on its own belief. Our harness
does not accept an episode’s first answer. It asks the agent once to check whether its evidence identifies the
answer. The agent can then repeat its answer, change it, or keep working. This check raises Base’s accuracy
by 6.1 to 11.3% on RoboQuest. On HiddenGames and Pro-Worlds, it changes accuracy by at most 1.9%, as
table 36 shows. On the Tabletop tasks, Base keeps working after the check in only 2 to 17% of the checked
episodes, as section K shows. Otherwise, it repeats its answer. In these tasks, Base (w/ belief) has a belief
line that already names a single answer in 38 to 72% of its early commits. Because the harness does not read
the observations, it cannot tell the agent when its evidence is sufficient. RLVB writes its belief line from its
own observations, and that line guides most of its decisions to commit.
Limits.
An agent that keeps working gains only where its steps bring evidence. Among the HiddenGames
tasks, RLVB gains most on the Tabletop tasks, where every valid step adds evidence, with 51% on lamps and
35% on cups, as section F shows. On RoboQuest, where about one action in five meets a requirement, the
gain on the three models is 14 to 33%, and on Pro-Worlds, where about one move in twenty adds evidence,
the gain is 12 to 15%. In MiniGrid and MineSweeper, a step raises the posterior of the correct answer by
about 1% or less, and RLVB shows no gain on these tasks. As RQ2 shows, RLVB has not learned to commit
to its best answer in MineSweeper when its steps stop adding evidence or when no step remains. Across the
three models, RLVB ends 19% of its HiddenGames episodes without an answer, compared with 7% for Base,
almost all of them in MineSweeper.
Reading the evidence is the next bottleneck. RLVB answers with sufficient evidence far more often than
the base model. Even so, only 65.7% of these answers are correct in HiddenGames, 73.7% in Pro-Worlds,
and 89.8% in RoboQuest. Most of these wrong answers come from the two Qwen3-VL models. In the
Tabletop tasks, RLVB on these two models is correct on 55.6% and 52.3% of its answers with sufficient
evidence, against 86.6% for RLVB on Qwen3.5-4B. On lamps, Base never reaches sufficient evidence on
these two models. RLVB reaches sufficient evidence in 295 episodes per model and answers correctly in
48% and 33% of them. Because RLVB acts on its belief line, we expect a better reading of the observations
to translate into better answers. However, its reward scores only the action. It trains how the agent
reads these observations only through the actions that follow.
5
Limitations
RLVB needs a task with known rules. The environment can compute the exact posterior only when it knows
the prior over the hidden answer and the likelihood of each observation. Many puzzles, games, and simulated
22
household tasks meet this condition. For tasks with unknown or open-ended rules, the posterior must be
estimated.
Very large action spaces need a shallower or sampled lookahead.
The cost of the teacher’s
lookahead grows with the number of actions and observations. Finally, a belief line names a set of possible
answers. This set states the posterior exactly only when every possible answer is equally likely. The belief
line cannot express finer degrees of certainty. With training, wrong answers given with enough evidence also
rise from 2–4% to 10–15% of the episodes, mostly in the Tabletop tasks (tables 6 and 30). On these tasks,
the agent misreads observations that already settle the answer. It then gives the wrong answer that its belief
line names (section 4.6). The action term also scores which step the agent takes, but the trained agent does
not choose more informative steps. On Pro-Worlds, a step by the posterior teacher raises the posterior by
5.9%, compared to 0.7 to 1.3% for a step by any method. The trained agents also stay far below the posterior
teacher (for example 32.4% against 100% on Pro-Worlds). The agents have not yet learned much of what
the exact posterior allows.
6
Conclusion
We showed that a simulator can compute the exact posterior of an evidence-seeking task from its privileged
information. This posterior gives a process reward at every turn. The posterior scores the agent’s choice
between taking more steps and answering. It can also score the agent’s stated belief. RLVB learns from
this reward with a policy gradient, and RLVB (EM) learns from it with EM. Across three vision-language
models and three benchmarks, both RLVB learners improve the base model in every setting. With the same
learner, the posterior process reward beats the outcome reward by 18.1% on average. On each of the three
benchmarks, RLVB also beats a process reward from an LLM judge on average. The trained agent answers
with sufficient evidence in 51%, 12%, and 74% of episodes, compared to 8%, 0.4%, and 36% for the base
model. It does this by acting on its own belief. With the same belief line, it commits on only 0.1 to 2.2%
of turns with insufficient evidence where this line still names several answers, compared to 3 to 68% for the
base model. Across all turns, its belief line is not consistently more exact than the base model’s. Each of its
steps adds about as much evidence as a step from the base model. A check in the harness does not raise the
base model’s accuracy on HiddenGames and Pro-Worlds because the harness cannot read the observations.
We expect a better reading of the evidence to carry into the answers when an agent acts on its belief. RLVB’s
steps are not yet more informative than the base model’s. An agent with sufficient evidence can still misread
its observations. We want to extend RLVB to tasks where the posterior must be learned, to improve the choice
of the next step, and to train the agent to read its observations correctly once its evidence is sufficient.

The posterior teacher in detail
This section describes the posterior teacher of section 3.2 in detail.
Boundary values.
In equation (3), V (0)(b) = log c(t) + log ˆρ+(b). Here ˆρ+ is an upper confidence bound
on ˆρ that adds one standard deviation, as given in equation (13). This bound is high for belief classes with
few measured answers. The lookahead thus gives a high value to evidence that leads to these classes. The
time of ba,o is t + 1. A belief at the step limit t = T and a belief that identifies the answer both have the
value log c(t) + log ˆρ(b). For an answer with b(y) = 0, we floor the accuracy at 10−9. Such an answer has the
value log c(t) + log 10−9. An outcome that ends the episode without an answer, such as a revealed mine, has
the value of knowing nothing, log c(t) + log(1/|Θ|). A lookahead of depth 2 evaluates every pair of evidence
actions with their observations. Its cost thus grows as (|A| |O|)2 Bayes updates per history.
Task lookaheads.
We use equation (3) with depth 2 for the Tabletop tasks. The other tasks have many
evidence actions. Each of them computes the same kind of value, log c(t) + log ˆρ, with its own lookahead.
MiniGrid, FrozenLake and Sokoban value each move by the best view reached on a shortest plan starting with
this move. MineSweeper values each reveal by the posterior after it. The cost above holds for the depth-2
recursion only.
Why the logarithm.
The average of log ˆρ counts progress at every step. Each halving of the possible
answers adds log 2, whether it comes early or late. For a uniform posterior, this average is the expected
information gain on the answer. When the answers are many, the average of ˆρ itself stays close to 0 until the
answer is almost decided. For example, on a 6 × 6 MineSweeper board with 6 mines and 1,947,792 mine sets,
the average of ˆρ cannot rank the actions. It also gives almost no cost to a risk that ends the episode.

teacher scores each new line against bt, which comes only from the evidence. A line that copies an earlier

Fitting the turns.
For both learners, three rules decide how the trainer fits a turn. For the policy gradient,
the first rule applies only to turns with positive weights. A turn with a negative weight keeps all of its tokens.
First, with the belief term, a fitted turn trains its whole text only when its belief line is verified. The belief
line must satisfy S(β; b) ≥S(β∗; b) −0.1. Otherwise, its belief-line tokens are not counted and its reasoning
gets weight 0. The turn then trains its accepted action alone, with the reasoning and the line kept as context.
With the action term alone, every fitted turn trains its whole text. Second, a turn has two blocks of tokens,
the reasoning and the decision. Each block takes its own gradient step with its own clip of the gradient norm.
The objective and its minimizer stay the same. The split keeps the long and noisy gradient of the reasoning
from setting the pace of the short decision block. Third, the likelihood πϕ uses the sampler of the policy.
For the reasoning, it uses the sampling temperature, the top-p and top-k cuts and the presence penalty. For
the decision, it uses the same temperature and presence penalty with no cut. Under a cut, the likelihood of
a token is its tempered probability renormalized over the kept tokens. The sampled token always counts as
kept. The fixed point is thus the policy used by the agent.

C.4 Rules against steps without evidence
Why we tested the rules.
With phases of 5 updates, the agent of Qwen3.5-4B learned a second way
to delay its answer. It took steps that bring no evidence, such as turning on the spot in MiniGrid. The
action term lets such a step pass. In the teacher’s score, a step that brings no evidence costs only its time
discount, which is 0.2 nats in most tasks and 0.5 nats in MineSweeper. The acceptance rule accepts every
turn whose score is within log 20 = 3.0 nats of the teacher’s best action. A step without evidence is therefore
still accepted. The measured answer accuracy ˆρ of equation (12) can add to this. Where the agent’s answers
are often wrong, ˆρ is low. Answering then has a low value, and the teacher itself asks for more steps.
The three rules.
All three rules change the reward of the action term, and all three are computed from
the exact posterior.
1. Exact answer accuracy. The estimate ˆφ of equation (12) stays at its prior mean of 1, so ˆρ(b) = bmax. This
is the accuracy of an agent that always reads its evidence correctly. The teacher’s choice between a step
and an answer then depends on the evidence alone and not on the agent’s own errors.
2. A cap on steps without evidence. An illegal action gets a teacher score of at most the best action’s score
minus 3.5 nats. So does a step that brings no expected evidence and is not one of the teacher’s best steps.
The acceptance rule therefore rejects both. The teacher’s own moves in the training games follow the
same capped scores.
3. The teacher’s turn as a positive example. With the cap, more histories have no accepted turn of the agent.
At such a history, the update can only lower the probability of the agent’s turns. We then add the teacher’s
own belief line and action to the turns of this history as a positive example.

5 updates and 25 updates, and we tested it on the same three test seeds. Table 12 compares the two runs.

E
Benchmarks in detail
HiddenGames.
HiddenGames is a mixture of seven tasks from different agent benchmarks. In each task,
the agent must find a hidden target from what it observes. MineSweeper (Wang et al., 2026a) hides the
mines of a small board. In MiniGrid KeyCorridor (Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2023), the agent must find the
room that holds the key. In FrozenLake (Wang et al., 2025a), we hide the goal and the distant holes. In
Sokoban (Wang et al., 2025a), we hide the target square. The agent must thus explore before it can plan.
The three Tabletop tasks are rendered scenes of a table (Tao et al., 2025). The agent must find which button
lights which lamp, which cup hides a named object as in figure 1, or which block slides farther. It sees only
two camera images after each action. The environment of every task knows the prior and the likelihood of
each observation. The teacher of section 3.2 can thus compute the exact posterior. We train and test on all
seven tasks. The test split consists of 756 games whose seeds, maps and Tabletop colors are never seen in
training.
Pro-Worlds.
We introduce Pro-Worlds, a benchmark of how visual agents gather, retain and combine
evidence before they commit to an answer.
Each game hides one of a few answers, for example which
emotion a face shows or which fish a picture shows. The game draws its world as a grid of sites, each a tile
of 16 × 16 color cells. The agent sees one site at a time through a window, rendered as a 256 × 256 picture.
At each turn, the agent either moves the window by one site with ACTION: MOVE UP, DOWN, LEFT or
RIGHT, or submits an answer with ACTION: SUBMIT. A move at the edge keeps the window in place but
still counts. A play allows at most 35 moves. It ends with a submission, with a move when no moves are left,
or after 43 turns. The prompt gives the rules of the game, the list of answers and every picture seen so far
with its position. We place the start where the first view never decides the answer.
The engine knows every possible world of a game and can thus compute the exact posterior. This posterior
holds the worlds consistent with every site seen so far, with the same prior weight for each answer. As in the
other benchmarks, a submission is premature when the posterior of the top answer is below 0.95. Table 14
lists the 15 test games. We test on 20 plays of each game, 300 plays in all. Eight of the games are the
training games of the benchmark, drawn at random from the 15. However, no model in this paper trains on
Pro-Worlds.
RoboQuest.
RoboQuest (Liu et al., 2026) consists of ten mobile manipulation tasks built around search,
inspection and interactive testing. In each task, such as finding the odd parcel by weighing, the agent must
gather information through physical interaction before it decides to finish. Liu et al. report that agents often
stop exploring too early (Liu et al., 2026). Unlike the other two benchmarks, RoboQuest runs in a physics
simulator of real kitchens. Its possible states cannot be listed, and there is no exact posterior. Many of its
answers are also actions in the scene rather than a choice among listed options. The belief lines of all agents
are therefore free text, such as Possible: blue water bottle may be in drawer 2 or another compartment.
We use RoboQuest to test whether what an agent learns from exact posteriors transfers to a task without one.
We use the text version of its decision-level setting, where the agent chooses high-level actions and scripted
helpers carry them out. We test on 50 games of each task, 500 games in all.

The PPO (belief + action) baseline
PPO (belief + action) states a belief before each action. An LLM judge rewards this belief at every turn. A
turn consists of the reasoning, a belief state and an action. The belief state is a list of bullet points inside
<belief_state> tags, each with one word of certainty such as confirmed, probable or ruled out. The action
is inside <action> tags. The turn receives the same input as the other methods, with the rules of the task,
the actions and observations so far and the current observation. It also receives the belief state of the previous
turn.
The belief reward of a turn is Rb = f (st + sc + d + o)/4. Here f scores the format of the belief state by the
number of bullets, a certainty word in each bullet, no repeats and the length. The judge score st measures
whether the new belief state takes in the newest observation. The judge score sc measures whether its claims
are true. d is the entropy of the certainty words, and o is the outcome reward. To score sc, the judge checks
the claims against a text description of the true hidden state. Only the judge sees this description. The judge
is DeepSeek V4.1 Flash at temperature 0, without thinking. A failed judge call receives the batch mean of
its term.
The tokens of the belief state are trained on Rb, normalized over the belief states of the batch. The reasoning
and action tokens are trained with PPO on the outcome reward 0.999t · ⊮[correct]. PPO uses a learned value
function and GAE (Schulman et al., 2016) with γ = λ = 1. Training uses LoRA with rank 16 and alpha 32,
a learning rate of 10−5 and 50 updates of 16 episodes each, with no warm start. At test time, the prompt
keeps the rules of the belief state but drops the list of task-specific fields and the format example used in
training.

Why RLVB works: details
This section provides the details and additional tests underlying section 4.6. Unless a table or figure states
otherwise, all numbers are for Qwen3-VL-4B.
Measures.
A step refers to a move on Pro-Worlds, a lift or press in the Tabletop tasks, and an action such
as opening a drawer or weighing two parcels on RoboQuest. A step adds evidence when it changes the exact
posterior or, on RoboQuest, when it meets a new evidence requirement of the task, such as the agent now
seeing a target object. On HiddenGames, we use the five tasks with an answer: the three Tabletop tasks,
MiniGrid, and MineSweeper. On RoboQuest, requirements can be counted in eight of the ten tasks, so we
omit Stamps and Puzzle Box from these measures. The chance of a correct answer is the exact posterior of
the top answer, representing the accuracy of an agent that reads its evidence without error.
Reading the belief lines on RoboQuest.
On RoboQuest, belief lines are free text. The task has no
posterior. We read every belief line in the test episodes of Base (w/ belief) and RLVB on the 361 of 400
instances that at least one method solves. These are 56,556 lines from the three models and three test seeds.
Language-model readers judge each line against the trace of an episode that solved the same instance. The
readers do not see which agent wrote the line. They record whether the line names a single answer or leaves
part of the answer open. They also record whether it keeps the true answer. When the agent submits at that
turn, they record whether the submission gives the answer that the line names. A second reader labels 2,595
of these lines. It agrees on whether the line names a single answer for 2,539 of them, on whether it keeps the
true answer for 2,514, and on whether the submission gives the named answer for 2,562.

Actions against the best action.
To test whether training alters action selection, we compare each
chosen action against the best actions available in that state, as ranked by the posterior teacher. We evaluate
on Pro-Worlds and HiddenGames excluding the Tabletop tasks, where all valid actions have equal value
under the teacher. Because RoboQuest has no teacher, we instead measure the share of actions that meet
one more requirement. A random action is a best action in 33% of HiddenGames states and 44% of Pro-
Worlds states. Every method selects a best action more often than a random action, exceeding random by 2
to 7% in HiddenGames and by 8 to 10% on Pro-Worlds, with all differences significant.
RLVB and Base select a best action at similar rates. In HiddenGames, the share is 40% for RLVB and 37% for
Base, a small difference of 2.5% (95% interval from 1.2 to 3.9%). On Pro-Worlds, the corresponding shares
are 53% and 52%, with an interval for the difference that includes zero (from −0.5 to +1.5%). Across the
HiddenGames tasks, the difference runs in both directions: RLVB exceeds Base in FrozenLake (43% versus
39%), falls below Base in MineSweeper (39% versus 47%), and matches Base in MiniGrid and Sokoban.
As a second measure, we evaluate the value of the chosen action relative to the best action. Although the
share of best moves does not differ on Pro-Worlds, the value of the chosen move is significantly lower for
RLVB by 3.0% (62.8% of the best move’s value compared to 65.8% for Base). In MiniGrid, the chosen action
falls 0.21 to 0.23 nats below the best action across all methods, while in MineSweeper it falls 0.99 nats below
for RLVB compared to 0.55 for Base and 1.45 for PPO (belief + action). Both measures show only small
differences in either direction, so training has little effect on action choice.

A forced answer at the last turn.
For RoboQuest episodes that end without an answer, we test a
simple rule: once the agent has used all its steps, we begin its reply as a submit so that the model only writes
the answer. With this rule, the on-policy variant solves 21 of its 98 episodes without an answer, raising its
accuracy on RoboQuest from 58.8% to 63.0%. The rule has almost no effect on Base, which ends at most 1
of 500 episodes without an answer.
Do the further steps of the on-policy variant bring evidence?
The on-policy variant ends many
more episodes without an answer than RLVB. Table 37 tests whether its final turns in these episodes still add
evidence, counting every turn (including rejected actions) as adding evidence whenever the exact posterior
changes or, on RoboQuest, a new requirement is met. In MiniGrid, none of the last ten turns adds evidence
in any of its 90 episodes without an answer; 89 of these episodes end after 64 turns at the starting posterior
of one third. In MineSweeper, the last ten turns add no evidence in 94 of its 96 such episodes, compared to

episode of the on-policy variant without an answer already holds sufficient evidence (63 of 63) for 3.9 turns

2.8% and 1.2% of episodes without an answer.

The first difference is cost: RLVB takes 6.4 hours in total, with its two sampling passes taking 47 and 74
minutes and each of its 50 updates then taking about 5 minutes. We stopped the on-policy variant after 46
updates, which took 20.8 hours. Because every update of the variant includes sampling, updates grow slower
as training proceeds, averaging 20 minutes over the first ten updates and 39 minutes over the last ten. As
the middle panel of figure 13 shows, the games sampled by the on-policy variant lengthen from about 5 turns
in the first ten updates to about 20 in the last ten because the model answers less and less often. In contrast,
the games sampled by RLVB have 5 turns in the first phase and 7.5 in the second.
The second difference is what the model learns. The on-policy variant’s longer training does not pay off. On
the same test episodes, the variant falls behind RLVB on all three benchmarks (figure 13). The gap is 4.9% on
HiddenGames, 0.6% on Pro-Worlds, and 1.6% on RoboQuest. The variant reaches sufficient evidence as often
as RLVB, but it then often gives no answer (table 23). Its extra steps in MiniGrid and MineSweeper bring
almost no evidence (table 37). A likely reason is the reward. It gives a very low value to an early answer, and
it charges very little for one more step after the evidence is sufficient. Under on-policy sampling, each update
draws its games from a model that already answers slightly less often than the previous one. The middle
panel of figure 13 is consistent with this drift toward fewer answers. With phases, in contrast, the model
playing the games is renewed only once during training. The on-policy variant’s reasoning is also cut off more
often at the 8,192-token limit. After a cutoff, the variant mostly takes more steps instead of answering. We
keep the phases because they take a third of the time and produce a model that ends fewer episodes without
an answer. Section C.4 describes three more rules tested against steps that bring no evidence.
In the test episodes, the on-policy variant runs for too long. On Qwen3-VL-4B, it takes 18.9 steps per episode
in the five HiddenGames tasks with an answer, compared to 7.9 for RLVB. It ends half of these episodes without
an answer, compared to 18% for RLVB (table 24). It commits before its evidence is sufficient in only 7% of
these episodes, compared to 28%. Unlike RLVB, it often keeps working after its belief line names a single
answer (section 4.6). Table 37 shows what its extra steps add, and section K describes what it knows when
it does not answer.
