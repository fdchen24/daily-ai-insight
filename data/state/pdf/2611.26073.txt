ABSTRACT
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as judges and graders to re-
place human annotators, raising the question of when such replacement is statis-
tically justified. To support such decisions, the Alternative Annotator Test (ALT-
TEST) was introduced for dense settings where all items are annotated by the same
annotators. However, in many large-scale annotation settings, items are annotated
by different subsets of annotators. We therefore propose the Alternative Annotator
Test with Resampled Annotations (ALTRA-TEST), a statistical test that does not
require that all items are annotated by the same annotators. ALTRA-TEST uses a
bootstrap that resamples items, draws k human annotations for each item, and sub-
stitutes one of them with an LLM annotation. This produces paired all-human and
mixed human–LLM panels. A replacement is supported when the inter-annotator
agreement (IAA) of the mixed panel is statistically non-inferior to the IAA of the
all-human panel, i.e., when the LLM preserves the signal that the human annota-
tors share. We establish that ALTRA-TEST is a consistent test of asymptotic level
α. We then evaluate it through simulations and real-world experiments in which
we test 18 LLMs and 5 prompting techniques on 20 datasets across text, image,
audio, and video. Across these evaluations, about 100 items often suffice for the
test. In simulations, where we know whether the LLM is a better or a worse anno-
tator than the humans, ALTRA-TEST makes more correct replacement decisions
than existing baselines. In real-world data experiments, LLMs pass the test in
about half of the setups, depending mainly on the task. Since replacement is not
always justified, we encourage researchers to test it for their task1.

INTRODUCTION
Creating labeled datasets has long depended on human annotation, one of the slowest and most
expensive stages of the AI pipeline (Ding et al., 2023; Tan et al., 2024). Researchers use human an-
notations to train models and to evaluate them, and they draw empirical conclusions from both (Mei
et al., 2026; Omar et al., 2026b), so annotation quality directly affects the validity of the resulting
models and findings. Large language models (LLMs) offer an appealing alternative: they can an-
notate quickly, cheaply, and at a scale that is difficult to achieve with experts or even with crowd
workers (Chiang & Lee, 2023; Gilardi et al., 2023; T¨ornberg, 2023; Nahum et al., 2025). Indeed,
LLMs are increasingly used as annotators, judges (LLM-as-a-judge), and raters (Zheng et al., 2023;
Wei et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Wu, 2025; Mei et al., 2026).
In many AI applications, such as response quality evaluation, there is no ground truth to check
labels against (unlike, e.g., checking whether code runs or a math answer is correct), and human
judgment defines the correct label (Raykar et al., 2010; Paun et al., 2018). Human annotations can
serve two different goals. In most cases, they are used to train, evaluate, or judge models. This
requires one target per item, so what matters is the signal that the annotators share, and not the
variation between individual annotators. In other studies, the disagreement is itself the object of

Basile et al., 2021; Uma et al., 2021; Plank, 2022). In this paper, we focus on the first goal, although
we also propose adaptations of our test for the second. Replacing human annotators with LLMs is
therefore useful only if the replacement preserves this shared signal (Egami et al., 2023; Gligoric

been shown to disagree with experts even when their responses appear fluent or confident (Zhou
et al., 2024; Griot et al., 2025; Szymanski et al., 2025; Tseng et al., 2025; Wu & Aji, 2025; Omar

Elangovan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Accordingly, using an LLM as an alternative to humans
requires statistical justification for the replacement (Bavaresco et al., 2025; Calderon et al., 2025b).
Since there is no ground truth to compare with, researchers assess human annotation quality using
inter-annotator agreement (IAA) (Hayes & Krippendorff, 2007; Klie et al., 2024): if independent
annotators consistently agree above chance, the task is well-defined, and the labels capture a mean-
ingful shared signal rather than random noise (Artstein & Poesio, 2008; Krippendorff, 2011). Even
when IAA is modest, as in subjective tasks where some disagreement is inherent (Basile et al.,
2021; Davani et al., 2022; Plank, 2022; Sap et al., 2022; Fleisig et al., 2023), agreement clearly
above chance still indicates a shared, if noisy, signal. LLM annotations are typically evaluated using
accuracy, F1, or correlation with a majority-vote label (Ziems et al., 2024; Bavaresco et al., 2025;
Elangovan et al., 2025; Guerdan et al., 2025). However, since some disagreement is inherent, in-
dividual human annotators also disagree with the majority-vote label, so even a human would not
score perfectly on these metrics. Their ceiling depends on the task, and without knowing it, it is un-
clear whether a given score is good or poor. As a result, such measures cannot provide a statistically
grounded replacement decision. A replacement test should therefore mirror how we evaluate human
annotation quality, and test whether the LLM keeps the IAA over all human annotations statistically
non-inferior to that of the humans.
Two recent works formulate human replacement by LLMs as a statistical procedure, the Alternative
Annotator Test (ALT-TEST; Calderon et al. (2025b)) and the Statistical Turing Test (STT; He et al.
(2025a)). Both assume a representative set of items, which we call the evaluation set, each labeled
by multiple human annotators. Crucially, they also require the same group of annotators to label
every item in this set. However, this requirement is restrictive, and in many common annotation
settings it does not hold. In crowdsourcing, workers sometimes choose their assignments and may
stop after a few items. In preference annotation, a large pool of participants contributes only a few
judgments each, and in demanding tasks, such as verifying the factuality of a long document, an
annotator can complete only a few items. Finally, many existing datasets omit the identities of the
annotators (Siththaranjan et al., 2024). In all of these settings, researchers observe multiple human
labels for each item, but the items are not all labeled by the same annotators, making existing tests
inapplicable. Indeed, 11 of our 20 datasets do not satisfy their requirements.
2
In this work, we introduce the Alternative annotator test with Resampled Annotations (ALTRA-
TEST2; Figure 1), which does not require that the same annotators label all the items in the evaluation
set. The test uses a paired percentile bootstrap: in each bootstrap sample, it resamples items with
replacement, draws k human annotations per item, and constructs a mixed panel by replacing one
human annotation with the LLM annotation. Unlike existing tests, it resamples the annotations
within each item, and therefore never uses the identities of the annotators. It then tests whether the
mixed-panel IAA is non-inferior to the human-only IAA. An adaptive margin permits a small IAA
loss proportional to the observed human disagreement, so the same test applies to objective tasks,
in which annotators agree strongly, and to subjective tasks, in which some disagreement is inherent.
E.g., our calibrated default of c = 0.1 allows at most a 10% increase in disagreement. We say that
an LLM passes the test when we reject the null hypothesis that the mixed-panel IAA is inferior.
We theoretically establish that ALTRA-TEST is a consistent test of asymptotic level α, i.e., as the
number of items grows, it accepts valid replacements with probability approaching 1 and falsely
accepts invalid ones with probability at most α. We then use simulations, in which items have
a latent gold label that annotators observe with noise, and we control whether the LLM is more
or less noisy than the humans. This enables us to evaluate the replacement question directly, as we
measure each procedure’s better-model pass rate and worse-model pass rate (i.e., correctly accepting
simulated LLMs that are less noisy than simulated humans versus falsely accepting noisier ones). In
the setup that the existing tests require, in which the same annotators label all items, ALTRA-TEST
dominates ALT-TEST and STT, with a higher better-model pass rate at any given worse-model pass
rate. We also evaluate the ALTRA-TEST in the setup that motivates it, in which the items are not
all labeled by the same annotators, and there, its pass rates remain the same. The simulations also
calibrate the default margin constant to c = 0.1 and show reliable behavior with around 100 items.
In addition, we evaluate the ALTRA-TEST on 20 real-world datasets across text, image, audio, and
video, spanning objective and subjective tasks with a wide range of human IAA levels. We test
18 LLM judges and 5 prompting techniques. Under the default CoT setting, LLMs pass the test
in 71 of 201 judge–dataset pairs (35.3%); when each judge uses its best prompting technique, this
increases to 95 of 201 (47.3%). We find that replacement is strongly dataset-dependent, and in many
passing cases the LLM not only passes the test but increases IAA. Consistent with the simulations,
sample-size analyses show that around 100 items often suffice for LLMs that pass with 300 items.
We encourage researchers to apply the ALTRA-TEST before delegating annotation to an LLM.
As the test examines whether the LLM preserves the signal that the annotators share, its result is
meaningful only when this signal exists, and we recommend applying it when human agreement is
at least fair (IAA ≥0.2). We hope it supports more explicit and statistically justified decisions.

RELATED WORK
IAA as a proxy for human annotation quality.
When ground-truth labels are unavailable, re-
searchers commonly assess annotation quality through IAA (Artstein & Poesio, 2008; Krippendorff,
2011). In this work, we use Krippendorff’s α, a standard chance-corrected IAA coefficient that
supports multiple annotators, missing annotations, and nominal or ordinal labels (Hayes & Krippen-
dorff, 2007; Krippendorff, 2011). IAA scores should be interpreted relative to the task: objective or
well-specified tasks are expected to yield high agreement, while difficult, ambiguous, or preference-
based tasks may yield lower but still meaningful agreement (Pavlick & Kwiatkowski, 2019; Plank,
2022; Lissak et al., 2024). This interpretation depends on the purpose of annotation. When dis-
agreement is itself the object of study (Aroyo & Welty, 2015; Basile et al., 2021; Uma et al., 2021;
Plank, 2022), forcing high IAA may remove the variation that the study aims to measure. Our focus
is different (see Section 1), and for such studies we propose variants of our test (see Section 3.2).
Even in subjective tasks, such as response-preference annotation, where human IAA may have an
inherent ceiling, positive and non-negligible IAA (e.g., 0.2 or higher) indicates that the annotations
carry a shared signal that an LLM replacement should reflect. We test whether LLMs preserve it.
Testing whether LLMs can replace human annotators.
Various methods propose correcting or
calibrating model-generated labels (Angelopoulos et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026),
but they do not decide whether replacement is justified. Two recent procedures address replacement

tests whether an LLM is non-inferior to that annotator in its agreement with the remaining annota-
tors. STT (He et al., 2025a) tests whether human and mixed human–LLM panels are statistically
equivalent. Both require the same annotators to label all the items, a requirement that often does not
hold (see Section 1). ALTRA-TEST removes this requirement. Like ALT-TEST and unlike STT, it
answers a non-inferiority question. This fits the replacement decision, since LLM annotations are
much cheaper than human ones, and researchers may prefer an LLM even at the cost of a small loss.
An equivalence test does not accept an LLM that increases IAA, even if it captures the shared signal
more reliably than a typical annotator. Like STT, it uses bootstrap resampling, but unlike both, it
does not replace all annotations of a single annotator. Instead, for each sampled item, it draws k
human annotations, replaces one with the LLM annotation, and bases the decision on a percentile-
bootstrap statistic. Its margin also differs. ALT-TEST uses a fixed margin that is chosen according to
the type of the annotators, and STT derives its margin from the agreement gap between two groups
of humans, while the margin of ALTRA-TEST adapts to the observed human disagreement. When
all three tests can be applied, ALTRA-TEST dominates both (see Section 4).

METHOD
Setup and notations.
Let k ≥3 be the panel size used by the test, and let D = {x1, . . . , xN} be
a dataset of N items. For each item xi, we observe Mi human annotations Hi = {hi,1, . . . , hi,Mi},
with Mi ≥k. We do not require annotator identities, and we use each item’s annotations only as a
set of labels. The LLM annotation is f(xi). For each item, let yi,h be a panel of k annotations from
Hi, and let yi,m be formed by replacing one annotation in yi,h with f(xi). Stacking these item-level
panels gives two annotation matrices: the human-only matrix Yh and the mixed matrix Ym, both of
dimension N ×k. Let R be an agreement or reliability score applied to an annotation matrix, where
higher values indicate a more reliable annotation panel. We define 1−R as the disagreement level.
In the main experiments, we use Krippendorff’s α, the standard chance-corrected IAA coefficient
for nominal and ordinal labels; we give its formal definition in Appendix C.1. Finally, we define:
Rh = R(Yh),
Rm = R(Ym),
∆= Rm −Rh.
The substitution gap ∆measures the effect of replacement; a positive value means that the mixed
panel is more reliable than the human-only panel.

Adaptive margin.
Researchers who apply the ALTRA-TEST can accept a small loss in the IAA
score of the mixed panel. This reflects the practical trade-off behind replacement: LLM annotations

adaptively. In well-defined or objective annotation tasks, human annotators are expected to agree

3.2
VARIANTS FOR ANNOTATOR PERSPECTIVES
The ALTRA-TEST examines whether the LLM preserves the signal that the annotators share, which
is our main focus (see Section 1). When the goal is instead to represent the different perspectives of
the annotators, the test is not sufficient, since the replaced annotation is drawn from the whole pool,
and an LLM can pass while it is not aligned with a smaller group of annotators (see Section 4.4).
For this goal, we propose two variants of the test.
Group-wise testing.
When each annotation carries a group label, such as a demographic attribute,
and the LLM should align with each group, the test can be run separately within each group, with
panels drawn only from that group’s annotations. This requires at least k annotations per item in
each tested group. Researchers can then require the LLM to pass in every group or in specific groups.
Equivalence.
Without group labels, a stricter criterion can be used. An LLM that makes the
annotations more consistent than those of the humans is as undesirable as one that makes them less
consistent, as it hides the variation among annotators. The equivalence variant, EQ-ALTRA-TEST,
therefore tests whether the substitution gap is within the margin in both directions, and the LLM
passes only if it does not increase agreement by more than the margin (see Appendix B.1).

SIMULATION-BASED ANALYSIS
Simulation studies of statistical tests typically estimate Type I error and power under the null and
alternative hypotheses of the test. For the ALTRA-TEST, these hypotheses are defined by IAA
scores, which are only a proxy for the replacement question, since they do not measure whether
the LLM is closer than a human annotator to the ground-truth label. In this section, we go beyond
this proxy and answer the replacement question directly. We generate the ground-truth label of each
item and control the noise of the humans and of the LLM. We define the better-model pass rate
and the worse-model pass rate, where a better model is a simulated LLM that is less noisy than
the simulated humans and a worse model is one that is noisier. A good test should accept better
models, and worse models should fail it. These measures resemble power and Type I error, but they
are defined by ground-truth accuracy, which also gives a common criterion for comparing tests with
different hypotheses.
6
We design the simulations in three stages. We start with a basic setup, in which every item has
one ground-truth label, all annotators make the same kind of errors, and every annotator labels
every item. This setup satisfies the requirements of the existing tests, so we use it to compare the
ALTRA-TEST with them and to calibrate its margin (see Section 4.2). We then relax each of these
assumptions and vary the parameters of the test and of the task, to examine whether the results still
hold (see Section 4.3). Finally, we examine what passing the test means in two cases in which a
ground-truth label is less meaningful, when annotators form groups with different perspectives and
when human agreement is very low (see Section 4.4).
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Data generation. We simulate annotation tasks with a known ground-truth label for each item. For
each simulated dataset, we first generate the true item labels and then generate human and LLM
annotations by adding noise. The human annotators share a noise level, ηh, and the LLM has its
own noise level, ηf. Lower noise means a higher probability of providing the ground-truth label, so
ηf < ηh defines a better-model case and ηf > ηh defines a worse-model case. Note that nothing in
this simulation is specific to humans or LLMs, as we simulate a pool of annotators, which we call
the humans, and a single candidate annotator, which we call the LLM.
Following Calderon et al. (2025b), each simulation begins by drawing the prior probability of each
label, and then drawing a ground-truth label for each item. This produces a synthetic dataset with
known ground-truth labels. We then generate human and LLM annotations as follows: with proba-
bility 1−η, the annotator returns the ground-truth label; with probability η, it returns a label sampled
from the same label prior. In the basic setup, we draw the label prior from a Dirichlet distribution

Baselines. We compare the ALTRA-TEST with the two known statistical procedures closest to the
replacement question. Both procedures require the same annotators to annotate all items. The first
baseline is the ALT-TEST (Calderon et al., 2025b). It uses a leave-one-annotator-out procedure: each
annotator is held out in turn, and a statistical test compares whether the LLM is non-inferior, up to
a margin ε, to the held-out annotator in its alignment with the remaining annotators. The second
procedure is STT (He et al., 2025a), which uses a bootstrap over items. It first computes the IAA of
the all-human panel. Then, for each annotator, it replaces all their annotations with the LLM ones
and computes the mean IAA of the resulting mixed panels. It uses Two One-Sided Tests (TOST;
Rogers et al., 1993) to test whether the all-human and mixed-panel IAAs are equivalent, i.e., within
a margin defined by a frac parameter. We evaluate STT-Alt, a non-inferiority adaptation of STT that
uses only the lower one-sided test and for completeness, we also compare the EQ-ALTRA-TEST
(see Section 3.2) with the original STT. Appendix A.1 provides full implementation details.

4.3
ROBUSTNESS OF THE TEST
In this section, we examine whether the results hold beyond the basic setup. We briefly describe the
main findings here, and provide the full results in Appendix D.
Test and task parameters. Across all the examined numbers of annotators per item m, panel sizes
k, and numbers of labels L, the worse-model pass rate remains below 0.05 (Figure 7). A larger m
stabilizes the better-model pass rate and a larger L increases it, so tasks with few annotations per
item or with binary labels require more items. A larger k provides only modest gains, and k = 3 is
the most annotation-efficient choice. Among the agreement metrics, Krippendorff’s α and Fleiss’ κ
yield the highest better-model pass rates (Figure 8a). The comparison with the baselines also holds
for other generative families (Figures 5a and 5b).
Annotator bias. In the basic setup, all annotators make the same kind of errors. When every
human annotator has a personal bias toward specific labels, the ALTRA-TEST still outperforms the
baselines (Figure 9a). We also simulate a bias that the humans and the LLM share, which is a
limitation of every test that relies on human annotations. Also in this case, the worse-model pass
rate of the ALTRA-TEST remains below 0.05 (Figure 8b).
8
Sparse annotations. To enable the comparison with the baselines, in all the simulations above
every annotator labels every item. However, the ALTRA-TEST is designed for sparse annotations,
in which every annotator labels only a few items and the baselines cannot be applied. We therefore
vary the number of items that each annotator labels, from 1 to all items. The pass rates do not change
materially (Figure 9b), so the ALTRA-TEST behaves the same with sparse annotations.
4.4
ANNOTATOR PERSPECTIVES AND LOW HUMAN AGREEMENT
Finally, we examine two cases in which the result of the test should be interpreted with care. The
full results are in Appendices B.2 and D.3.
Annotators with different perspectives. In subjective tasks, annotators may form groups that hold
different perspectives. We simulate two such groups, a larger one and a smaller one, each with its
own ground-truth label for every item, and an LLM that follows the larger group, the smaller group,
or a mixture of them (see Appendix B.2). As shown in Figure 3, the test behaves as expected when
the goal is to preserve the shared signal. An LLM that follows the larger group passes at high rates.
In contrast, an LLM that follows the smaller group rarely passes when the larger group dominates
the pool. When researchers want the LLM to represent each perspective, we recommend using the
variants of the test (see Section 3.2).
Low human agreement. Since the margin increases with human disagreement, an uninformative
LLM may pass when human agreement is very low. We find that a random LLM never passes when
the human IAA is above 0.1, and starts to pass below this level (see Appendix D.3). We therefore
recommend applying the test only when the human IAA is at least 0.2.

REAL-WORLD EXPERIMENTS
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
We evaluate the ALTRA-TEST on 20 datasets spanning text (12), image (5), audio (2), and video
(1). The datasets cover nominal and ordinal tasks with two to ten labels, at least three annotations
per item, and human agreement ranging from ¯Rh = 0.01 to 0.90. We use a subset of 300 items per
dataset and use only items with at least three human labels. Table 5 describes all 20 datasets.
We evaluate 18 LLMs as candidate annotators, assigning to each modality the models that na-
tively accept its input: Qwen3-8B (Q3-8b) and Qwen3-14B (Q3-14b), Llama-3.2-3B (L3.2-3b)
and Llama-3.1-8B (L3.1-8b), and Gemma-3-4B (G3-4b) and Gemma-3-12B (G3-12b) for text; the
vision–language models Qwen2.5-VL-3B (Q2.5V-3b) and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Q2.5V-7b), Llama-3.2-
11B-Vision (L3.2V-11b) and the two Gemma-3 models for image, with the Qwen2.5-VL pair also
handling video through sampled frames; and Qwen2.5-Omni-3B (Q2.5-O-3b) and Qwen2-Audio-
7B (Q2-A-7b) for audio. Alongside these 11 open-weight models, spanning roughly 3B to 14B pa-
rameters, we evaluate seven proprietary judges: Gemini-3.1 Pro (Gem-P), Gemini-3.7 Flash (Gem-
F) and Gemini-3.5 Flash-Lite (Gem-L) on all four modalities; GPT-5.6 Terra (GPT-T) and GPT-5.6
Luna (GPT-L) on text and image; and GPT-Audio-1.5 (GPT-A) and GPT-Audio-Mini (GPT-A-M)
on audio. This gives 11 text, 10 image, 7 audio and 5 video judges. Dataset details, references and
links for all models are provided in Appendix A.3.
Each judge is evaluated using up to five prompting techniques (Mizrahi et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025):
zero-shot, few-shot, chain-of-thought (Wei et al., 2022), an ensemble of these three (Nahum et al.,
2025), and GEPA prompt optimization (Agrawal et al., 2025). GEPA is applied only to open-weight
models because of its computational cost. We run the ALTRA-TEST using panels of k = 3 anno-
tations (with kc = 3), Krippendorff’s α as the agreement coefficient, B = 300 bootstrap iterations,
a significance level of α = 0.05, and the simulation-calibrated margin constant c = 0.1. Since
real-world data have no gold labels with which c can be chosen, we use the value calibrated in the
simulations and do not vary it.

5.3
ANNOTATOR PERSPECTIVES IN REAL DATA
In Section 4.4, we show with simulations that passing the test means that the LLM preserves the
signal that the pool of annotators shares, and that an LLM can pass while it is not aligned with a
smaller group of annotators. However, when researchers want the LLM to represent the perspectives
of different groups of annotators, and not only the shared signal, passing the test with the whole pool
is not sufficient. For such cases, we propose two variants of the test (see Section 3.2). In group-
wise testing, the test is applied separately within each group, and in the equivalence variant, an
LLM that increases the agreement also fails. We next apply group-wise testing to real-world data,
and examine whether LLMs that pass the test are also aligned with each group of annotators. We
evaluate the equivalence variant with simulations in Appendix B.4.
We use DICES-350, a conversational-safety dataset in which the demographics of the annotators
define 13 groups, by age, education, gender, and race. When panels are drawn from the whole pool
and every LLM uses its best prompting technique, four LLMs pass the test, Gemma-3-12B, Gemini-
3.1-Pro, GPT-5.6-Terra, and GPT-5.6-Luna. When we require an LLM to pass in every group, only
11
Gemma-3-12B passes, and the other three fail in the Latinx group (see Appendix B.3). Group-wise
testing therefore exposes a misalignment with a specific group, which pooled testing averages out.
6
CONCLUSION
As LLMs are increasingly used in place of human annotators, their efficiency must be accompanied
by statistical evidence that the replacement preserves annotation quality. We propose ALTRA-
TEST, the Alternative Annotator Test with Resampled Annotations, which formulates this as a non-
inferiority test: it asks whether the inter-annotator agreement (IAA) of a mixed human-LLM panel
is statistically non-inferior to that of an all-human panel. Unlike prior replacement tests, ALTRA-
TEST can be applied to sparse annotation settings, where different items are labeled by different
subsets of annotators and no shared panel is required.
In simulations, ALTRA-TEST achieves a stronger trade-off than existing procedures: for the same
rate of false replacements, it accepts more valid ones. Around 100 annotated items often suffice for
the test. Replacement is possible in many settings, but the outcome depends more on the dataset
than on the judge or prompting technique. The main implication is that LLM annotators should be
validated per task rather than adopted as general practice.
Because the test is relative to the observed human pool, its verdict should always be reported along-
side human IAA: when human agreement is itself very low, a passing result does not show that the
labels contain a useful signal. For the same reason, the test cannot detect errors that the LLM shares
with the human annotators. In addition, passing the test means that the LLM preserves the signal
that the annotators share, and when the goal is to represent the perspectives of different groups of
annotators, the group-wise and equivalence variants of the test should be used. Finally, in this paper,
the LLM provides a single annotation for each item. We leave for future work an extension of the
test that considers the distribution of the LLM annotations for each item, and examines whether it
is aligned with the distribution of the human annotations. Such an extension is also suitable for the
goal of representing the perspectives of the annotators. We hope ALTRA-TEST helps researchers
make more rigorous and transparent decisions about when LLMs can replace human annotators.

A.1
ALT-TEST AND STT
In this section, we describe the two existing tests with which we compare the ALTRA-TEST, the
ALT-TEST (Calderon et al., 2025b) and the Statistical Turing Test (STT; He et al., 2025a), and how
we implement them. Table 4 summarizes the differences between the three tests.
To make the comparison fair, all tests receive the same input whenever their requirements allow it.
In the simulations, we generate every dataset and every subsample of items once, and apply all tests
to it. In the real-world experiments, all tests receive the same LLM annotations, and for each dataset,
we use the items that satisfy the requirements of all the compared tests. Differences between the
results therefore come from the tests and not from the data.
ALT-TEST.
The ALT-TEST compares the LLM with each human annotator separately. Each an-
notator is held out in turn, and the test examines whether the LLM is aligned with the remaining
annotators at least as well as the held-out annotator, up to a margin ε. The tests of the different
annotators are corrected for multiple comparisons, and the LLM passes when it wins against at least
half of the annotators. We use the implementation of Calderon et al. (2025b).
The margin of the ALT-TEST is chosen according to the type of the annotators, with ε = 0.10 for
crowd workers, 0.15 for skilled annotators, and 0.20 for experts. In the simulations, we therefore
examine a range of margins and mark these three values. In real-world data, the test can be applied
only when the identities of the annotators are available and every annotator labels enough items, for
which we require at least 30 of the evaluated items. We therefore apply it only to the datasets that
satisfy these conditions.

Statistical Turing Test (STT).
The STT examines whether the LLM is indistinguishable from a
human annotator. Each annotator of an all-human panel is replaced by the LLM in turn, and the
agreement of the resulting mixed panels is compared with the agreement of the all-human panel
using a paired bootstrap. Since the STT is an equivalence test, the LLM passes only when both
one-sided tests reject. We implement the test following He et al. (2025a).
The margin of the STT is estimated from two disjoint groups of human annotators who label the
same items, and it is multiplied by a scaling factor frac. The original paper presents several values
of frac and does not recommend one. We therefore examine a range of values in the simulations and
use frac = 0.5 in the real-world experiments. The requirement of two groups of annotators holds
in our simulations, but among our real-world datasets, it holds only for DICES-350. We therefore
evaluate the STT in the simulations and on DICES-350.
STT-ALT.
The STT tests equivalence, while the ALT-TEST and the ALTRA-TEST test non-
inferiority, so their results cannot be compared directly. When the LLM is better than the humans,
a non-inferiority test should accept it, but an equivalence test may correctly not accept it, since the
LLM differs from the humans. We therefore introduce STT-ALT, which adapts the STT to the
alternative annotator question.
STT-ALT is identical to the STT, with the same sampling, agreement score, bootstrap, and margin,
but it applies only the lower one-sided test. It therefore tests whether the mixed panel is not worse
than the all-human panel by more than the margin. Since both tests use the same bootstrap statistics,
every LLM that passes the STT also passes STT-ALT.
A.2
SIMULATIONS
In this section, we provide the full details of the simulations. We start with the basic simulation of
Section 4, and then describe the additional settings, which vary the label distribution, the behavior
22
of the annotators, and the level of human agreement. The simulations with groups of annotators are
described in Appendix B.2.
Basic simulation.
Each simulated dataset contains 600 items, generated as described in Section 4.
We examine 25 conditions, one for each pair of noise levels (ηh, ηf). In 10 conditions the LLM is
noisier than the humans (worse-model), in 10 it is less noisy (better-model), and in 5 the noise levels
are equal. For each condition, we generate 10 independent datasets and draw from each of them 10
independent subsamples of N items, without replacement. Subsamples from the same dataset may
overlap, but no item is repeated within a subsample. This gives 100 test decisions per condition and
2500 decisions overall. Every run uses B = 500 bootstrap iterations. Since k = kc in the default
setting, the margin is ε(b) = c(1 −R(b)
h ), and when we vary k, we apply the full kc/k adjustment.
Generative families.
In the basic simulation, we draw the label prior from a Dirichlet distribution
and sample the noisy labels from this prior, which gives a skewed nominal distribution. We repeat
the full noise grid with two additional distributions, which change both the gold labels and the errors.
• Uniform. The gold labels are uniform over the L labels, and a noisy label is sampled
uniformly from the remaining L −1 labels. This represents balanced nominal tasks.
• Gaussian. The gold labels follow a discretized normal distribution, and a noisy label is
round(y + N(0, σ)), reflected at the boundaries, where σ is chosen to obtain the target
error rate η. This represents ordinal tasks, in which errors fall on nearby labels.
Annotator behavior.
In the basic simulation, all annotators err in the same way, and every anno-
tator labels every item. We relax both assumptions.
Personal bias. In practice, each annotator may have a personal bias and tend toward specific labels
when making an error. We therefore give every human annotator j a personal bias dj, which is a
distribution over the labels. When annotator j errs, the label is sampled from (π+λdj)/(1+λ) with
λ = 2, and not from π. The basic simulation is the special case λ = 0. To generate the biases, we
draw for each dataset a shared direction b0 ∼Dirichlet(1L), and then draw dj ∼Dirichlet(κb0)
with κ = 4, so the biases of different annotators are related but not identical. The LLM has no
personal bias and errs according to π, as in the basic simulation.
Shared bias. We also simulate a bias that is shared by different annotators. For each item, one label
other than the gold label is chosen in advance as a distractor. When an annotator errs, the label is the
distractor with probability p = 0.5, and otherwise it is sampled from π, as in the basic simulation.
We examine two cases. In the first case, both the humans and the LLM err toward the distractor, so
their errors are correlated. In the second case, only the humans do.
Sparse annotations. Finally, we simulate the setting for which the ALTRA-TEST is designed, in
which each annotator labels only a few items. Each annotator labels t items, and we examine twenty
values of t from 1 to 200. Every item still receives m = 6 labels from six different annotators, so the
pool contains 1200/t annotators, from 1200 at t = 1 to 6 at t = 200. The annotators have a personal
bias, as described above. At t = 200, every annotator labels every item, which is the dense setting.
For t < 200, the ALT-TEST and the STT cannot be applied, so we evaluate only the ALTRA-TEST.
Low human agreement.
To examine the test when human agreement is very low, we use the basic
simulation with higher human noise levels, so that human IAA falls in three low ranges, 0.1 to 0.2,
0.05 to 0.1, and below 0.05. We pair these humans with an LLM that carries no information about
the items. We examine an LLM that assigns random labels, and an LLM that always assigns the
most frequent label of the dataset.

B
ANNOTATOR PERSPECTIVES
The ALTRA-TEST asks whether an LLM preserves the signal that the annotators share. This is the relevant
question when human labels are used to train, evaluate, or judge models. In these uses, a model is trained
toward, or scored against, one target per item, so what matters is the judgment on which the annotators converge.
In some studies, however, annotators hold distinct perspectives, for example across demographic groups, and
the study aims to preserve these perspectives (Aroyo & Welty, 2015; Basile et al., 2021; Uma et al., 2021;
Plank, 2022).
The standard test is not designed for this second goal, for two reasons. First, the replaced annotation is drawn
from the whole pool of an item’s annotations. The test therefore measures the agreement of the pooled annota-
tions, to which a larger group contributes more annotations than a smaller one. Second, the test is one-sided, so
it accepts an LLM that raises agreement, although such an LLM may remove the variation that the study aims
to capture.
In this section, we address both points. In Appendix B.1, we start by defining two variants of the test, group-
wise testing and an equivalence test. Then, in Appendix B.2, we use simulations with groups of annotators to
show how the standard test behaves when perspectives differ. In Appendix B.3, we apply group-wise testing
to a real dataset, and finally, in Appendix B.4, we evaluate the equivalence variant in simulation. We leave for
future work an extension of the test that considers the distribution of the LLM annotations for each item, and
examines whether it is aligned with the distribution of the human annotations.
B.1
VARIANTS OF THE TEST
Group-wise testing.
Suppose that every annotation carries a group label, such as a demographic attribute
of its annotator. For a group g, let Hg
i ⊆Hi denote the annotations of item xi that come from this group. The
group-wise test applies the ALTRA-TEST without any change to the items with at least k such annotations, and
it draws the panels only from Hg
i . The human-only agreement, the margin, and the decision statistic are then
all computed within the group, so the test compares the LLM with a typical annotator of that group.
Researchers can require the LLM to pass in every group, or only in the groups of interest. Since this involves
several tests, researchers should account for multiple comparisons when drawing conclusions about individual
groups. In addition, the chance that an LLM passes in every group decreases as the number of groups grows.
Each tested group also needs enough items with at least k annotations from that group. In Section 4, we show
that about 100 items are enough for a high better-model pass rate.
Equivalence.
The ALTRA-TEST is a non-inferiority test, so an LLM that increases the agreement of the
panel passes it. In some settings, we instead want to test whether the LLM is indistinguishable from a human
annotator. This is the case when the annotations should reflect human judgment and its variation, since labels
that are more consistent than the human ones misrepresent the annotators. In such settings, an LLM that
increases agreement by more than the margin should fail the test, like an LLM that decreases it.
We therefore define the EQ-ALTRA-TEST, which tests whether the substitution gap is within the margin in
both directions:

is better.

B.2
SIMULATIONS WITH ANNOTATOR GROUPS
Setup.
We simulate a task in which the annotators form two groups with different perspectives, a larger
group and a smaller group. Each group has its own label prior and its own gold label for every item, and the
annotators of a group are noisy around the gold label of their group, as in Section 4. We vary the sizes of
the two groups from 9+1 to 5+5 annotators. The simulated LLM follows one of four profiles. It labels like
an annotator of the larger group, like an annotator of the smaller group, like an equal mixture of the two, or
like a mixture in proportion to the group sizes. Since no profile is correct by construction, we report the pass
rate of each profile, with c = 0.1 and N = 200. We also repeat the simulation when every human annotator
has a personal bias bj ∼Dirichlet(1L) and errs according to (πg + bj)/2, where πg is the label prior of the
annotator’s group.
The test behaves as expected for a shared signal.
Figure 3 presents the results. When annotators form
groups, the signal that the pool shares is mainly the judgment of the larger group. Accordingly, an LLM that
follows the larger group passes the test at high rates, while an LLM that follows the smaller group rarely passes
when the larger group dominates the pool, even when it is less noisy than the humans. Such an LLM disagrees
with most of the annotators, so replacing a human annotation with its annotation reduces the agreement of the
pool. As the two groups approach equal size, neither group dominates the pool, and the gap between the profiles
closes.
The two mixtures lead to the same conclusion. An LLM that mixes the groups in proportion to their sizes
behaves like an annotator drawn at random from the pool, and its pass rates are close to those of the LLM that
follows the larger group. The equal mixture represents the smaller group more than the pool does, and it passes
less often when the groups are unequal.
For every profile, the pass rate decreases as the LLM becomes noisier, so the test remains sensitive to the quality
of the LLM. However, noise alone does not determine the result. An LLM that follows the larger group can pass
even when it is somewhat noisier than the humans, since it agrees with most annotators more than a randomly
drawn annotator does. Finally, the results with and without a personal bias are nearly identical.
When perspectives should be represented.
The same behavior is not desired when the goal is to rep-
resent each perspective. In this simulation, an LLM that follows the smaller group faithfully rarely passes,
and an LLM that ignores the smaller group passes. For this goal, we therefore recommend the variants of Ap-
pendix B.1. In Appendix B.3, we apply group-wise testing to a real dataset, and in Appendix B.4, we evaluate
the equivalence variant in simulation.
B.3
GROUP-WISE TESTING ON DICES-350
DICES-350 reports the demographics of its annotators. We use them to define 13 groups, according to age,
education, gender, and race (Table 6). Every annotator labels every item, so each group has between 7 and 75
annotations per item. The human agreement is similar in all groups, between 0.14 and 0.25, compared with
0.20 for the whole pool. Every LLM uses its best prompting technique.
When panels are drawn from the whole pool, four LLMs pass the ALTRA-TEST. When panels are drawn
within each group and an LLM must pass in all 13 groups, only G3-12b passes. Gem-P and GPT-T fail only in
the Latinx group, and GPT-L fails in three groups, including the Latinx group. In all these cases, the decision
statistic is only slightly below zero. Group-wise testing therefore exposes a misalignment with one group,
which pooled testing averages out. The choice between the two depends on whether the goal is to match the
pooled signal or to represent each group.
Selecting panels with diverse labels cannot replace group labels, because label diversity alone does not distin-
guish a minority perspective from annotation noise.

This section establishes two properties of the ALTRA-TEST. First, the substitution gap decreases at rate 1/k as
the panel size grows, supporting the panel-size adjustment in the adaptive margin. Second, under standard reg-

a consistent test of asymptotic level α.

SIMULATIONS: ADDITIONAL RESULTS
In this section, we present additional simulation results. All results use c = 0.1 unless stated otherwise. In
Appendix D.1, we start by repeating the comparison with the baselines from Section 4 in other settings, with
other distributions of the labels and with other numbers of items, and we show how we calibrated c. Then,
in Appendix D.2, we examine how the parameters of the test and of the task affect the results. Finally, in
Appendix D.3, we examine other behaviors of the annotators, and the case in which human agreement is very
low.
D.1
COMPARISON WITH THE BASELINES AND CALIBRATION
Other generative families.
In Section 4, the labels follow a skewed distribution, in which the label prior is
drawn from a Dirichlet distribution. We examine whether the comparison with the baselines also holds for two
other distributions, which we describe in Appendix A.2. The first is a uniform distribution, which represents
balanced nominal tasks. The second is a Gaussian distribution, which represents ordinal tasks.
Figures 5a and 5b repeat the comparison of Figure 2a for these two distributions. As in Section 4, the ALTRA-
TEST achieves the best trade-off. Its curve is at or above the curve of the ALT-TEST in nearly all of the valid
region, and STT-ALT is below both. Both distributions are easier than the skewed one, and the ALTRA-TEST
and the ALT-TEST reach a better-model pass rate of 1.00 within the valid region. The comparison in Section 4
is therefore the most conservative of the three. We also find that the default c = 0.1 is valid for all three
distributions.
Number of items.
In Section 4, we compare the tests with N = 200 items. Since annotated items are often
scarce, we repeat the comparison with N ∈{50, 100, 150, 300} (Figure 5c). The ALTRA-TEST achieves the
best trade-off at every N, and its advantage is largest when there are few items. As N grows, all tests approach
a better-model pass rate of 1.00, and the differences become small.
The default c = 0.1 is a good choice for every number of items. At every N, it is within the valid region,
with a worse-model pass rate of about 0.01. With few items, this value is conservative, and a larger c would
increase the better-model pass rate while remaining within the valid region. Researchers who have few items
can therefore calibrate c for their number of items.
Calibration of c.
We choose the constant c with the basic simulation. Figure 6a presents the better-model
and worse-model pass rates for different values of c. The better-model pass rate increases sharply until c = 0.1
and changes little for larger values. The worse-model pass rate is about 0.01 up to c = 0.1 and then increases,
and it reaches α = 0.05 near c = 0.135. We therefore choose c = 0.1, which gives a better-model pass rate of
0.98 and a worse-model pass rate of 0.01.
Figure 6b presents the pass rate for every pair of noise levels at c = 0.1. In every better-model condition, the
LLM passes in at least 0.92 of the runs. Only one worse-model condition exceeds α, with a pass rate of 0.08.

Panel size k.
The margin includes the adjustment kc/k, which should keep the meaning of c the same for
every panel size (see Appendix C.2). Figures 7a and 7d support this. When k varies from 3 to 7, the better-
model pass rate at N = 200 is at least 0.98 for every k. At N = 100, it is somewhat lower for k = 3 and
stable for k ≥4.
Number of annotators per item m.
The test samples k annotations from the m annotations of each item.
Figures 7b and 7e show that a larger m slightly improves both pass rates. At N = 200, when m increases from
3 to 12, the better-model pass rate increases from 0.96 to 0.99, and the worse-model pass rate decreases from
0.02 to 0.01. The effect is larger at N = 100. When the items have few annotations, we therefore recommend
using more items.
Number of labels L.
Figures 7c and 7f show that binary tasks are the most difficult. With L = 2, the
better-model pass rate at N = 200 is 0.82, compared with at least 0.98 for L ≥4, and it is lower at N = 100.
With two labels, the agreement expected by chance is high, so the same error rate changes the chance-corrected
agreement less, and the substitution gap is harder to detect. Binary tasks therefore require more items.
Agreement metric.
The ALTRA-TEST can be applied with different agreement metrics. Figure 8a repeats
the basic simulation with four other metrics. Krippendorff’s α and Fleiss’ κ give nearly identical results, with
the highest better-model pass rates and a worse-model pass rate of about 0.01. Percent agreement, which does
not correct for chance, is slightly lower. The two error-based metrics, MSE and RMSE, perform worse. MSE
reduces the better-model pass rate to 0.88, and with RMSE the worse-model pass rate is 0.09, above α. These
metrics do not correct for chance, so c = 0.1 is no longer a fixed proportion of the human disagreement, and a
smaller value of c should be used for RMSE.
D.3
ANNOTATOR BEHAVIOR AND LOW HUMAN AGREEMENT
Personal bias of annotators.
In the basic simulation, all annotators make the same kind of errors, since
an annotator who errs samples a label from the label prior. In practice, each annotator may have a personal bias
and tend toward specific labels when making an error. We therefore simulate human annotators with a personal
bias. When such an annotator errs, the label is sampled from a mixture of the label prior and a distribution that
is specific to this annotator (the full details are in Appendix A.2).

Bias shared by the humans and the LLM.
Next, we examine what happens when the humans and the
LLM have the same bias, so that they tend to make the same error. Such shared errors are a limitation of every
test that relies on human annotations as the reference, since an LLM that repeats the errors of the humans agrees
with them. For every item, we choose in advance one incorrect label, and an annotator who errs chooses this
label with probability 0.5 (see Appendix A.2). We examine two cases. In the first, both the humans and the
LLM have this bias. In the second, only the humans have it.
Figure 8b presents the results for the default setting of each test. When both the humans and the LLM share the
bias, the ALTRA-TEST behaves as in the basic simulation, with a worse-model pass rate of 0.02 and a better-
model pass rate of 1.00. When only the humans have the bias, a better LLM agrees less with the humans, and
the better-model pass rate of all three tests decreases to about 0.90. Also in this case, the worse-model pass
rate of the ALTRA-TEST remains 0.02, while the worse-model pass rates of the ALT-TEST and STT-ALT are
above α. To conclude, while shared errors are a limitation of every test that relies on human annotations, the
ALTRA-TEST is less affected by them than the other tests. Its worse-model pass rate stays below α in both
cases, and its better-model pass rate is at least as high as those of the other tests.
Sparse annotations.
To support an apples-to-apples comparison with the baselines, we have so far consid-
ered only simulations with shared annotators, in which every annotator labels every item. In these simulations,
we found that the ALTRA-TEST outperforms the baselines. However, the motivation for the ALTRA-TEST
is a different and very common setup, in which the annotations are sparse and every annotator labels only a
few items. In this setup, the baselines cannot be applied, so we cannot compare with them. We therefore next
analyze the behavior of the ALTRA-TEST alone. We vary the number of items that each annotator labels from
t = 1 to t = 200, where t = 200 means that every annotator labels all items. The annotators have a personal
bias, as above.
Figure 9b presents the results. The number of items that each annotator labels does not affect the test. At every
t, the better-model pass rate is at least 0.98 and the worse-model pass rate is at most 0.02, including t = 1, in
which no two items share an annotator. This is expected, since the test uses only the annotations of each item
and never the identity of the annotators.
Low human agreement.
We designed the adaptive margin to increase with the human disagreement, since
we want the test to support subjective tasks, in which some disagreement between the annotators is inherent. In
such tasks, the test therefore permits a larger decrease in agreement. This raises the question of what happens
when human agreement is very low. In this case, the margin is large, and an LLM that carries no information
about the items may pass the test.

passes. When it is between 0.05 and 0.1, the random LLM passes in 6% to 12% of the runs, and below 0.05, in

E.2
COMPARISON WITH EXISTING TESTS
In the simulations, we compare the ALTRA-TEST with the ALT-TEST and the STT. In real-world data, these
tests can be applied only to datasets in which the same annotators label many items. Nine of our datasets
satisfy the requirements of the ALT-TEST, and only DICES-350 satisfies the requirements of the STT. Table 12
compares the tests on these datasets. Every LLM uses its best prompting technique, and for every dataset, all
tests use the same items. For this comparison, we use B = 300 bootstrap iterations. Since real-world data have
no gold labels, we cannot tell which test is correct, and we only examine whether the tests agree.
The ALTRA-TEST and the ALT-TEST (with ε = 0.1) give the same result in 68 of the 94 cases, and they
accept a similar number of LLMs, 44 and 42. For example, both tests accept every LLM on CEBaB and none
on Loneliness. The disagreements do not go in one direction. The ALTRA-TEST is stricter on four datasets and
more permissive on three. The largest differences are on EPIC, in which the ALTRA-TEST accepts 9 LLMs
and the ALT-TEST accepts none, and on DICES-350, in which the ALTRA-TEST accepts 4 and the ALT-TEST
accepts 9.
On DICES-350, the STT accepts only two LLMs. As an equivalence test, it does not accept an LLM that
increases the agreement. STT-ALT, which tests non-inferiority, accepts one more LLM.

E.3
NUMBER OF ITEMS AND PANEL SIZE
Number of items.
In Section 5, we report the minimum number of items with which the LLMs pass the
test. Here, we present the full analysis. Figures 10 and 11 present the decision statistic q∗
α as a function of the
number of items N, for all 20 datasets and for every LLM with its best prompting technique.
The result of the test stabilizes with fewer items than the value of the statistic. LLMs that pass with 300 items
usually already pass with about 100 items (Table 2). The value of q∗
α continues to change with more items,
and the number of items it requires depends on the dataset. It stabilizes earlier in datasets with high human
agreement and later in datasets with low agreement. Between N = 200 and N = 300, it changes by at most
0.05 for every LLM. We therefore recommend using at least 100 items, in line with the simulations in Section 4.
More items mainly make the value of q∗
α more precise, which is useful for comparing LLMs.
Panel size.
In all the experiments, we use panels of k = 3 annotations. A larger panel includes more
annotations per item, which may be important in datasets with low human agreement. We therefore examine
whether the results change with the panel size, and vary k ∈{3, 5, 7, 10} on six such datasets, which are
DICES-350, ChaosNLI, Scruples, Vibe-Arena, T2V-Preference, and TTS-Preference. Every LLM uses its best
prompting technique, and the margin is scaled by kc/k. Of the 55 results, 49 are identical with k = 10 and
with k = 3. In the other six, the LLM fails with k = 3 and passes with k = 10, and in all of them the decision
statistic is close to zero (at most 0.01 with k = 10). Three of these cases are on DICES-350, and the others are
on Scruples, Vibe-Arena, and TTS-Preference. The results are therefore stable across panel sizes, and k = 3,
which requires the fewest annotations, is sufficient in practice.
E.4
PROMPTING
Number of few-shot examples.
In the main experiments, the few-shot prompt includes five examples.
Figure 12 examines this choice. It presents q∗
α when the number of examples varies from zero to fifteen, on five
datasets. In most cases, the statistic changes little beyond three to five examples. There are exceptions. EPIC is
more sensitive to the number of examples, and on SOMOS, two of the Gemini models continue to improve up
to fifteen examples. Overall, only four results change beyond five examples. We therefore use five examples,
which gives most of the benefit with a shorter prompt.
Reasoning effort.
In the main experiments, CoT prompting uses a mild reasoning effort. Figure 13 exam-
ines this choice for the three Gemini models, for which the API controls the reasoning effort. On average, more
reasoning helps, and the mean of q∗
α increases from 0.02 with low effort to 0.04 with high effort. However, the
effect is mixed. High effort is better than low effort in ten of the fifteen cases, and in several cases the statistic
decreases. We therefore use mild effort, which costs much less than high effort. These results also suggest that
the low pass rate of CoT in Section 5.2 is not only a result of a limited reasoning budget.
