ABSTRACT
Foundation models can reason about complex physical tasks, yet translating this intelligence into reliable
robot execution remains challenging. We argue that a critical, often overlooked factor is the robot-use
interface (RUI), which determines how agents express and execute physical actions. To systematically
study its impact, we introduce RUIBench, comprising 30 manipulation tasks across five suites spanning
world knowledge, in-context learning, discovery, dynamic interaction, and contact-rich manipulation.
We further develop RUIKit, a unified framework that crosses six action abstractions with two interaction
forms, yielding 13 interfaces under shared perception and control. In simulation, changing only the
RUI shifts a frontier agent’s success rate from 45% to 81%, and that of a fine-tuned 4B model from
13% to 47%. Our analysis reveals that effective interfaces reduce unnecessary decisions while
preserving essential feedback, with their benefits shaped by agent behavior and physical uncertainty.
We further uncover a verification gap, where agents frequently declare success despite observable
failures, revealing the need for task-level verification beyond individual actions. Together, these findings
establish RUI design as a critical factor in embodied agent reliability.

1 Introduction
Foundation models are moving from understanding the physical world to acting in it [2, 39, 74]. While
vision-language-action models learn to predict robot actions [6, 7, 43], recent agentic systems enable
general-purpose models to control robots through tool calls and executable programs [15, 16, 27, 45,
52, 76]. This introduces a critical design choice beyond the model itself: how should an agent interact
with a robot? We define the robot-use interface (RUI) as the actions an agent can command and
how they are organized for execution. From low-level motion commands [3, 4] and image-grounded
points [25, 57] to semantic skills [1, 37] and executable programs [52, 71], the RUI determines what
the agent must decide, what can be delegated to execution, and when control returns to the agent.
This distinction becomes critical in complex physical tasks. These tasks require agents to gather
missing information, react to dynamic events, and correct errors through physical feedback. An
interface that demands a new model decision for every small movement can make execution costly and
error-prone, while one that provides insufficient feedback may fail when the world deviates from the
plan. Thus, reliable execution depends not only on what an agent knows, but also on how decisions
and feedback are distributed between the agent and the robot.
However, existing embodied benchmarks compare models under fixed interfaces [18, 23, 47, 82, 84],
while interface studies typically examine selected design choices [27, 64, 68]. Consequently, the
impact of interface design remains poorly understood. In our experiments, changing only the RUI shifts

frontier agents (Fig. 1a). This raises our central question: What makes a good robot-use interface?
To answer this question, we introduce RUIBench, a benchmark combining 30 agentic manipulation tasks
with a controlled evaluation protocol. These tasks go beyond simple pick-and-place, requiring agents to
reason, acquire information through interaction, and adapt to changing physical conditions. They span
five suites: world knowledge, in-context learning, discovery, dynamic interaction, and contact-rich
manipulation. The protocol limits agents to RGB-D observations and robot states, keeps the environment
evolving during model reasoning, and evaluates task success independently of the agents’ claims. We
further develop RUIKit, a unified framework that disentangles two design choices: action abstraction,
which determines what an agent can command, and interaction form, which determines how actions are
organized and executed. RUIKit crosses six action abstractions with two interaction forms to instantiate
12 interfaces, plus a free-choice interface, all within a shared harness where only the RUI varies (Fig. 1).
Across frontier and small models in simulation and 650 real-robot episodes with frontier agents,
we find that effective RUIs reduce unnecessary decisions while preserving essential feedback
(Fig. 1b), which also makes them easier for small models to learn (Fig. 1d). Fine-grained interaction
can fragment a clear plan into excessive model decisions, a failure we call execution fragmentation.
Composing actions into programs reduces this burden when agents effectively exploit multi-step
execution and intermediate feedback. However, real-world execution changes which interfaces are
effective, as physical uncertainty demands more opportunities for local correction (Fig. 1c). We
further uncover a verification gap: agents frequently declare success despite observable failures, while
checking individual actions does not guarantee task completion. Together, these findings highlight the
importance of allocating decisions, feedback, and verification at appropriate levels.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• Agentic benchmark. We introduce RUIBench with 30 manipulation tasks across five suites and a
controlled evaluation protocol for systematically studying RUIs.
• Unified framework. We develop RUIKit, spanning 13 interfaces across six action abstractions and
two interaction forms under shared perception and control.
• Empirical insights. We reveal how decision burden, physical feedback, and task-level verification
shape embodied performance across agents and environments.
2 Related Work
Robot-Use Interfaces for Foundation Models. Foundation models control robots through learned
action policies [6, 7, 43] or interfaces that connect general-purpose models to physical actions. Existing
systems employ semantic skills and learned-policy tools [1, 13, 28, 37, 48, 51, 61, 63, 67, 83, 85, 89],
executable programs [17, 35, 40, 52, 71, 76, 79], spatial constraints and value maps [36, 38, 60],
image-grounded points and paths [25, 33, 45, 50, 57, 74, 86, 88], end-effector poses [44], and
discrete motion commands [3, 15, 31]. However, these interfaces are typically developed within
individual systems, making their independent effects difficult to isolate. Recent studies compare
selected action abstractions [27], programmatic versus per-action execution [19, 64, 68], interface
families [4, 53, 65, 91], harness components [12, 24, 73, 80], and planner-policy interfaces [34]. Yet
how these design choices jointly shape agent behavior remains poorly understood. RUIKit addresses

3 RUIBench
RUIBench provides the tasks (Fig. 2) and evaluation protocol (Sec. 3.2) for studying how RUIs shape
embodied agent performance. For a given agent and task, it fixes the robot, perception, timing, and
scoring, and varies the RUI through RUIKit (Sec. 4).
3.1 Agentic Task Design
Task design. A clear task goal does not imply a fully specified path to achieving it. Successful
manipulation often requires agents to reason beyond the initial instruction, acquire information through
interaction, and adapt to physical feedback. RUIBench captures these demands through 30 tasks in five
complementary suites (Fig. 2). World knowledge requires reasoning from prior knowledge, as in the
Tower of Hanoi. In-context learning requires using previously shown visual examples, as in building a
house from a picture. Discovery requires physical exploration, as in finding a fake coin with a balance.
Dynamic interaction requires responding to time-sensitive events, as in Simon Says. Contact-rich
manipulation requires precise adjustments during contact, as in inserting a graphics card. Negative
controls confirm that tasks depend on their intended capabilities (App. A). A scripted reference that

3.2 Evaluation Protocol
To isolate interface effects while preserving the demands of physical interaction, RUIBench enforces
non-privileged observations, continuous physical time, and independent outcome evaluation.
Non-privileged observations. Agents receive task instructions, hints available to human operators,
and, for in-context learning tasks, a previously shown video or image. All other information comes
from RGB-D cameras and robot-state readings through shared tools. SAM 3 [10] detects and segments
objects, while 3D positions are recovered from noisy sensor depth rather than privileged simulator states.
On 100 annotated frames, this pipeline detects 93% of objects with localization errors of 0.5 to 0.7 cm.
Continuous physical time. The environment continues evolving while the agent reasons. In simulation,
each model response incurs a fixed 8 s delay, during which the robot holds its pose while other objects
continue moving. This approximates the 75th percentile of 4,067 responses measured from three
frontier models, preventing differences in serving latency from confounding interface comparisons.

Independent outcome evaluation. Each episode starts from a fresh simulator instance and ends
when the agent declares completion, gives up, or reaches its turn budget or 30 min time limit. For
reference, scripted policies complete tasks in 38 s on average (Tab. 2), while some dynamic events
last only 16 to 20 s. Reaching the goal does not end an episode. Instead, success is determined
independently from the physical trajectory, verified offline from execution logs, and withheld from
the agent. The agent must therefore decide when the task is complete, allowing its completion
claims to be compared against actual physical outcomes.
Evaluation measures. Beyond task success and API cost per episode, we measure two aspects of
interface behavior. Decision burden is characterized by model turns per episode and, for programs,
actions executed per program. Completion judgment is evaluated through three rates (Tab. 6): the
fraction of episodes declared complete, the fraction of completion claims that are false, and the fraction
of failed episodes that end in a completion claim. Together, these measures distinguish execution
performance from the agent’s ability to recognize task completion.

4.1 Interface Design Space
Robot-use interfaces differ in what actions they
expose and how those actions are organized
for execution. We characterize this design
space along two complementary axes: action
abstraction and interaction form (Fig. 1).
Action abstraction. This axis determines
what an agent can command. We consider
six abstractions: Joint specifies joint-space
targets; Delta specifies relative translations
and rotations; Step provides fixed-magnitude motion commands; Pose specifies absolute end-
effector targets; Point grounds actions in image-space locations; and Skill exposes semantic op-
erations such as picking, placing, pressing, pushing, and turning. They differ in representation and
control granularity rather than forming a strict hierarchy.
Interaction form. This axis determines how actions are composed and when control returns to the
agent. In the per-action form, each model reply issues at most one action per arm, after which control
returns to the agent. In the program form, the agent writes a Python program [52] that composes

actions through sequences, loops, and conditional branches. Programs can also query observations and
adapt subsequent actions without another model reply. For a given abstraction, both forms use the
same action primitives, so they differ only in how actions are organized.
Interface configurations. Crossing six action abstractions with two interaction forms yields 12
fixed RUIs. We also introduce RUI-Free, which exposes all abstractions in both forms and lets
the agent choose, giving 13 interfaces. Per-action RUIs are named by their abstraction, such as
skill, while program-based RUIs use the prefix program, such as program-skill. Importantly,
Skill and Program represent different forms of delegation: skills encapsulate predefined robot
operations, whereas programs compose operations through agent-generated code. They can therefore
be combined rather than treated as alternatives. All configurations share the same infrastructure,
allowing either axis to vary while the other stays fixed.
4.2 Unified Agent Harness
Shared perception and control. RUIKit provides 24 underlying tools across six families (Fig. 3
and Tab. 8).
All RUIs share the same perception and control infrastructure, including camera
observation, object detection and segmentation, depth-based 3D localization, and robot-state estimation.
Shared utilities further support memory, visual marking, object tracking, grasp and motion planning,
and reachability checks. Agents also access task information, such as instructions, demonstrations,
and events, and issue waiting, stopping, completion, or give-up commands through common tools.
Only the exposed action commands and their interaction form vary across RUIs.
Execution and agent integration. RUIKit implements robot actions and observations as reusable
functions.
Each interface selects its action primitives and interaction form, with a versioned
identifier such as rui.code-skill/v1.
Frontier coding agents access these functions through
the Model Context Protocol (MCP), while smaller open models use a minimal tool-calling
loop. Programs execute in a sandbox exposing only permitted functions. Both agent types use
the same robot-side infrastructure and interface definitions.
Validation and deployment. RUIKit records tool calls, robot trajectories, observations, and outcomes
for offline evaluation. An independent scorer evaluates goal satisfaction, task constraints, and simulation
validity from the logged trajectories. Each session is also audited for unauthorized tool use, file or
network operations, and access to benchmark internals. A real-robot backend for the Franka arm reuses
the same interface definitions, tools, and harness, enabling all 13 RUIs to run without modification.
5 Experiments
5.1 Experimental Setup
Models. We evaluate two frontier coding agents, Claude Code with Claude Opus 5.5 and Codex with
GPT-6 Astra, both at high reasoning effort and connected through MCP (Sec. 4.2). We also evaluate
Qwen3.5-4B [62] through a minimal agent loop without thinking, both zero-shot and after fine-tuning.
For each of the 12 fixed RUIs, we fine-tune a LoRA adapter [32] on 300 training trajectories, 30%
from successful Qwen3.8-Max episodes and 70% from scripted policies. About half of the scripted
trajectories include injected deviations and recovery. Training and test seeds are disjoint (App. C.1).

5.2 Interface Effects in Simulation
Finding 1: Embodied performance depends on both the agent and its interface. Changing only the
RUI shifts Claude Code’s success from 45.3% to 80.7%, far exceeding the 2.1-point difference between
the frontier agents averaged across all RUIs (Tab. 3). API cost also varies by nearly threefold. This
shows that the interface is not merely a means of issuing actions: it determines how many decisions an
agent makes and what each decision must accomplish. Importantly, the same interface does not benefit
every agent equally. For Claude Code, programs raise average success from 64.0% to 75.0%, reducing
median turns from 30 to 12 and mean API cost from $1.40 to $0.77. Claude Code composes 3.3 actions
per program, and 54% of its programs observe the world after acting. Codex averages 0.9 actions and
typically wraps a single action in code, gaining only 2.7 points from the program form (App. C.2).
Skills offer another way to reduce model decisions by encapsulating predefined operations, achieving
the fewest turns and lowest costs among per-action RUIs for both agents. RUI-Free further illustrates

Learning to use an interface. The small-model experiments examine whether this ability can be
acquired through fine-tuning rather than assumed from model scale. Zero-shot, Qwen3.5-4B succeeds
in only 1.9% of episodes across the 12 fixed RUIs, and does best with Skill (6.7%), which encapsulates
whole operations. Fine-tuning on 300 trajectories per RUI raises its success to 27.3% at a one-time
cost of $0.2K to $0.9K, but how much it learns depends on the RUI (Tab. 3). Abstractions that name
a target rather than a motion (Pose, Point, and Skill) reach 35.3% to 46.7% in the per-action form,
whereas Joint, Delta, and Step stay between 12.7% and 21.3%. They also shorten episodes: each costs
$0.36 to $0.56, less than any frontier configuration, whereas a Joint or Delta episode costs $1.66 to
$2.07. Programs, in contrast, lower the average success from 29.6% to 25.1% and help only with Joint,
whose per-action episodes run longest. Thus, a good RUI is also easy to learn: the small model learns
most where the abstraction removes decisions and per-action execution keeps feedback. Pose and Skill
are the two best per-action RUIs for all three agents, even though Pose scores 0.0% zero-shot. With
per-action Pose, the small model even matches Claude Code with per-action Step (46.7% vs. 45.3%).

Finding 2: Efficient execution requires both
fewer decisions and effective feedback. Fine-
grained per-action control can fragment a clear
plan into repeated model decisions, increas-
ing latency, cost, and opportunities for error.
We call this failure execution fragmentation.
Programs can reduce this burden, but their ben-
efits may come from both action composition
and intermediate feedback.
To distinguish these effects, we introduce an
action sequence variant that executes multiple
actions without intermediate observations
and returns their results only at the end. On
the Delta, Step, and Point interfaces, Claude
Code improves from 57.8% with per-action
execution to 65.6% with action sequences,
while median turns fall from 29 to 16.5 (Tab. 4). Allowing observations and conditional execution
within programs further raises success to 75.6%, with 14 turns. Thus, action composition reduces
decision burden, and in-program feedback adds further gains.
These benefits depend on task demands (Tab. 10). In-context learning tasks benefit substantially from
executing known action sequences, while knowledge, discovery, and dynamic tasks rely more on interme-
diate feedback. Contact-rich manipulation benefits from neither consistently, suggesting that its required
corrections may exceed what current program-level interaction can provide. The key distinction is
therefore not simply short versus long execution, but where feedback can influence the next action.

Finding 3: Real-world execution changes
interface preferences. Interface advan-
tages observed in simulation do not neces-
sarily transfer to physical execution (Tab. 5).
For Claude Code, programs outperform
per-action execution by 23.3 points in sim-
ulation but lose this advantage on the real
robot (36.0% vs. 42.0%). Their sim-to-
real drops are 54.0 and 24.7 points, re-
spectively. Codex shows a smaller shift,
with per-action execution slightly outper-
forming programs on the real robot. Free
choice transfers worst: RUI-Free drops
from 100.0% in simulation to 44.0% on the
real robot for both agents. Among action
abstractions, Delta leads for both agents,
with Pose close behind for Codex.
These differences highlight the importance
of feedback and local correction under
physical uncertainty. More than in simulation, real execution is affected by depth noise, calibration
offsets, and slipping grasps, with the two cameras sometimes locating the same object up to 1.5 cm apart.
Successful trajectories often involve placing an object, inspecting the result, and making incremental cor-
rections. In contrast, a Claude Code program placed three dominoes without checking their spacing and
incorrectly declared completion. Such cases suggest that the benefit of action composition depends not
only on reducing model decisions, but also on preserving opportunities to correct execution errors.
Limits of current interfaces. Some tasks require feedback at timescales or precision levels beyond
what model-level interaction can reliably provide. On the real robot, Simon Says remains challenging
because the target moves while the agent reasons. Codex’s image-point programs provide an instructive
exception, using in-program observations to track and reach the target across all five layouts. This
suggests that time-critical reactions may need to operate within programs or lower-level controllers.
Contact-rich manipulation poses a complementary challenge. In simulation, inserting a graphics card
and folding a T-shirt remain difficult across nearly all RUIs. Their success depends on fine corrections
that are poorly resolved by the available observations: a 2 cm T-shirt misalignment spans only about
eight pixels in the head camera. Together, these cases expose the need for feedback and control
mechanisms that operate at appropriate temporal and spatial resolutions.

Finding 4: Observing failure does not
guarantee recognizing it. Beyond exe-
cuting actions, an agent must determine
whether the task has actually succeeded.
Yet 82.1% and 70.9% of failed episodes
end in a completion claim for Claude Code
and Codex, respectively (Tab. 6). We call
this failure mode illusory completion.
Crucially, these failures are often visually
detectable (Fig. 5). In an offline analysis
of 23 tasks (App. C.4), a fresh judge using
only the final frames the agent had already received recognizes 70.2% of Claude Code’s false claims
and 80.9% of Codex’s. Most of these claims are made without another observation (69.7% and
92.1%, respectively). For Codex, the offline judge uses the same underlying model in a fresh context,
suggesting that the problem is not simply a lack of visual evidence. Rather, access to evidence does
not guarantee evidence-grounded completion judgments.
Action-level checks are insufficient for task-level verification. We introduce post-condition checks
for grasping and placing in the Skill and Program-Skill interfaces. These checks use only observable
robot states and camera measurements, achieving 88.6% agreement with 149 human-verified labels
(grasps 97.3%, placements 80.0%) and 94.5% agreement with 524 simulator records.
Despite this agreement, the checks provide limited improvements (Tab. 4). Reporting failed actions
lowers the share of failed episodes that end in a completion claim from 87.5% to 81.2%, and automatic
retries lower it to 75.0% without improving task success. Only 2 of 32 corrections recover a failed grasp.
An agent may successfully execute individual actions while still failing the overall task. This exposes a
fundamental distinction: action-level feedback cannot substitute for task-level verification.
6 Conclusion
What a robot agent can accomplish depends not only on its model, but also on the interface through
which it acts. We introduced RUIBench and RUIKit to systematically study this relationship across
30 agentic manipulation tasks and 13 interfaces. Our findings reveal that effective RUIs reduce
unnecessary decisions while preserving essential feedback, with their benefits shaped by agent
behavior and physical uncertainty. Such interfaces are also easier for small models to learn. We
further uncover a verification gap, where agents frequently declare success despite observable failures.
Together, these results establish RUI design as a fundamental problem of allocating decisions, execution,
and feedback between agents and robots. The key is not simply what actions an agent can perform,
but when to decide, when to correct, and how to verify success.
Limitations and future work. Extending the real-robot study beyond five tasks on one arm would test
how widely the observed interface shifts hold. Contact-rich and time-critical tasks remain difficult
across nearly all RUIs, calling for feedback and control at finer temporal and spatial resolutions, such
as reactions inside programs or lower-level controllers. Closing the verification gap further requires
task-level verifiers that check completion against observations rather than individual actions.
