Abstract. Videos reveal how the world changes, but rarely the actions that cause those changes. Latent action
learning recovers such supervision from visual transitions, yet often learns the underlying dynamics largely
from scratch. We ask whether a pretrained video generator can instead provide the prior for both inverse and
forward dynamics. We introduce O2LaF, built on a simple principle: one prior, two dynamics. We initialize
both models from the same pretrained video DiT and adapt them to their distinct roles: a feed-forward
transition encoder with learnable action queries for inverse dynamics, and a causal, action-conditioned video
generator for forward dynamics. By controlling information access and optimizing both dynamics end-to-end
through a compact bottleneck, O2LaF learns transferable actions from unlabeled videos. Across navigation
and robotic manipulation, O2LaF sets a new state of the art in few-shot action probing and enables stronger
zero-shot action transfer across visual contexts and embodiments. Beyond generative video priors, we further
extend our framework to predictive representation priors such as V-JEPA2, achieving competitive performance.
Our findings suggest a broader paradigm for latent-action learning: repurposing pretrained video models to
discover transferable actions and predict their consequences.
1. Introduction
Videos provide abundant observations of how the world changes, but rarely reveal the actions that cause
those changes [1–3]. This missing supervision limits our ability to learn controllable world models [4–11] and
embodied agents [12–16] from internet-scale video.
Latent action learning [2, 17, 18] offers a way to recover such supervision directly from visual transitions. A
latent action model typically consists of an inverse dynamics model (IDM), which infers a latent action from
an observed transition, and a forward dynamics model (FDM), which predicts its consequence:

A compact bottleneck limits what 𝑧𝑡can encode, encouraging it to retain important information to explain
visual changes. Learning useful latent actions therefore depends on both dynamics: the IDM must infer the
transition, while the FDM must depend on the inferred action to predict its consequence.
Existing methods differ in where pretrained priors enter this pipeline (Fig. 2), leaving a gap on each side.
On the inverse side, IDMs are learned from scratch, either on raw pixels [3, 19, 20] or on top of frozen
pretrained features [21, 22, 26]. They must learn to infer actions from transitions largely through the FDM’s
reconstruction supervision, and such specialized latent action models are outperformed by general visual
foundation models [26]. Olaf-World [25] aligns latent actions with an external video representation to improve
transferability, but its IDM is still learned from scratch. On the forward side, recent methods adopt a
pretrained video generator as the FDM [23, 24], whose initial-frame conditioning supplies appearance, reducing
its leakage into the latent actions. However, they keep the standard diffusion recipe, under which the generator
can learn to disregard the latent action [24], underusing its prior as a learning signal. Yet modern video
Diffusion Transformers (video DiTs) [27] are pretrained at scale to model how visual states evolve over
time [28–34]. Their representations encode rich temporal structure, while their generative dynamics learn to
predict complex visual changes. Much of the prior needed for latent action learning therefore already exists
inside a pretrained video generator. This raises a simple question: can a pretrained video DiT serve both
inverse and forward dynamics for latent action learning? Doing so is nontrivial: the two dynamics place
different demands on the same video prior, requiring careful control over what each can access.
We introduce O2LaF, a self-supervised latent-action framework built on a simple principle: one prior, two
dynamics. We initialize the IDM and FDM from the same pretrained video DiT and adapt them independently
for their respective roles. For inverse dynamics, we repurpose the video DiT as a feed-forward transition
encoder, where action queries extract one compact latent action per visual transition under causal temporal
attention. For forward dynamics, we retain the generative role of the video DiT and introduce causal joint
prediction, which predicts the future state sequence jointly, conditioned on the inferred latent actions, while
preserving temporal causality. Unlike visual synthesis, where training often emphasizes intermediate-noise
timesteps [35, 36], the FDM here also provides the learning signal that shapes the latent-action space. At these
partially noisy timesteps, the noisy future still reveals much of the target, allowing the FDM to predict the
future with little reliance on the latent action. We therefore train at high noise, which suppresses this target
leakage and forces the FDM to depend on the inferred action. The two dynamics are optimized end-to-end
through a compact continuous latent-action space.
We further systematically study how the two dynamics shape the learned action space. For example, causal
joint prediction outperforms autoregressive prediction by removing clean-history shortcuts, while role-specific
noise levels reveal distinct information requirements for the two dynamics. Beyond generative video priors, we
also extend the repurposing principle to self-supervised predictive representation models [37] and language-
supervised video multimodal models [38] as alternative IDM backbones. Interestingly, stronger action probing
performance does not necessarily translate into better downstream generative control, highlighting the
importance of validating latent actions beyond probing through downstream applications.
We evaluate O2LaF across camera navigation and robotic manipulation. It sets a new state of the art in
few-shot action probing across both discrete and continuous action spaces, indicating stronger alignment with
ground-truth actions. Beyond probing, the learned actions also transfer without target adaptation, enabling

2. Related Work
2.1. Latent Action Learning from Videos
Latent action learning turns large-scale action-unlabeled video into a reusable intermediate control rep-
resentation. The same representation can be consumed by a world model to control future visual evolu-
tion [3, 20, 22, 25, 39], or produced by a policy or planner as a surrogate for physical actions [2, 19, 21, 40–42].
Recent work further seeks to make this control space transferable across contexts and embodiments [25, 43–45].
Existing methods differ in where pretrained priors enter the inverse–forward dynamics pipeline (Fig. 2). Early
approaches learn both transition inference and prediction directly from the target videos [2, 3, 19, 20]. To
reduce shortcuts from raw-pixel reconstruction, later methods move the prediction target into pretrained
semantic feature spaces, emphasizing motion and task-relevant change over appearance details [21, 41, 46]. A
related direction strengthens the forward model with pretrained video priors, reducing appearance burden or
replacing the from-scratch FDM [23, 24, 47]. Pretrained video representations have also been used as external
temporal supervision to orient latent actions across visual contexts [25].
Orthogonal to where the prior enters, prior work also constrains the latent space through discrete or continuous
bottlenecks [22, 47, 48], semantic or cross-view objectives [49–52], motion and consistency supervision [53–56],
and physical or geometric grounding [40, 57]. These constraints are complementary to the structural question
of how the dynamics themselves are initialized. Motivated by the shared need for transition reasoning in both
inverse and forward dynamics, O2LaF repurposes the same pretrained video DiT for both.
2.2. Repurposing Pretrained Video Diffusion Transformers
Pretrained video generative models encode reusable spatiotemporal structure beyond synthesis. Prior work
exploits their internal representations for correspondence, tracking, and geometry [58–60], while more recent
methods repurpose the generative backbone itself for feed-forward perception and temporal reasoning [61–63].
Together, these works show that video generative pretraining provides reusable temporal computation, not
only a synthesis model.
This repurposing is increasingly extending to action learning. Recent robot-policy and world-action models use
pretrained video generators as predictive representations, policy backbones, or priors for action experts [64–69].
These methods transfer video priors into learning a physical action space grounded by robot supervision.
O2LaF instead uses the video prior to discover the action representation itself from action-free video.
3. Method
We introduce O2LaF, a self-supervised latent action learning framework. Our key premise is that large-scale
video pretraining already captures how visual states evolve, which both inverse and forward dynamics need.
We therefore initialize both from the same pretrained prior, but adapt each to its role by controlling what
information it can access.

Action-aware video prior. Prior work [58–60, 63] has shown that pretrained video DiTs contain strong
temporal correspondence, enabling subjects or points to be tracked across video frames. We ask a different
question: do these models also attend to regions relevant to the ongoing action? We conduct a simple diagnostic.
We feed clean video latents to a frozen video DiT at 𝑡= 0 with the text condition “What is the action in the
video?” and visualize the resulting cross-attention. As shown in Fig. 3, the responses follow the active hands
and manipulated regions, shifting as the interaction changes across frames and clips. Although qualitative,
this observation motivates repurposing the pretrained video prior for transition-level action inference.

3.4. Video DiT as Forward Dynamics
The FDM predicts the visual consequences of inferred latent actions, providing the predictive supervision for
the IDM. To make this supervision informative, we adapt the pretrained video DiT’s temporal structure and
conditioning to control information access, limiting visual shortcuts while preserving its generative capability.
Initial-state and action conditioning. The FDM receives the clean initial state 𝑥0 and the temporally
aligned latent-action sequence z. The initial state provides visual context, including appearance and spatial
structure, outside the latent-action bottleneck [23], encouraging latent actions to capture transferable dynamics
rather than scene-specific appearance. Following standard DiT conditioning [27], the aligned actions are
projected, combined with the corresponding flow-timestep embeddings, and injected into every Transformer
block through AdaLN.

3.5. End-to-End Latent Action Learning
The IDM and FDM are connected through the sampled latent actions and optimized end-to-end (Fig. 5).
Role-specific noise sampling. Noise plays different roles in the two dynamics. For the IDM, it controls the

while 𝜋FDM can emphasize high-noise states as described in Sec. 3.4.

Inference and action transfer. While the FDM shapes the latent-action space during training, it serves as

enabling zero-shot cross-context action transfer.
4. Experiments
We evaluate O2LaF along two questions. First, do the learned latent actions align with real controls across
discrete navigation and continuous manipulation (Sec. 4.2)? Second, can the same actions serve as a unified
control interface for video world model in unseen visual contexts, without target-specific adaptation (Sec. 4.3)?
4.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate O2LaF across two domains with distinct visual dynamics. For camera navigation, we
train on MiraData [75] and evaluate on MIND [76]. For robot manipulation, we train exclusively on egocentric
human demonstrations from EgoDex [77] and directly evaluate on in-lab GR-1 robot demonstrations [39].
MiraData captures global scene and camera motion, whereas EgoDex focuses on fine-grained hand–object
interactions. No action annotations are used to train the latent action models.
Baselines. We compare with representative latent-action methods spanning the learning paradigms illustrated
in Fig. 2. For navigation, we compare with AdaWorld [20] and Olaf-World [25], all trained on the same MiraData
videos. For manipulation, we compare with DreamDojo [39], VideoWorld2 [23], LAPA-DINOv3 [19, 26], and
VILLA-X [40] using their released models. Notably, O2LaF uses only EgoDex subset of DreamDojo’s large
scale egocentric training data. We evaluate representation quality using frozen encoders under identical probing
protocols, and action-conditioned generation through a controlled second-stage world-model comparison.
Implementation details. Both the IDM and FDM of O2LaF are initialized from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B and
adapted with rank-128 LoRA. The IDM uses causal video-token attention, while the FDM uses frame-causal
attention with joint future prediction. We use a 32-D Gaussian latent action with 𝛽= 10−4 and keep

4.2. Latent Action Quality
A useful latent action should make the underlying control easy to recover with little labeled supervision. We
therefore freeze each latent-action encoder and fit lightweight linear probes [78] across increasing label budgets
to evaluate control decodability.
Evaluation protocol. We evaluate discrete camera control and continuous robot manipulation. On MIND,
we predict four simultaneous ternary controls (two translation and two rotation axes) using independent,
class-balanced logistic regression probes. We report joint exact-match accuracy, requiring all four predictions
to be correct. For manipulation, we use DreamDojo’s In-lab Eval benchmark [39], comprising GR-1 robot
demonstrations. We fit Ridge regression probes for wrist translation and rotation on both arms. We report
EEF 𝑅2, averaged over translation, rotation, and both arms. Label budgets range from 1 to 50 videos for
navigation and 1 to 32 episodes per task for manipulation.
Results. Fig. 6 shows that O2LaF consistently improves linear decodability across domains and label budgets.
Performance rises sharply with the first few labeled examples, indicating that control-relevant information is
readily accessible with limited supervision. Notably, O2LaF achieves an EEF 𝑅2 of 0.277 on GR-1, compared
with 0.149 for DreamDojo, despite training its encoder exclusively on human demonstrations without robot-
domain adaptation, suggesting that the learned latent actions capture motion structure transferable across
human and robot embodiments. Together, these results demonstrate that O2LaF learns latent actions that
admit label-efficient mappings to both discrete navigation controls and continuous robot motions.
4.3. Zero-Shot Cross-Context Action Transfer

5. Understanding the Two Dynamics
Although sharing the same pretrained video prior, the IDM and FDM serve distinct roles in latent action
learning. We systematically investigate how their design choices shape the learned action representations and
their utility for controllable video generation.
5.1. Adapting Pretrained Dynamics
Pretrained video DiTs encode rich spatiotemporal knowledge, but their representations are not explicitly
optimized for action inference. We investigate whether this knowledge can be extracted through a lightweight
action head or requires adapting the pretrained backbone. Since the IDM learns through predictive supervision
from the FDM, we further study whether a stronger FDM improves the learned action representations.
Tab. 3a shows that a frozen video DiT with a trainable action head performs substantially worse than LoRA
adaptation, while rank-16 LoRA approaches rank-128 performance. Meanwhile, scaling the FDM from 1.3B
to 14B consistently improves action decodability with a fixed-size IDM (Tab. 3b). These results suggest that
effective latent-action learning benefits from both adapting pretrained representations and strengthening
predictive supervision.

5.4. Balancing Action Learning and Generation
Jointly learning latent actions and a generative video world model is a compelling objective [24]. Yet the FDM
faces a practical trade-off: learning informative actions requires limiting visual shortcuts, whereas high-fidelity
generation benefits from broader noise coverage. In O2LaF, causal prediction and high-noise supervision serve
the former objective, but compromise the latter. We investigate whether both objectives can be reconciled
within end-to-end training.
We first study FDM noise sampling and IDM gradient routing. Tab. 5 shows that mixed noise sampling
substantially improves generation over high-noise-only training while maintaining comparable action probing
performance. However, restricting IDM gradients to high-noise timesteps provides no consistent probing gains.
Given the inherent tension, we adopt a two-stage approach [25], freezing the learned IDM and training a
bidirectional FDM. This substantially improves generation quality. Moreover, initializing from the jointly
trained FDM, rather than the original pretrained video DiT, further improves both visual quality and
action-following fidelity.
6. Conclusion
We presented O2LaF, a self-supervised framework that repurposes a pretrained video DiT for both inverse and
forward dynamics. By tailoring the two dynamics to their distinct roles and controlling information access,
O2LaF learns transferable latent actions from unlabeled videos. Across navigation and robotic manipulation,
it achieves state-of-the-art few-shot action probing and enables zero-shot action transfer across visual contexts
and human–robot embodiments. These findings establish pretrained video generators as reusable dynamics
priors for action discovery and controllable world modeling.
Limitations and future work. Future work includes improving the interpretability of the learned latent-
action space and extending video-predictive dynamics to efficient planning and closed-loop control. See
Appendix D for further discussion.
