Task success alone does not specify which behaviors post-training should reinforce. Even successful
teacher trajectories can contain unsupported assumptions, unproductive repetition, or superficial
verification. We propose Skill-FT (Skill-Guided Fine-Tuning), which uses natural-language skills
to suppress undesirable habits and elicit underrepresented effective behaviors during teacher roll-
outs, then fine-tunes a student model on the resulting trajectories. We introduce AutoMaster, an
autonomous post-training system in which an LLM agent develops these interventions through
trajectory analysis, skill design, data selection, and feedback from rollouts and evaluation. Across
Terminal, Search, and software engineering tasks with 4B and 9B student models, AutoMaster im-
proves mean performance over Vanilla SFT on successful unguided teacher trajectories by 1.42–8.99
percentage points, with the teacher, task pool, training set size, and SFT recipe held fixed. Together
with the ablations and trajectory analyses, these results suggest that behavioral shaping can make
a fixed model a more effective teacher. We further find that retaining skill instructions during SFT
yields higher mean performance than removing them across all six settings, despite their absence at
evaluation and identical supervised outputs. These findings suggest that preserving skill context as
part of a training demonstration can support better behavioral internalization.

Introduction
LLM agents are beginning to undertake model development, from creating training data to running
post-training pipelines (Kulikov et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026c; Rank et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026a).
This opens a path toward autonomous post-training, in which an agent diagnoses model weaknesses,
proposes interventions, and uses training feedback to decide what to try next. A central question is
what such a system should seek to change.
A common approach to post-training is supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on successful teacher trajecto-
ries (Chen et al., 2023; Ta et al., 2026). Yet task success does not specify which behaviors post-training should
reinforce. A teacher can reach the correct outcome despite unsupported assumptions, unproductive
repetition, or superficial verification. Fine-tuning on such successful trajectories can reinforce these
behaviors alongside the actions that support success. When the teacher rarely demonstrates a useful
behavior, even a large pool of successful trajectories may offer few examples for the student to
learn from. We therefore argue that autonomous post-training should actively shape how desirable
demonstrations are generated as well as select which ones the student learns from.
To this end, we propose Skill-FT (Skill-Guided Fine-Tuning), which uses natural-language skills to
shape teacher behavior during rollout. A skill specifies how to reason or act in situations that recur
across tasks, such as checking a claim against independent evidence. We append the applicable
skills to the system prompt to discourage undesirable habits and elicit useful behaviors that are
rare in unguided rollouts. We then fine-tune the student on selected teacher trajectories with their
original system prompts, including any skill instructions that guided the teacher.
Developing these interventions involves two questions: whether a skill elicits the intended behavior
from the teacher and how well the student learns from the resulting demonstrations. We introduce
AutoMaster, an autonomous post-training framework in which an LLM agent decides what the
teacher should demonstrate and which trajectories the student should learn from. The agent
inspects trajectories to identify behaviors worth changing, designs skills for relevant tasks, and tests
them in pilot teacher rollouts. It uses these trials to refine the skills and their scope, then applies
promising interventions to larger batches and selects demonstrations based on both task outcomes
and observed behaviors. In every round, SFT starts from the same initial student checkpoint. The
agent uses rollout evidence and student evaluation to decide whether to revise a skill, change where
it is applied, or select different demonstrations in the next round.
We evaluate AutoMaster on terminal, search, and software engineering tasks with Qwen3.5-4B
and Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026) as students. With the teacher, training task pool, number of
demonstrations, and SFT recipe held fixed, AutoMaster improves mean performance over Vanilla
SFT on successful unguided teacher rollouts by 1.42–8.99 percentage points across the six settings.
Removing either skill guidance or agent-directed selection lowers performance in every setting. The
full method also outperforms SFT on unguided demonstrations matched by task ID and per-task
sample count, showing that matching the training tasks alone does not recover the full gains.
These comparisons reveal post-training headroom that Vanilla SFT on successful unguided rollouts leaves
untapped. By using Skill-FT to shape what the teacher demonstrates and selecting which trajectories
enter student training, AutoMaster makes the same model a more effective teacher.
We further find that reducing a training–evaluation prompt mismatch can make the same trajectories
less effective for learning. With the same trajectories and SFT recipe, retaining skill instructions
during training yields higher performance than removing them across all six settings, even though
all students are evaluated without generated skills. The training examples suggest a possible
explanation: retaining skill instructions keeps behavioral rules and their conditions of application
explicit in the input. Removing them preserves the same reasoning and actions as supervision, but

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• Behavioral shaping beyond task success. We introduce Skill-FT, which uses natural-language
skills to shape the reasoning and actions a fixed teacher demonstrates during rollout, including
on tasks it already solves.
• Autonomous post-training framework. We develop AutoMaster, a framework in which an
LLM agent designs, executes, and refines behavioral interventions. It jointly optimizes skill
guidance and data selection using rollout evidence to guide subsequent exploration.
• Skill context for behavioral internalization. Controlled comparisons across the six settings
show that retaining skill instructions in the SFT input improves student performance by 2.15–
4.01 percentage points even without test-time skills. This suggests that preserving the context
behind supervised reasoning and actions can support better behavioral internalization.

Related Work
Autonomous Post-Training. Recent systems use LLM agents to implement training experiments
and revise them in light of the results (Karpathy, 2026; Chen et al., 2026a; Wei, 2026). FT-Agent (Li
et al., 2026) uses structured planning and feedback analysis to refine data and training strategies,
while RSI-Master (Du et al., 2026) uses persistent experiment records and reviewer feedback to
guide exploration. AutoMaster focuses on two decisions: how to guide a fixed teacher with skills
and which of its demonstrations to use for student training.
Skill-Based Agent Learning. Prompt and skill optimization methods use execution feedback to
refine natural-language guidance without updating model parameters (Agrawal et al., 2025; Shi
et al., 2026). Related work couples the evolution of skill libraries or procedural scaffolds with model
training (Xia et al., 2026; Ding et al., 2026; Ge et al., 2026). Skill-to-LoRA (Zhang and Qi, 2026) trains
skill-specific adapters on skill-guided demonstrations, while Skill-SD (Wang et al., 2026) uses a
skill-conditioned teacher to score tokens in the student’s own rollouts for on-policy self-distillation.
Our work examines both how skills shape teacher demonstrations and whether retaining the skill
instructions during SFT improves learning when those instructions are absent at evaluation.
Learning from Agent Trajectories. Prior work improves reasoning and agent capabilities by
learning from generated trajectories (Chen et al., 2024b,a; Zeng et al., 2024), including trajectories
that demonstrate error recovery (Fu et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). Behavior Priming (Jin et al., 2026)
selects trajectories exhibiting beneficial behaviors, while PROOF-Gen (Ta et al., 2026) uses per-task
prompt optimization to recover verified demonstrations from teacher failures. Skill-FT complements
trajectory selection and failure recovery by actively shaping behavioral patterns during teacher
rollouts, including on tasks it already solves.

3.1
Skill-FT: Skill-Guided Fine-Tuning
To shape the behavioral patterns represented in training trajectories, we guide the teacher with
natural-language skills. These skills are designed to suppress undesirable habits and elicit effective
behaviors that are underrepresented in unguided rollouts. A skill specifies how to reason or act in
relevant situations and may include behavioral principles, concrete procedures, or domain-specific
knowledge. Skills can be shared across tasks or combined to address different aspects of the same
task. For a task x, let s denote the combined text of the selected skills. We append s to the base
system prompt p and generate a trajectory through interaction with the task environment:
τ ∼Rollout(πT, x; p ⊕s),
(1)
where πT denotes the teacher model and ⊕denotes text concatenation. This guidance conditions
the teacher’s reasoning and actions during execution, enabling behavioral interventions even on
tasks it already solves.
To internalize the demonstrated behaviors, we fine-tune the student on selected teacher trajectories
using standard SFT. For each trajectory, we retain the original system prompt, including any skill
instructions, so the student sees the same instructions that guided the teacher. At evaluation, the
student follows the benchmark’s official protocol without the generated skills.

3.2
Inner Loop: Developing Behavioral Interventions
The agent begins by examining the training tasks and initial trajectories from both the unguided
teacher and the initial student. It looks for habits to discourage and useful behaviors that the teacher
rarely demonstrates, including on tasks the teacher already solves. In later rounds, the agent also
inspects student evaluation trajectories to see where the intended behaviors appear and which
weaknesses persist. These observations guide the skills it designs and the training tasks to which it
applies them.
As shown in Figure 2, pilot rollouts test whether candidate skills elicit the intended behaviors
without introducing new problems during task execution. The agent uses the resulting trajectories
to refine both the skill instructions and their scope of application, repeating trials as needed.
The agent then scales promising interventions to batched rollouts and selects trajectories it judges
valuable for training, considering both task outcomes and demonstrated behaviors. The agent may
also select unguided teacher trajectories that already exhibit useful behavioral patterns. Feedback
from generation and selection can prompt further revisions to skills or task coverage, followed by
additional rollouts as needed. The selected trajectories form the training set for the outer loop.
3.3
Outer Loop: Model Training and Feedback
The outer loop evaluates the student trained on the demonstrations selected in each round. In round
r, the inner loop produces a training set Dr. SFT starts from the same initial student checkpoint θ0
in every round:
θr = SFT(θ0, Dr).
(2)
The agent receives both scores and trajectories from evaluating θr without generated skills. Scores
summarize how well the student performs, while trajectories let the agent inspect how it handled
individual tasks. The agent can compare these observations with earlier skills, teacher rollouts,
and training sets to decide whether to revise a skill, change its task coverage, or select different
demonstrations in the next round. This experiment history carries forward, while each student is
trained from θ0.

3.4
Infrastructure
The infrastructure lets the AutoMaster agent test how a skill shapes teacher behavior and examine
whether the student exhibits that behavior after fine-tuning (Figure 3). From its Docker container,
the agent uses the Model Context Protocol (MCP) (Anthropic, 2024) to access tasks and initial
trajectories, submit teacher rollout jobs, and launch SFT on selected demonstrations. The rollout
harness calls the teacher API, while the evaluation harness queries the student model served by
SGLang (Zheng et al., 2024). Both harnesses use a shared sandbox cluster that runs task tools and
verifiers in isolated Docker containers, allowing multiple tasks to execute concurrently.
When an SFT job completes, the execution controller checks that the checkpoint is available and
automatically schedules evaluation. The training and evaluation GPU clusters share a checkpoint
store: SGLang workers load the weights directly from the location where the SFT service wrote
them, avoiding an intermediate copy to the evaluation cluster. The controller also records job
and stage progress so that it can restart a failed inference worker and resume evaluation without
rerunning SFT.
Across rounds, AutoMaster links each student’s evaluation scores and trajectories to its checkpoint,
its training set, and the skills used during teacher rollouts. When a weakness recurs in student
evaluations, the agent can inspect earlier rollouts to see whether the teacher demonstrated the
desired behavior and check which of those demonstrations entered the training set. This evidence
informs whether the agent revises a skill, changes its data selection, or both in the next round.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and Task Pools.
We evaluate Terminal, Search, and software engineering (SWE) tasks
using Terminal-Bench 2.1 (Merrill et al., 2026), BrowseComp (BC; using the Lite-185 subset released
by Traverse) (Wei et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2026), and Doc2Repo (Chen et al., 2026b), respectively. For
each domain, we randomly sample 1,000 training tasks from public datasets (Meng et al., 2026;
Deng et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026b) to form a fixed task pool shared across all methods.
Models and Baselines. We use Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026) as students.
Across all experiments, the LLM agent runs in Codex with GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI, 2026) at xhigh
reasoning effort, while DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Preview (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) serves as the teacher at high
reasoning effort. We compare against Base, the student without additional fine-tuning, and Vanilla
SFT, which performs standard SFT on 512 trajectories randomly sampled from successful unguided
teacher rollouts on the shared task pool.
Training and Evaluation. For AutoMaster, the agent starts with trajectories generated by the
initial student and the unguided teacher on the training task pool, with a budget of five outer-loop
rounds. Across all settings, each training run starts from the corresponding initial checkpoint
and performs full-parameter SFT on 512 trajectories for three epochs, with a learning rate of 10−5
and a global batch size of 32. We evaluate final-epoch checkpoints without generated skills using
domain-specific harnesses (Meng et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b), with identical
settings across methods within each domain. We measure task accuracy on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and
BC and mean test pass rate on Doc2Repo, reporting mean ± SD over three evaluation runs per
checkpoint. Further experimental details are provided in Appendix A.

4.2
Main Results
Table 1 shows that AutoMaster achieves the highest mean score across all six benchmark–model
comparisons. Vanilla SFT improves over Base in every setting, demonstrating the effectiveness
of learning from successful teacher trajectories. Building on this baseline, AutoMaster yields
further gains of 1.42–8.99 percentage points with the same training set size and SFT recipe. Paired
confidence intervals for these gains are reported in Appendix A.6. The gains are larger for the 9B
model than for the 4B model across all three domains.
On Terminal-Bench 2.1, Vanilla SFT raises the 9B student’s accuracy from 25.47% to 26.59%. With the
same teacher, training task pool, number of demonstrations, and SFT recipe, AutoMaster reaches
35.58%. The modest gain from imitating successful unguided rollouts therefore understates what this student
can learn from the same teacher: changing how demonstrations are generated and selected yields a
further gain of 8.99 percentage points in mean accuracy.
4.3
Ablation Studies
To understand how AutoMaster improves on Vanilla SFT, we separately ablate skill guidance and
data selection. In w/o Skill Guidance, the LLM agent selects training trajectories from unguided
teacher rollouts. In w/o Data Selection, the agent designs and refines skills for a fixed, randomly
sampled subset of training tasks, without agent-directed trajectory selection. Both variants retain
autonomous experimentation with the same teacher, task pool, training set size, and SFT recipe.
Removing either component lowers mean performance in all six settings, but their relative con-
tributions vary (Figure 4). On Terminal-Bench 2.1 with the 4B student, skill guidance without
agent-directed selection yields higher mean accuracy than selection from unguided rollouts (20.22%
versus 17.60%). Both fall below Vanilla SFT (22.47%), whereas the full method reaches 28.09%. On
BrowseComp at the same model size, the ordering reverses: selection from unguided rollouts yields
45.77%, compared with 43.78% for guidance without selection. One possible explanation is that the
value of selection depends on what the teacher already demonstrates: selection can favor trajectories
that exhibit useful behaviors, while guidance can elicit behaviors that are rare in unguided rollouts.
To test whether the choice of training tasks alone explains the gains, we construct Task-Matched SFT
from unguided rollouts by the same teacher, matching both the task IDs and the per-task sample
counts of AutoMaster’s final training set. With task composition held fixed, AutoMaster achieves
higher mean scores in all six settings, by 1.08–10.11 percentage points. Across the four Terminal
and Search training corpora, both the unguided rollout and the selected AutoMaster demonstration

Analysis
5.1
Behavioral Shaping Beyond Task Success
Task success can conceal weaknesses in a teacher’s reasoning and actions. To examine the behaviors
targeted by Skill-FT, Figure 5 contrasts unguided and skill-guided teacher trajectories on the same
tasks across three domains.
The first two cases illustrate behavioral differences that task outcomes alone cannot distinguish.
In Figure 5(a), both executions satisfy the numerical error threshold, yet the unguided teacher
claims fourth-order accuracy despite measured convergence of approximately second order. The
guided teacher uses independent numerical checks to revise and validate its method. Similarly,
both search executions in Figure 5(b) reach the correct answer. The unguided trajectory repeatedly
pursues unverified award guesses before finding supporting records, while the guided trajectory
uses retrieved episode–director records to identify and verify a candidate. A filter based solely on
successful completion would leave these behavioral differences unexamined.
The third case highlights the importance of diagnosing verification failures. Both executions initially
construct a self-test with an input different from the specification while retaining the original
expected order. The unguided teacher reverses the global sorting rule to satisfy this mistaken
expectation. The guided teacher checks the input encoding, corrects the self-test, and preserves the
specified rule, passing 38 of 38 tests compared with 32 of 38. The useful behavior here is to establish
what a failed check actually implies before changing the implementation.

5.2
Preserving Skill Context for Better Internalization
We investigate how to help the student internalize skill-guided behaviors. Specifically, we examine
whether the skill instructions used to guide teacher rollouts should be retained during fine-tuning.
We compare Keep, which retains these instructions in the training input, with Remove, which omits
them. Both conditions use the same trajectories and SFT recipe. All models are evaluated without
generated skills, following the official evaluation protocols.
Removing the skill instructions leaves the supervised reasoning, responses, and tool calls unchanged
while making the training prompt more like the evaluation prompt, which contains no generated
skills. Yet students perform better when the training input retains the instructions that guided the teacher.

5.3
Autonomous Experimentation Across Rounds
Having examined how skill context affects learning from teacher demonstrations, we next investi-
gate how AutoMaster develops the interventions used to generate and curate those demonstrations.
The agent draws on accumulated rollout and training feedback to explore alternative behavioral
hypotheses and intervention strategies.
Figure 8 shows that continued experimentation identifies higher-performing candidates in all six
benchmark–model settings. The highest mean scores occur in rounds two through four, exceeding
first-round performance by 2.16–4.87 percentage points. Variation across rounds is consistent with
the exploratory protocol: the agent continues to investigate alternative interventions after finding a
strong candidate, without requiring each trial to improve on its predecessor. On BrowseComp-9B,
mean accuracy remains near 49% for the first three rounds before reaching 51.53% in the fourth.
This delayed improvement illustrates how continued experimentation can uncover further gains
after several rounds of limited progress.
The consistency of gains also differs across model sizes. The 9B model exceeds Vanilla SFT in mean
performance in every round across all three domains, whereas the 4B model does so throughout
all five rounds only on Terminal-Bench 2.1. The component ablations show a similar pattern: both
variants exceed Vanilla SFT in all three domains for the 9B model, but only on BrowseComp for the
4B model (Section 4.3). Together, these observations suggest that greater student capacity may support
more consistent gains from the interventions explored by AutoMaster.
6
Conclusion
We introduced Skill-FT for shaping teacher behavior with natural-language skills and AutoMaster,
an autonomous post-training system that designs, executes, and refines these interventions. Across
three domains and two model sizes, AutoMaster achieves higher mean performance than Vanilla
SFT. The results and trajectory analyses suggest that a fixed model can become a more effective
teacher through behavioral shaping, even on tasks it already solves. Retaining skill instructions
during SFT also yields higher mean performance than removing them, despite their absence at
evaluation, suggesting that preserving skill context can support better behavioral internalization.
