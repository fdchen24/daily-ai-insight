Abstract
Diffusion distillation accelerates image and video genera-
tion by compressing a pretrained teacher’s iterative sam-
pling process into a few network evaluations. Distribution-
based methods achieve this by matching the student’s out-
put distribution to the teacher’s.
This matching is often
driven by reverse-KL objectives, whose mode-seeking bias
favors dominant teacher modes at the expense of diversity
across samples. We introduce Posterior Matching Distilla-
tion (PMD), a new member of the distribution-based distil-
lation family that combines mode-covering posterior sam-
pling with any-step generation.
At zero population loss,
PMD recovers the teacher’s full posterior of clean images
at each trained noise level. Its stochastic student supports
any-step sampling budgets within the trained timestep grid
without further training. To learn these posteriors, PMD
matches exponentially reweighted averages of student sam-
ples conditioned on a fixed noisy state to corresponding
teacher posterior means. Under Gaussian corruption, ex-
ponentially tilted posterior mean is a single teacher evalua-
tion at a shifted input. We prove that, matching tilted means
over an open neighborhood uniquely identifies the full pos-
terior. PMD thus supervises the entire conditional distri-
bution through a family of mean constraints, without any
auxiliary student-score network. Experiments on SDXL and
FLUX.2 [klein] 4B demonstrate that PMD achieves the best
FID among 4-step methods on both models, while main-
taining competitive or leading performance across prompt-
alignment and perceptual-quality metrics.

1. Introduction
Diffusion and Flow models [2, 19, 30, 31, 53] have emerged
as a powerful approach to generative modeling, particularly
for images [10, 26, 27, 45, 57, 58] and video [1, 3, 25,
39, 55, 60], with growing applications to text [43]. Their
sampling process progressively transforms noise into a data
sample through a sequence of denoising steps, typically re-
quiring many neural network evaluations. Diffusion distil-
lation aims to reduce this computational burden by training
a student to generate samples in one or a few steps [4, 47–
49, 54, 61, 62].
However, a noisy state can correspond
to many plausible clean samples, while a conventional de-
noiser provides only a single estimate. This raises a funda-
mental question: what information about the teacher’s gen-
erative distribution should a few-step student preserve?
Existing diffusion distillation methods differ in what
they seek to preserve from the teacher. Trajectory-based
approaches aim to preserve the teacher’s sampling process
with fewer evaluations. These include progressive distil-
lation [48], which compresses successive denoising steps,
consistency distillation [54], which learns mappings to tra-
jectory endpoints, and flow-map distillation [4, 47], which
learns transitions between noise levels. Distribution-based
approaches, in contrast, seek to preserve the teacher’s gen-
erated distribution without requiring the student to follow
the same sampling trajectories.
Among them, Distribu-
tion Matching Distillation (DMD) [61, 62] aligns noise-
perturbed student and teacher distributions through score-
based objectives, while Moment Matching (MM) [49] en-
forces agreement between conditional expectations of clean
samples at intermediate noisy states.
Matching generated distributions does not necessarily
preserve the relationship between noisy inputs and clean
samples. A student may reproduce the teacher’s overall out-
put distribution while learning a different conditional distri-
bution at each noise level [61, 62]. This limitation is partic-
ularly relevant to Distribution Matching Distillation, which
uses an approximate reverse-KL objective to align noise-
perturbed student and teacher distributions.
The mode-
seeking nature of reverse KL can favor dominant modes at
the expense of less frequent outcomes, potentially reducing
sample diversity [32, 59]. Conditional supervision offers an
alternative [8]. Moment Matching compares the student’s
and teacher’s conditional means, but does so at a new noisy
state constructed from the student’s prediction, rather than
directly at its original input [49].
We introduce Posterior Matching Distillation (PMD), a
distribution-based distillation method that trains a stochas-
tic student to recover the teacher’s conditional distribution
of clean samples at each noise level. PMD matches ex-
ponentially reweighted averages of student samples condi-
tioned on a fixed noisy state to corresponding teacher poste-
rior means. In practice, stochasticity is introduced by sim-

• Direct posterior supervision.
We formulate a condi-

per tilt, avoiding expensive Monte Carlo sampling from

• Posterior identification and any-step sampling.
We

best FID among four-step methods.
2. Related Work
Trajectory-Based Distillation.
Progressive Distillation
[48] merges consecutive denoising steps, while Consistency
Models [54], Latent Consistency Models (LCM) [33], and
LCM-LoRA [34] learn mappings to trajectory endpoints.
Phased Consistency Models (PCM) [56] extend consis-
tency learning to shorter trajectory segments, while SDXL-
Lightning [28] combines progressive and adversarial dis-
tillation. Consistency Trajectory Models (CTM) [23] and
flow-map methods [4, 14, 17, 47] learn transitions between
arbitrary time pairs. Recent advances include Parallel De-
coding Distillation [52], targeting interval-averaged veloci-
ties, flexible sampling budgets, and parallel prediction, re-
spectively. On-policy approaches such as [7, 52, 64] imitate
teacher velocities along student trajectories , while Simpli-
fied Matching Distillation (SMD) [64] combines on-policy
velocity regression with a training-time residual proxy and
repulsion from weaker teacher predictions.
Distribution-Based Distillation.
Adversarial Diffusion
Distillation (ADD) [51] and Latent Adversarial Diffusion
Distillation (LADD) [50] use discriminator feedback for
few-step generation.
Distribution Matching Distillation
(DMD) [62] minimizes an approximate reverse-KL objec-
tive using teacher and auxiliary student score estimates,
while DMD2 [61] improves this framework through two-
timescale optimization, adversarial supervision, and mul-
tistep backward simulation. More recently, Reward-Tilted
Distribution Matching Distillation (RTDMD) [22] com-
bines distribution matching with reward-guided reinforce-
ment learning to improve human-preference alignment in
few-step generators.
Alternative objectives include f-
divergence matching in f-distill [59] and Fisher-divergence-
based distillation in Score Identity Distillation (SiD) [67].
In order to mitigate mode collapse, several works have com-
bined distribution matching with trajectory matching tech-
niques [16, 28, 62, 66]. Unlike methods that primarily align
generated marginals, PMD directly targets the teacher’s full
conditional posterior at each noisy input, providing super-
vision for preserving its multimodal structure.
Conditional Posterior Modeling. DDMs [8] train stochas-
tic denoisers to model conditional posteriors using proper
scoring rules, while iDDM [37] improves their computa-
tional efficiency. Moment Matching (MM) [49] instead dis-
tills a pretrained teacher by matching conditional means at
re-noised student predictions, using either an auxiliary de-
noiser or parameter-space optimization. PMD shares the
goal of posterior modeling but obtains supervision directly
from a frozen teacher through exponential tilting.
Exponential Tilting. Under Gaussian corruption a linear
exponential tilt is equivalent to shifting the noisy input at a
fixed noise level [9, 42]. This connection has been exploited
for reward-tilted sampling and alignment [40, 41] and for
reward guidance in discrete diffusion models [38]. Related
Gaussian-conditioning identities, in which the shift is ac-
companied by a change of noise level or covariance, appear
in transition sampling and inverse problems [20, 36]. Un-
like these works, PMD uses shifted evaluations of a frozen
teacher as training targets.
It matches a family of tilted
posterior means that uniquely identifies the full conditional
posterior, with each target obtained from a single teacher
evaluation rather than teacher posterior sampling.

4. Posterior Matching Distillation
4.1. The Tilt=Shift Identity
To obtain information beyond the posterior mean, we con-
sider an exponential tilt of the teacher’s conditional distri-
bution. Given a tilt vector λ ∈Rd, define
qλ(x | zt) = eλ⊤xq(x | zt)
Eq(x|zt)[eλ⊤x].
(4)
The factor eλ⊤x changes the relative probabilities of clean
images, giving greater weight to samples aligned with λ,
as illustrated in Fig. 3. Different tilts can therefore empha-
size different modes of the same posterior. Computing their
means would ordinarily require drawing multiple samples
from the teacher posterior and reweighting them. However,
the Gaussian corruption process admits a much simpler so-
lution.
Tilt=Shift Identity. For a fixed noise level with αt > 0
and σt > 0, define ρt = σ2
t /αt. Then, for any λ ∈Rd,
qλ(x | zt) = q(x | zt + ρtλ).
(5)
In particular, the mean of the tilted posterior is obtained
directly from the teacher denoiser:
Eqλ(x|zt)[x] = Dt(zt + ρtλ).
(6)
The identity shows that exponentially tilting the poste-
rior is exactly equivalent to shifting the noisy input to the
denoiser while keeping the noise level fixed. Consequently,
each tilted mean can be obtained through a single evalua-
tion of the frozen teacher at zt+ρtλ, without sampling from
the teacher posterior. The identity holds for any underlying
clean-data distribution under Gaussian corruption, assum-
ing an exact posterior-mean denoiser. Its proof is provided
in Appendix A.1.

4.4. Training PMD
We train PMD on noisy states encountered during the stu-
dent’s own sampling process. At each iteration, we select a
training noise level t and obtain zt through backward simu-
lation, starting from Gaussian noise zT . The preceding sam-
pling steps are performed without gradient tracking, and the
resulting zt is detached before computing the loss.
Given zt, we sample a tilt vector λ and evaluate the
frozen teacher at the shifted input zt+ρtλ. By the Tilt=Shift
Identity, this single evaluation provides the teacher’s tilted
posterior mean, Dt(zt + ρtλ). We then draw M indepen-
dent noise inputs u(1), . . . , u(M) and generate student pre-
dictions ˆx(m) = Gθ(zt, u(m), t). All predictions share the

4.5. Any-Step Sampling
PMD supports flexible sampling budgets without training
separate students for different numbers of steps.
Unlike
flow-map methods that explicitly learn transitions between
pairs of noise levels, PMD learns the conditional posterior
of clean images at each noise level. During sampling, the
student predicts a clean image from the current noisy state.
If another step is required, this prediction is re-noised to the
next selected level using the Gaussian corruption schedule,
and the student is evaluated again. The final prediction is
returned without re-noising.
5. Experiments
Models and baselines.
We evaluate Posterior Matching
Distillation (PMD) on text-to-image models from two fam-
ilies: SDXL [45] and FLUX.2 [klein] 4B [27]. Our pri-
mary setting uses four sampling steps. For SDXL, we com-
pare against LCM-LoRA [34], SDXL-Turbo [51], SDXL-
Lightning [28], Flash [5], PCM [56], DMD2 [61], and Mo-
ment Matching [49], as well as an undistilled teacher ref-
erence. For FLUX.2 [klein] 4B, the comparison includes
the base model, SMD [64], RTDMD [22], and Moment
Matching [49].
The number of network function evalu-
ations (NFE) is reported alongside generation quality to
make the inference budget explicit.
Implementation details. All experiments are conducted
on 8 NVIDIA L40 GPUs for 2000 student updates, with
a batch size of 16. We use a discriminator-to-generator up-
date ratio of 5:1. We use 4 student draws per training state.
We optimize with AdamW (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999), a learn-
ing rate of 5 × 10−7, gradient norm clipping at 10, and a
fixed GAN loss weight of 0.75. We initialize the student
from the undistilled pretrained model. To enable stochas-
ticity, we concatenate Gaussian noise-mixed and original
latent tokens, doubling the input projection dimension. Fur-
ther architectural details are provided in the Appendix C.
Evaluation metrics. We measure generation quality and
prompt adherence using GenEval [15], DPG-Bench [21],
OneIG-EN [6], HPSv3 [35], PickScore [24], and CLIP [46]
Score (CLIP-S), and report FID [18] as an additional distri-
butional quality measure. To assess intra-prompt diversity
independently of prompt adherence, we use the Vendi score
[12] and perceptual/feature-space diversity metrics based on
LPIPS [65], DINOv2 [44], and DreamSim [13]. Metric de-
tails are shared in Appendix C.4.
Qualitative Results. Fig. 5 provides qualitative compar-
isons of PMD against DMD2 on SDXL and RTDMD on
FLUX.2 [klein] 4B under identical text prompts. Across
both model families, PMD exhibits greater variation in sub-
ject appearance, scene composition, and backgrounds while
preserving the semantic content of the prompts. The fig-
ure also demonstrates PMD’s any-step capability: the same
trained student generates diverse samples with four and two
inference steps.
Additional results are provided in Ap-
pendix G.
Quantitative Results. Table 1 shows that PMD achieves
the best FID among the compared four-step methods on
both SDXL and FLUX.2 [klein] 4B, while leading in
OneIG-EN and PickScore on SDXL and HPSv3, PickScore,
and CLIP-S on FLUX.2. RTDMD [22], which uses ad-
ditional external reward supervision, achieves GenEval,
HPSv3, and PickScore scores of 0.9076, 15.59, and 23.99
on FLUX.2, but a substantially worse FID of 31.57 com-
pared to PMD’s 24.12.

We investigate two tilt-

and coarse, which samples directions at a lower spatial reso-

6. Conclusion and Limitation
We introduced Posterior Matching Distillation (PMD), a
distillation method that directly targets the teacher’s condi-
tional posterior through exponentially tilted means obtained
from single frozen-teacher evaluations. We prove that these
constraints uniquely identify the full posterior. Experiments
demonstrate the best diversity. While PMD provides di-
rect posterior supervision, it requires multiple student draws
during training and still relies on an auxiliary adversarial
loss to further improve visual realism.

C. Implementation Details
C.1. Shared Training Procedure
Initialization and stochastic inputs.
For both back-
bones, we initialize the student and frozen teacher from the
corresponding undistilled base model. The VAE and text
encoders remain frozen. At each student call, we mix the
current latent with independent Gaussian noise and concate-
nate this mixed latent with the original state. We widen only
the first input layer, retaining its pretrained weights and ini-
tializing the added raw-state weights to zero. The output
shape is unchanged. Independent noise draws make the stu-
dent stochastic; the added input channels preserve access to
the original conditioning state.
On-policy training and adversarial loss.
We obtain de-
tached training states by rolling out the current student. At
each selected noise level, the same four independent stu-
dent draws are reused across four tilted teacher queries,
using spatially coarse probes. We combine PMD with a
non-saturating logistic GAN loss of fixed weight 0.75. A
trainable discriminator head reads caption-conditioned fea-
tures from the frozen teacher. The discriminator uses in-
dependently noised real and generated latents at integer lev-
els sampled uniformly from {1, . . . , 999}, under each back-
bone’s native corruption schedule. Caption and reference-
image streams are sampled independently. We perform five
discriminator updates on fresh batches before each student
update, using global batch size 16 without student EMA.
To limit memory, we cache student outputs, compute their
loss gradients, and replay each draw with the same noise
for backpropagation. Tables 3 and 4 give the remaining set-
tings.

D.2. Tilt Norm
We ablate different tilt radii to understand how the strength
of exponential tilting affects posterior exploration and sam-
ple diversity. For small ∥λ∥, the exponentially tilted mean
admits the first-order expansion
E[ˆxeλ⊤ˆx]
E[eλ⊤ˆx] = E[ˆx] + Cov(ˆx)λ + O(∥λ∥2).
(80)
As ∥λ∥→0, the objective approaches ordinary mean
matching, with limited sensitivity to covariance and higher-
order posterior structure. Increasing the tilt radius strength-
ens the exponential reweighting, allowing PMD to probe
more pronounced variations and emphasize alternative
modes of the conditional posterior. However, excessively
large tilts can concentrate the exponential weights on a few
student samples, making finite-sample estimation less sta-
ble.
As shown in Table 5, larger tilt radii consistently improve
diversity, with U(1, 2) achieving the highest scores across
all four diversity metrics. In contrast, sampling radii from
a broader range, U(0.25, 2), achieves the best FID. These
results suggest a trade-off between exploring diverse poste-
rior modes and maintaining generation quality, motivating
the use of a mixture of smaller and larger tilt magnitudes.

The
Tilt=Shift Identity and the Posterior Identification Theo-
rem assume an exact posterior-mean denoiser, so all PMD
targets are evaluations of the frozen teacher at guidance
scale 1; a classifier-free-guided prediction is, in general,
not the posterior mean of any fixed clean-image distribu-

at zero PMD loss is therefore the teacher’s unguided con-
ditional posterior. Text-to-image teachers sampled without
guidance are known to produce broad but low-fidelity and
weakly prompt-aligned samples, and a student that matches
this posterior faithfully inherits both properties. The low
ImageReward and CLIP-S of the PMD-only student thus
reflect the unguided teacher distribution, and its diversity
scores should be read together with these quality scores,
since part of the measured variation comes from samples
that the guided teacher would not produce.

F. User Study Details
We conducted a human evaluation with 50 participants to
assess the perceptual quality of Posterior Matching Distil-
lation (PMD) against three baselines: SMD, RTDMD, and
FLUX.2 [klein]. The evaluation considered three criteria:
diversity, realism, and prompt alignment.
Evaluation Protocol.
We selected 24 text prompts and
generated four images per prompt using different random
seeds, resulting in 96 images per method. For each prompt,
the four generated images were presented together in an
image grid, allowing participants to assess variation across
samples conditioned on the same text description as shown
in 10. Participants were asked to rate each image grid along
three dimensions:
1. Diversity: How diverse are the generated images?
2. Realism: How realistic are the generated images?
3. Prompt alignment: How well do the generated images
align with the provided text prompt?
Each criterion was evaluated on a five-point Likert scale,
ranging from 1 (Very Bad) to 5 (Very Good).
For each
baseline, we compared its ratings with those of PMD and
computed the percentage of comparisons in which PMD re-
ceived a higher rating, an equal rating (tie), or a lower rat-
ing. The aggregated results are presented in Fig. 11.
Results.
PMD substantially improves sample diversity,
receiving higher ratings in 64%, 85%, and 66% of com-
parisons against SMD, RTDMD, and FLUX.2 [klein], re-
spectively, while receiving lower ratings in only 6%, 3%,
and 4%. For realism and prompt alignment, most compar-
isons result in ties, indicating broadly comparable perfor-
mance. PMD shows its strongest realism advantage over
RTDMD, winning 45% of comparisons versus 12% losses.
Overall, these results demonstrate that PMD significantly
enhances diversity while maintaining competitive realism
and prompt adherence.
