GroupChat-Bench: Benchmarking LLM Agents on
Long-Term Understanding of Realistic Group Chats
Jiho Kim1*, Jeewon Yang1*, Woosog Chay2, Hyeonji Hwang1, Daeun Kyung1,
Hyunseung Chung1, Eunbyeol Cho1, Yeonsu Kwon1, Yohan Jo2, Edward Choi1

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents are in-
creasingly expected to participate in persistent
group communication, yet existing dialogue
benchmarks largely focus on short, dyadic,
or single-session interactions. We introduce
GroupChat-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating
long-term understanding in scripted multi-party
dialogues that serve as a proxy for group chats.
Built from TV show scripts to avoid privacy
concerns, each conversation stream spans five
consecutive seasons and approximately 1,300
sessions. The benchmark tests whether agents
can track participant-specific information, inter-
personal relationships, and contextual changes
over time. Its question pools combine refined
fan-quiz-style questions with template-based
questions generated from temporal knowledge
graphs. Agents are evaluated as dialogue ses-
sions unfold chronologically, with access only
to the conversation history available when each
question is posed. To probe the influence of
memorized knowledge, GroupChat-Bench in-
cludes unanswerable questions and settings
with new or swapped character names. We
also propose GroupChat-Mem, which com-
bines structured memory with a raw session
repository. Experiments reveal limitations in
existing memory systems, while GroupChat-
Mem provides a stronger baseline. Code and
data are available at https://github.com/
jwyang21/groupchat_bench/.1

Introduction
Large language model (LLM) agents are increas-
ingly deployed in shared digital environments, in-
cluding household assistants used by multiple fam-
ily members and enterprise agents integrated into
collaboration platforms (Clark, 2025; Slack Tech-
nologies, 2026; Microsoft, 2026; Zoom, 2026). In

such settings, communication unfolds among multi-
ple participants over time, requiring agents to move
beyond one-to-one interaction models and engage
in sustained group communication.
Such group interactions exhibit three character-
istics that pose key challenges for LLM agents, as
illustrated in Figure 1. First, they require multi-
party grounding: agents must determine not only
what each utterance means, but also who it con-
cerns, how it is addressed, and how it fits within the
relationships among participants (Le et al., 2019;
Qamar et al., 2023). Second, group chats exhibit
temporal persistence: personal and relational in-
formation accumulates over time, changes, and can
be reinterpreted across sessions, requiring long-
term contextual knowledge at both individual and
group levels (Fono and Baecker, 2006; Zhang and
Cranshaw, 2018). Third, group conversations are
highly entangled: fragmented utterances, pile-on
reactions, and interleaved topical threads disrupt
clean turn-taking, requiring agents to disentangle
salient context from noisy exchanges (Elsner and
Charniak, 2008; Mehri and Carenini, 2017).
However,
existing
dialogue

benchmarks do not sufficiently reflect these proper-
ties. Many focus on short, single-session, or two-
party conversations (Li et al., 2017; Zhang et al.,
2018; Sun et al., 2019; Cui et al., 2020); longer-
context benchmarks often remain two-party or rely
on overly clean synthetic dialogues (Jang et al.,
2023; Maharana et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025); and
existing multi-party datasets are organized as inde-
pendent scenes or short episodes, leaving memory
over long-term group histories untested (Yang and
Choi, 2019; Poria et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020).
To address this gap, we propose GroupChat-
Bench, a benchmark for evaluating long-term un-
derstanding of scripted multi-party dialogue as a
proxy for group chats. We use TV show scripts to
provide human-authored, long-horizon interactions

Related Work
Dialogue Understanding Benchmarks
Existing
dialogue understanding benchmarks mainly focus
on short, single-session, or two-party conversa-
tions (Lowe et al., 2015; Li et al., 2017; Zhang et al.,
2018; Sun et al., 2019; Cui et al., 2020). Multi-
party dialogue datasets (Yang and Choi, 2019; Po-
ria et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020) model interac-
tions among multiple speakers, but are organized
as independent scenes or short episodes, leaving
long-term accumulated group histories unevalu-
ated. FriendsQA (Yang and Choi, 2019) shares
our source material, yet evaluates span extrac-
tion within single scenes (∼0.4k tokens) and re-
tains original character names; we instead pose
questions over chronologically accumulating histo-
ries (∼350k tokens) under contamination controls
(§3.1.6). SHARE (Kim et al., 2025a) also uses
scripted dialogue, but focuses on shared memo-
ries between two individuals across independent
films for response generation rather than long-
term multi-party QA. Earlier long-term memory
benchmarks (Jang et al., 2023; Maharana et al.,
2024; Wu et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026b) primar-
ily study dyadic or synthetic dialogue histories,
offering limited coverage of the fragmented, entan-
gled exchanges found in human-authored multi-
party dialogue (Table 1).

Agentic Memory Systems Agentic memory sys-
tems have been developed to enable LLM agents
to maintain information across long-term interac-
tions by storing, updating, and retrieving informa-
tion from past contexts. HippoRAG 2 (Gutiérrez
et al., 2025) continually indexes new information
into a knowledge graph and retrieves it via multi-
hop graph search based on Personalized PageR-
ank (Haveliwala, 2002), while Mem0 (Chhikara
et al., 2025) extracts salient facts from conversa-
tions and consolidates them through LLM-driven
add/update/delete decisions relative to existing
memories. A-MEM (Xu et al., 2025) instead or-
ganizes memory as a network of interlinked notes,
where new information triggers both the creation
of new links and the evolution of existing notes.
LD-Agent (Li et al., 2025) maintains event and
persona memories for personalized long-term di-
alogue, but focuses on dyadic response genera-
tion rather than tracking relationships among mul-
tiple observed participants. We benchmark general-
purpose memory systems that can be applied to
long-term multi-party dialogue streams to assess
their ability to handle this setting.

GroupChat-Bench
3.1
Data Construction
GroupChat-Bench was developed using scripts of
three popular TV shows (i.e., Friends, The Big
Bang Theory, and The Office2). These scripts were
first preprocessed to serve as dialogue data. Next,
questions were generated for each script, drawing
from fan quizzes and a temporal knowledge graph
(TKG). We then applied character style transfer
and anonymized character names using new names,
with an adversarial setting based on name swap-
ping. Figure 2 illustrates the construction process.
3.1.1
Script Preprocessing
Each script spans five consecutive seasons (∼20
episodes per season), with each episode composed
of multiple scenes.
Beyond utterances, scripts
contain non-verbal descriptions of characters’ ac-
tions, expressions, and scenes, as well as produc-
tion metadata (e.g., writer names). We remove all
such content to create Scriptpre, which consists
solely of speaker-attributed utterances, mirroring
a text-based group chat in which participants ob-
serve only each other’s messages; any information
conveyed non-verbally in the original show is thus
excluded from the dialogue stream and, by con-
struction, from the question pool (§3.1.2, §3.1.3).
We then selected scenes involving the main char-
acter (i.e., Friends: Ross, The Big Bang Theory:
Sheldon, The Office: Michael3) from Scriptpre
and sequentially numbered them as dialogue ses-
sions Si, resulting in the final dialogue D =

{S1, S2, ..., SN}. From this point onward, we re-
fer to these selected scenes as sessions. Each se-
lected session retains all speaker-attributed utter-

other participants, rather than only the main charac-
ter’s utterances. This session extraction preserves
a coherent dialogue flow from the main character’s
perspective while also enabling the construction
of unanswerable questions. Specifically, scenes
without the main character are excluded from the
agent’s observed dialogue stream but retained for
evidence annotation, allowing us to create ques-
tions about facts that exist in the script yet remain
inaccessible to the agent. Additionally, since some
of our questions involve time conditions, we man-
ually assigned a date to each session in D, deter-
mined from conversation content and episode air
dates; the specific assignment rules are detailed in
Appendix A.
3.1.2
Fan Quiz-Based Question Generation
We utilized a fan quiz website FunTrivia4 to gen-
erate our questions. Fan quizzes cover a range of
difficulty levels and focus on major events from
each episode, making them promising for evaluat-
ing dialogue comprehension. We began by extract-
ing episode-specific quizzes from the site. Since
these quizzes were created by dedicated fans, many
required knowledge unrelated to the dialogue itself
(e.g., “What is the name of the actor who played
the clerk?”). To filter out these questions, we first
selected quizzes that could be answered by refer-
encing Scriptpre using an LLM. The LLM also
annotated the evidence scenes in Scriptpre that
support each question.5 The authors then manually
corrected the LLM annotations for quiz filtering
and evidence-scene mapping. Note that since both
procedures are performed against Scriptpre, the
retained questions rely solely on the utterance text,
not on non-verbal information.
We then labeled each question as answerable or
unanswerable for each session, based on whether
the main character had observed all correspond-
ing evidence scenes before the query time. If the
evidence appeared only outside the main charac-
ter’s observed sessions (i.e., D), the question was
labeled unanswerable. We also generated temporal
variants by adding session dates to questions, en-
abling evaluation of long-term memory (e.g., “How

3.1.3
Temporal Knowledge Graph-Based
Question Generation
Fan quizzes are useful for generating our ques-
tions, but since they are episode-specific and
user-generated, the questions don’t span multiple
episodes and the number of questions is limited
(∼1k). To address this, we constructed a knowl-
edge graph for each session and used it to generate
questions. Initially, we used the LLM to extract
triples (i.e., [head, relation, tail]) from each session
Si in D. The authors then manually reviewed all
extracted triples and removed incorrectly extracted
ones. We employed 32 relations (e.g., girlfriend)
derived from DialogRE (Yu et al., 2020), a high-
quality dataset where human annotators manually
extracted relations from Friends scripts, classifying
relationships between characters into 36 categories.
We adapted and modified these relations for our
purpose. More details about the relations are pro-
vided in Appendix C.1. Finally, we combined the
triples from each session with their dates to create
a temporal knowledge graph (TKG) composed of
quadruples (i.e., [head, relation, tail, date]).
Using the TKG, we generated questions for each
session by sampling one-hop or two-hop quadru-
ples and checking whether the main character could
answer them at the query time. Because the ques-
tion form and answerability depend on when the
question is asked, the same fact can be phrased dif-
ferently across sessions: [Rachel, boyfriend, Ross,
1994-08-08] yields “Who was Rachel’s boyfriend
on August 8th, 1994?” when asked later, but “Who
is Rachel’s boyfriend?” when asked on that date.
If no prior evidence exists, the correct answer is
“I don’t know.” We manually verified the answers
and applied the same procedure to two-hop ques-
tions. Examples of question templates and gener-
ated questions are provided in Appendix C.2.
3.1.4
Answer Choices Generation
To create multiple-choice questions, we crafted
answer choices for each question. All questions
include the choice “(E) I don’t know.”, which is
the gold answer for unanswerable questions. For
fan-quiz questions, the four choices and correct an-
swers follow the original quiz. For TKG-based
questions, distractors are sampled from tails of
other quadruples sharing the same relation; to make

3.1.5
Question Style Transfer
Although fan quiz-based and TKG-based ques-
tions provide broad coverage, their quiz-like or
templated structure can result in artificial phras-
ing. To make the questions more natural, we apply
character style transfer, rewriting each question in
the distinctive tone of the corresponding charac-
ter so that the characters themselves appear to be
asking (e.g., Generic style: “How did Rachel buy
her new boots?”→Style of Joey Tribbiani from
Friends: “Hey, how did Rachel manage to snag
those killer boots, huh?”). This transformation is
powered by the LLM, and subsamples are reviewed
by the authors to ensure that the original intent
was preserved. More examples of style-transferred
questions for each character are in Appendix D.
All LLM-assisted procedures in the data con-
struction process were performed using GPT-
4 (OpenAI, 2023).
3.1.6
Character Name Transformation
To reduce agents’ reliance on prior knowledge of
the TV shows, we replace original character names
with new names unseen in the source shows (e.g.,
Joey →John); we refer to this setting as New
Names and use it as the default in all experiments
unless otherwise noted. We additionally create a
more adversarial Swapped Names setting by ex-
changing names among characters (e.g., Joey ↔
Monica), which makes memorized character knowl-
edge actively misleading, as it now contradicts the
dialogue context.
3.2
Agent Evaluation
The final dataset consists of the dialogue D and
session-specific candidate question sets. Specifi-
cally, D contains N dialogue sessions, and each
session Si is associated with a candidate question
set Qi. Each Qi contains question-answer pairs
(qi,j, ai,j), where qi,j is a question that can be asked
during Si and ai,j is the corresponding gold answer.
Agents process sessions in chronological order
and answer one question per session. At each ses-
sion Si, we sample a question-answer pair (qi, ai)
from Qi under a stratified sampling policy, target-
ing 50% fan-quiz questions and 20% unanswer-
able questions to ensure coverage of both question
sources and answerability conditions. Each ques-
tion is posed at a randomly sampled point within
the session, and the agent can use only the dialogue
history observed up to that point. We repeat this
procedure three times with independently sampled
question sets, using the same questions for all mem-

3.3
Statistics and Evaluation Formats
Table 2 presents the statistics of GroupChat-Bench,
including the number of dialogue sessions and the
average number of question candidates per ses-
sion. GroupChat-Bench supports both multiple-
choice and open-ended question formats for evalu-
ation. In the multiple-choice setting, each question
provides five answer choices. During evaluation,
unanswerable questions are sampled to constitute
20% of the test questions, balancing the five an-
swer choices rather than reflecting their natural fre-
quency in the candidate pool. In the open-ended set-
ting, we used the token-level F1/EM (Exact Match)
metrics from SQuAD (Rajpurkar et al., 2016) for
evaluation. The detailed evaluation protocol is pro-
vided in Appendix E.3.
3.4
Quality Control
To assess annotation quality, sampled instances
from each of the four components — quiz filter-
ing, evidence-scene mapping, triple extraction, and
style transfer — were independently re-annotated
by a reviewer who did not participate in the ini-

4
GroupChat-Mem
In long-running group chats, evidence relevant to
a query may be distributed across sessions, while
other queries hinge on the precise wording of a par-
ticular exchange. Retrieving only raw dialogue can
miss distributed evidence, whereas relying solely
on compressed records can omit conversational de-
tail (Han et al., 2026). To meet both requirements,
we develop GroupChat-Mem, a dual-view memory
baseline that maintains an automatically induced
relational memory alongside a raw session reposi-
tory, retrieves evidence from both, and uses them
jointly for response generation (Figure 3).

the entire (partial) session and replaces any ear-

later utterances resolve references or clarify prior
statements. The extracted records form a graph-
structured memory in which subjects and objects
serve as nodes and relations as edges, each times-
tamped with its source session. Shared nodes con-
nect records from different sessions, linking related
evidence across session boundaries. In parallel,
each original session Si is stored in S, preserving
exact wording, local discourse context, and con-
versational nuances that relational extraction may
omit (see Algorithm 1 in Appendix E.1 for details).
Note that R is induced online from the observed
dialogue only and is fully independent of the TKG
used for benchmark construction — it accesses
none of the TKG’s relation schema, verified triples,
or annotations.
Memory Retrieval and Response Generation
Given a user query, GroupChat-Mem retrieves ev-
idence from both memory components, R and S.
This parallel retrieval combines structured rela-
tional information from R with detailed conver-
sational context from S, without committing to a
single retrieval source in advance.
For retrieval from R, GroupChat-Mem first
prompts the LLM to identify the top-n query-
relevant nodes and constructs a two-hop neighbor-
hood around each selected node. The records con-
tained in these neighborhoods are then linearized
and ranked according to their cosine similarity to
the query (Karpukhin et al., 2020), using OpenAI
embeddings (OpenAI, 2024). The top-kr records
are retained as the initial relational evidence.
For retrieval from S, each dialogue session is
treated as a retrieval unit. Relevant sessions are
retrieved using a combination of lexical and seman-
tic matching. Specifically, BM25 (Robertson and
Zaragoza, 2009) captures keyword-level relevance,
while embedding-based cosine similarity captures
semantic relevance. GroupChat-Mem retrieves ks
sessions in total, with half selected according to
their BM25 scores and the other half according
to their embedding-based cosine similarity to the
query. The retrieved sessions serve as raw session
evidence for response generation.
Using the evidence retrieved from both mem-
ory views, GroupChat-Mem prompts the LLM to
generate an initial response and assess whether the
query can be answered with sufficient support. If
the retrieved evidence is sufficient, the response
is returned directly. Otherwise, GroupChat-Mem
initiates a fine-grained traversal over the induced
relational memory.
Among the nodes appearing in the initially
retrieved records, GroupChat-Mem prompts the
LLM to select the node most relevant to the query.
Starting from the selected node, GroupChat-Mem
expands the relational neighborhood one hop at
a time. After each expansion, the LLM attempts
to answer the query using the accumulated rela-
tional evidence and assesses whether the resulting
answer is sufficiently supported. The traversal ter-
minates when a supported answer can be generated
or when the maximum hop limit is reached. If the
available dialogue evidence remains insufficient,
GroupChat-Mem returns “I don’t know.” The com-
plete retrieval procedure is summarized in Algo-
rithm 2 (Appendix E.1).

Experiments
5.1
Experimental Setting
In our experiments, we used the multiple-choice
format to efficiently and accurately assess the
agent’s dialogue understanding capabilities. To
fairly compare GroupChat-Mem with other base-
lines, we used Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (Qwen Team,
2026), Gemma4-26B-A4B (Google DeepMind,
2026), and GPT-OSS 20B (OpenAI, 2025) as the
LLM backbones. Detailed experimental settings,
including the hyperparameters used in our experi-
ments, are provided in Appendix E.
5.2
Baselines
We compare GroupChat-Mem against three rep-
resentative agentic memory systems, along with
two simple RAG baselines. For all baselines, we
used the official implementations and tuned their
hyperparameters and prompts for evaluation on
GroupChat-Bench.
BM25 and OpenAI Embedding serve as represen-
tative simple RAG baselines, using sparse lexical
retrieval and dense semantic retrieval, respectively.
HippoRAG 2 (Gutiérrez et al., 2025) is a graph-
based retrieval framework that constructs associa-
tive memory over textual evidence and retrieves
query-relevant passages through graph search.
Mem0 (Chhikara et al., 2025) maintains long-term
agent memory by extracting, updating, and deleting
compact memory items from ongoing interactions,
rather than storing the full dialogue history as-is.
A-MEM (Xu et al., 2025) constructs an agentic
memory system by converting past interactions into
memory notes and dynamically linking them ac-
cording to semantic and contextual relevance.

Impact of Pretrained Knowledge
Because
LLMs may have prior knowledge of the TV shows
used in our benchmark, we replace character names
with new names by default to reduce reliance on
memorized information. Nevertheless, in Direct
QA without dialogue history, models scored above
the random-choice baseline (20%) and reached up
to 32.3%, indicating that name replacement alone
is insufficient. To further examine the effect of
character names, we compare two additional set-
tings: Original Names and the adversarial Swapped
Names (e.g., Joey ↔Monica). As shown in Table 4,
performance improves with Original Names but de-
clines below the New Names default with Swapped
Names. This suggests that swapped names not only
reduce the usefulness of memorized knowledge but
also make it actively misleading by conflicting with
the dialogue context.
Challenges in Long-Term Multi-Party Dialogue
Understanding
We analyzed baseline perfor-
mance by the number of participants in the evi-
dence sessions and by dialogue length. As shown
in Figure 4(a), performance decreases as the num-
ber of participants increases, demonstrating the
difficulty of tracking information and relationships
across multiple speakers. Figure 4(b) shows a sim-
ilar decline as dialogue history grows, indicating
that retrieving and integrating relevant evidence
becomes increasingly challenging over long-term
interactions. However, GroupChat-Mem degrades
more gracefully than the other methods, highlight-
ing its robustness to extended conversational his-
tories. These results identify speaker multiplicity
and dialogue length as key challenges in long-term
group-chat understanding and demonstrate the ad-
vantage of GroupChat-Mem in the latter setting.
Further Analyses
We additionally examine the
effect of question format, the contribution of each
memory component, and the behavior of the in-
duced relational memory. First, evaluation for-
mat matters: GroupChat-Mem accuracy drops
by 13–29 points across backbones when moving
from labeled MCQ (choices given as explicit op-
tions) to open-ended evaluation (e.g., 77.8→64.5
for Qwen3.5; Appendix E.3.2). Because answer
choices bound the output space, the two formats
probe complementary abilities: labeled MCQ of-
fers reliable automatic scoring of discriminative un-
derstanding, motivating its use as our default, while
open-ended evaluation measures generative recall
and leaves considerable headroom. Second, com-
ponent ablations (Appendix F.2) show that both
memory views are necessary, though to different
degrees: removing the session repository is most
damaging (-19.8 points on average), as compressed
records omit the precise wording that many queries
hinge on, while removing the relational memory
costs 4.6 points, as evidence linked across distant
sessions can no longer be resolved. Disabling the
iterative graph traversal further degrades perfor-
mance by 1.5 points, confirming its role in incre-
mentally gathering additional evidence when ini-
tial retrieval is insufficient. The appendix also re-
ports a breakdown of accuracy by question type
(Appendix E.4), the distribution of gold answer
choices (Appendix E.5), and qualitative examples
showing how GroupChat-Mem retrieves evidence
across session boundaries (Appendix G).
6
Conclusion
We introduced GroupChat-Bench, a benchmark for
evaluating long-term multi-party dialogue under-
standing over chronologically accumulating group-
chat histories. The benchmark tests whether LLM
agents can track participant-specific information,
evolving relationships, and contextual changes
across extended interactions. We also proposed
GroupChat-Mem, which combines relational mem-
ory with a raw session repository. Experiments re-
veal limitations in existing memory systems, while
GroupChat-Mem provides a stronger and more ro-
bust baseline for long dialogue histories.
Limitations
Scripted dialogue as a proxy for group chats.
Ideally, GroupChat-Bench would be built from
naturally occurring group communication, such
as corporate Slack channels or workplace meet-
ing transcripts. However, such data is difficult to
access and release: it raises serious privacy and
consent concerns for the participants, and is typ-
ically treated as proprietary corporate assets. We
therefore use TV scripts as a proxy, since they pro-
vide human-authored, long-horizon multi-party di-
alogue with persistent character relationships, free
of these constraints. To partially compensate for
the lack of real-world data, we selected shows
that cover diverse everyday contexts, including in-
terpersonal relationships (Friends), academic set-
tings (The Big Bang Theory), and workplace in-
teractions (The Office), though they remain con-
strained to narrative-driven dialogue. These scripts
retain key conversational phenomena such as frag-
mented turn-taking, pile-on reactions, and topic
shifts across speakers (Figure 1), but they do not
capture some surface characteristics of text-based
messaging, such as typos, emojis, or asynchronous
short reactions. We expect the core challenges eval-
uated by GroupChat-Bench (multi-party ground-
ing, temporal persistence, and evidence distributed
across noisy exchanges) to transfer to such settings,
though extending the benchmark with naturally oc-
curring (appropriately consented) chat data is a
natural next step.
Data contamination.
Since the source shows
likely appear in pretraining corpora, we mitigate
contamination by replacing character names with
unseen ones (the New Names setting), adversarially
swapping names among characters (the Swapped
Names setting), and including unanswerable ques-
tions. We further quantify residual leakage directly
via the Direct QA baseline (Section 5.3). While
these measures make memorized knowledge unreli-
able or even misleading for solving the benchmark,
they cannot rule out that models exploit memorized
plot structure. Constructing a comparable bench-
mark from dialogue guaranteed to be unseen during
pretraining remains an open challenge for the field.

A
Date Assignment
We first extracted elements from the scripts that
could indicate dates (e.g., Valentine’s Day, Christ-
mas Eve). Then, we reviewed the scripts again
to analyze the relative timing of the sessions. For
example, if there is a line mentioning that Chan-
dler broke up with his girlfriend two days ago, we
annotated the session where he broke up with his
girlfriend as occurring two days prior to the men-
tioned session. Next, while watching each episode,
we pinpointed sessions where the dates might have
changed by observing whether the characters’ out-
fits changed between sessions. Finally, we assigned
a specific date to each session based on the actual
broadcast date of the episode, adjusting for the rel-
ative differences in dates and events such as Christ-
mas.
B
Question Generation Based on Fan
Quizzes
For each scene si,k from episode pi in Scriptpre,
we define the set of answerable questions as
FanAi,k and the set of unanswerable questions
as FanUi,k. The process of generating questions
based on fan quizzes is as follows.
First, we collected quizzes for each season
and episode of Friends, The Big Bang Theory,
and The Office from the FunTrivia website. For
each episode pi in Scriptpre, we used GPT-4
to determine if the crawled questions CrQi =
{qi,0, qi,1, ..., qi,l} could be answered using only
pi. If a question qi,m could be answered, GPT-4
identified the scenes ESi,m that provide evi-
dence for the answer, compiling them into Qi =
{(qi,m, ESi,m)}l
m=0. Subsequently, the authors re-
viewed each ESi,m, made necessary corrections,
and annotated whether a single scene from ESi,m
was sufficient to answer qi,m or if multiple scenes
were needed to be considered simultaneously. For
each si,k within pi, we assessed the answerability
of the questions in Qi.
For each si,k, if a question qi,m could be an-
swered using just one scene, and si,k occurs af-
ter the initial appearance of the main character in
ESi,m, we included qi,m in FanAi,k. This ensures
that the main character had adequate exposure to
the relevant evidence. Additionally, for questions
requiring verification across multiple scenes, if the
main character appears in all ESi,m scenes and si,k
occurs after the last scene of ESi,m, we included
qi,m in FanAi,k. If the main character does not ap-
pear in any of the ESi,m scenes, qi,m was included
in FanUi,k since the main character has not expe-
rienced any evidence to answer the question. The
rest are not included in the dataset as it is unclear
whether they are answerable per scene. Addition-
ally, to generate questions that require long-term
memory, we added the most recent date of the evi-
dence scenes for each question.

E.1
Algorithm
This section explains the full GroupChat-Mem al-
gorithm in detail. Algorithm 1 describes how the
two memories, R and S, are constructed. Extrac-
tion always takes the complete partial session Si,j
as input rather than the latest utterance alone, so
references resolved later in the session are reflected
in the resulting records. A query posed at turn
j is answered over the point-in-time views R(i,j)
and S(i,j), which add the current partial session to
what was committed before Si; records from differ-
ent sessions are connected, linking evidence across
session boundaries.
Algorithm 2 details retrieval and response gen-
eration. When the initial answer over the parallel
evidence of Stage 1 is “I don’t know.”, Stage 2 re-
peatedly asks the LLM which entity to look up next
and expands that entity’s relational neighborhood,
re-answering after each expansion. The traversal
stops when an answer is produced, when the LLM
reports no further entity to examine, or when the
hop limit H is reached, in which case GroupChat-
Mem returns “I don’t know.”

First, we provide multiple-choice questions in
both template and natural language formats. For ex-
ample, a template-based question might be, “Who
was going out with Paul in September 1994?”
with choices “(A) Emily, (B) Monica, (C) Ryan,
(D) Rachel, (E) I don’t know”. In contrast, the
same question in natural language format could
be phrased as, “Who was going out with Paul in
September 1994? Was it Emily, Monica, Ryan,
Rachel, or do you not know?”
Additionally, we offer the option to ask questions
in an open-ended format (e.g., “Who was going out
with Paul in September 1994?”) without provid-
ing answer choices. This approach allows us to
evaluate the agent’s ability to generate open-ended
responses. The open-ended format is particularly
useful for fan quiz-based questions, where some an-
swers may require longer responses (e.g., Question:
“Why did Monica and Chandler say they were late
getting to the hospital?” Correct answer: “Monica
went back for her jacket”).
For natural language multiple-choice and open-
ended questions, we used a generative reading-
comprehension protocol rather than exact multiple-
choice label matching. Since the model produces
free-form answers, we adopt the official SQuAD
v1.1 evaluation (Rajpurkar et al., 2016): each an-
swer is normalized (lowercasing, punctuation and
article removal, whitespace normalization) and
scored against every candidate option using token-
level F1 and exact match.
E.3.1
Choices in Multiple-Choice Questions
The number of questions based on fan quizzes was
significantly smaller than the questions based on
the TKG. Thus, 50% of the questions were in-
tentionally extracted from the fan quiz-based dur-
ing the simulation. Since each question has five
choices, unanswerable questions were set to com-
prise 20% of the total to fairly stratify the correct
answers.

Ablation Study
F.1
Hyperparameter Ablation
To determine the values of each hyperparame-
ter used in Groupchat-Mem, we conducted grid
searches. In detail, the performance of Qwen3.5
was evaluated on the even sessions of Friends
dataset with new names.
Table 17 shows that
(kr, ks) = (250, 40) is an optimal combination
among all the candidates that we tried. The per-
formance of LLM tends to improve as kr and ks
increase, but it saturates beyond a certain point.
Table 18 shows that using n = 1 results in the
highest performance, closely followed by n = 3.
Considering that seed nodes (i.e., the query-related
candidates extracted by LLMs) act as starting point
for traversing the relational memory, using n = 1
could be riskier than using n = 3; a single mis-
match leaves no seed nodes and thereby retrieves
no additional relational evidence in n = 1, whereas
two other candidates still remain in n = 3. There-
fore, we finalized 3 as a value of n.

Qualitative Analysis
G.1
Comparing retrieved evidence from
GroupChat-Mem and Baseline Methods
To understand the source of GroupChat-Mem’s
gains over the three baselines (HippoRAG 2, A-
Mem, Mem0), we compare the evidence each mem-
ory system retrieves for a fixed question. We con-
sistently observe that the evidence required to an-
swer the question is retrieved only by GroupChat-
Mem, while it is absent from the retrievals of most
baselines (at least two of the three). Figure 5 illus-
trates a representative case: for the question "Who
Figure 5: GroupChat-Mem retrieves the dated gold memory and answers correctly, whereas all three baselines
retrieve many dialogue sessions that do not contain the gold entity and therefore fail.
was the made-up childhood friend John used to
talk about?", GroupChat-Mem retrieves the gold
relational evidence [John, imaginary childhood
friend name, Maurice, January 15, 1998] in a
compact, structured form and answers correctly.
In contrast, A-Mem, HippoRAG 2, and Mem0
each retrieve lots of unstructured dialogue that does
not contain the answer entity (Maurice), and there-
fore all fail. This suggests that GroupChat-Mem
could retrieve more accurate and answer-bearing
evidence compared to all baselines.
G.2
Visualization of the Induced Relational
Memory
Figure 6 visualizes the relational memory R in-
duced by GroupChat-Mem, centered on one main
character from the Friends dataset under the New
Names setting, with snapshots sampled at three
different time points. The visualization illustrates
two properties of R. First, relational evidence is
consolidated into a structured form rather than dis-
persed across multiple sessions. Second, the in-
formation associated with each character is con-
tinually updated as the dialogue progresses. This
representation reduces the burden on the LLM of
comprehending long, unstructured session histo-
ries, and makes gold evidence more explicit and
easily inspectable.
