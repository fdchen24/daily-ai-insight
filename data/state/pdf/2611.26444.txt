A BST R ACT
Embodied intelligence aims to enable robots to perform complex physical tasks with human-like adaptability
and reliability, which requires three complementary capabilities: history-based cognitive reasoning, precise
and timely physical execution, and the composition of reasoning and execution through environmental
feedback. Yet existing benchmarks often evaluate reasoning and execution within the same tasks, or evaluate
them in separate assessments without examining their composition, thus providing limited insight into
whether failures arise from reasoning, physical execution, or their composition. To fill this gap, we introduce
Mind2Act World, a diagnostic benchmark comprising 30 simulated manipulation tasks across five real-world
application domains. Each task features three difficulty levels and two scene settings (Clean and Random),
yielding 180 configurations. These tasks are organized into three views: Mind emphasizes decisions based
on historical observation, Act assesses precise and timely execution, and Mind2Act integrates reasoning and
physical execution in closed-loop tasks, where action outcomes guide subsequent decisions. By contrasting
performance across the three views, Mind2Act World reveals whether a system lacks a capability or fails to
compose the capabilities it already has. Furthermore, we develop a diagnosis harness that attributes the failed
episodes of three representative systems to Mind, Act, or their composition and summarizes each system in a
per-model report called a profile card. Our evaluation of multimodal agents, vision-language-action models
(VLAs), and world-action models (WAMs) shows that competence in a single view does not carry over to
Mind2Act tasks. Even the strongest agent succeeds in 52.7% of Mind and 39.0% of Act episodes but in only
12.0% of Mind2Act episodes. The strongest agent retains task state but struggles with sustained contact,
whereas end-to-end policies execute practiced motions but retain little task state. These findings point to the
composition of reasoning and execution through environmental feedback as the main open problem.
1 Introduction
“Knowledge is the beginning of action and action is the completion of knowledge.”
— Wang Yangming
The central ambition of embodied intelligence is to enable robots to perform complex physical tasks with
human-like adaptability and reliability. Studies of human sensorimotor control indicate that adaptive
and reliable manipulation involves reasoning over task-relevant information, executing physical actions
precisely, and adapting behavior through environmental feedback [1–3]. In the cooking task of Fig. 1,
a robot must remember which ingredient to add next, coordinate both arms to add the ingredient, and
switch the stove from heating to keep-warm mode based on temperature feedback. These requirements
motivate three capabilities. Mind decides what to do from earlier observations. Act produces the required
physical effect in time. Mind-Action Composition closes the loop, so that remembered information
guides each action and each physical outcome updates the next decision.
Recent embodied systems spanning multimodal agents [32], vision-language-action models (VLAs) [19,
20], world-action models (WAMs) [23, 26], and hybrid architectures [27, 28] have substantially advanced
robotic manipulation. However, their capabilities in Mind, Act, and Mind-Action Composition remain
poorly understood, which makes it difficult to identify capability bottlenecks and guide further development.
Two limitations of existing embodied benchmarks [4, 6–8, 12] explain why. First, conventional benchmarks
often assess cognitive reasoning and physical execution within the same tasks and report only whether a task
succeeds [4–6], so a failure cannot be traced to Mind or Act. Second, although recent benchmarks have
introduced finer-grained diagnostics and separate assessments of reasoning and physical execution [8, 11–
13], they rarely evaluate Mind-Action Composition, particularly whether physical outcomes can effectively
inform subsequent decisions. Closing this gap requires tasks that stress one capability while keeping the
others present but easy, a judgment of composition based on verified physical outcomes, and a comparison
of systems with different action interfaces and inference speeds on equal terms.
To this end, we introduce Mind2Act World, a diagnostic benchmark for Mind, Act, and Mind-Action
Composition in robotic manipulation (Fig. 1). Its 30 tasks come from five real-world application domains
and are organized into three views of ten tasks each, covering 12 capability categories. Each view
emphasizes one demand by design. Mind tasks keep physical operations simple, Act tasks state the

required operation explicitly, and Mind2Act tasks chain dependent operations so that each physical
outcome decides what comes next. Success is judged from physical predicates on the environment, and the
evaluator records stages and events within each episode. Each task has three difficulty levels that raise its
demands while keeping the objective fixed. Each level runs in a Clean and a Random scene, which share
the same instance but differ in clutter, materials and lighting (180 configurations in total). Agents and
end-to-end policies share the same tasks, information contract and evaluator, and 4,500 demonstrations
(75.8 hours) are provided for fine-tuning end-to-end policies. A diagnosis harness combines rule-based
analysis of the evaluator’s stage and event records with an LLM judge to attribute each failed episode of
three representative systems to Mind, Act, or their composition. It summarizes each of these systems
in a per-model report called a model profile card (Fig. 6; Appendix E.4).
Our evaluation on Mind2Act World reveals a clear pattern. Existing architectures are uneven across
Mind and Act, yet all struggle with Mind-Action Composition. The strongest agent retains task state
well but struggles to sustain contact, and its timed successes are measured with a simulator that pauses
during inference. End-to-end policies respond within a fraction of a second and execute practiced motions,
but retain little task state, and the memory of the VLAs largely disappears under scene randomization.
Notably, on Mind2Act, the strongest system succeeds in only 12.0% of episodes, far below its 52.7%
on Mind and 39.0% on Act, and no end-to-end policy exceeds 3.6%. Competence in either capability
therefore does not carry over to Mind2Act tasks.
The contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
• We introduce Mind2Act World, a diagnostic benchmark comprising 30 manipulation tasks grounded
in real-world requirements. Organized into three views, it systematically evaluates Mind, Act, and
Mind-Action Composition.
• We develop a diagnosis harness that combines rule-based analysis with an LLM judge to identify
the stage at which each failed episode first breaks down and to attribute the failure to Mind, Act,
or Mind-Action Composition. The harness also summarizes three representative models in model
profile cards of their failure patterns and behavioral tendencies.
• We evaluate representative multimodal agents, VLAs, and WAMs on shared tasks and scoring, with
separate leaderboards for agents and end-to-end policies. Our results show that systems differ in which
of Mind and Act is weaker, and that all of them struggle with Mind-Action Composition.
2 Related Work
2.1 Embodied Policies and Agents
Robot manipulation policies have evolved from imitation learning [15–17] to large-scale generalist policies.
Vision-language-action models such as OpenVLA [19], 𝜋0.5 [20], RDT2 [21] and G0.5 [22] improve
instruction following and cross-task transfer, while world-action models such as LDA-1B [24], Motus [25]
and DreamZero [26] couple predictive modeling with control. Agent-based systems take a different route,
turning language-model reasoning into executable programs [32] and feeding success detection back into
planning [67, 68]. Recent work also addresses memory-augmented manipulation [29, 30, 55], dynamic
manipulation [61, 62], and force- or tactile-aware control [65, 66].
Despite this progress, each line is usually evaluated where only its own demand is hard: memory methods
on tasks with simple execution, dynamic and contact-aware controllers on tasks whose target is given,
and agents on tasks where perceiving the outcome is assumed. In practice, a failure may come from
holding the wrong task state, missing the intended physical effect, or continuing without checking that
the effect occurred. Mind2Act World therefore makes all three demands hard within the same tasks
and evaluates agents, VLAs and WAMs on them.

2.2 Evaluation for Manipulation and Embodied Agents
Simulation benchmarks provide scalable testbeds for manipulation. RLBench [5], CALVIN [33] and
LIBERO [4] cover multi-task, language-conditioned and lifelong settings. RoboTwin 2.0 [34] evaluates
bimanual generalization, and THE COLOSSEUM [48] stresses distribution shift. BEHAVIOR-1K [7]
and RoboCasa [6] pose long household activities. Memory benchmarks such as RMBench [31] and
RoboMME [38] isolate history-dependent decisions, the latter with Easy, Medium and Hard levels
and timed stopping of moving objects. GeoMind [36] evaluates spatial reasoning and spatial memory
through structured manipulation tasks, with controlled scene variations and diagnostic metrics for
spatial understanding and execution failures. RoboDojo [8] and RobotWorld [9] diagnose capability
dimensions with stage scores or execution traces, and real-world benchmarks such as RoboArena [41]

5

assess deployed policies. For agents built on foundation models, EmbodiedBench [52] spans high-level
and low-level embodied tasks, and Embodied Playground [11] evaluates closed-loop behavior in which
earlier actions set up later prerequisites. X2Real [12] records subtask failure nodes, separates reasoning
from manipulation dimensions including dynamic operation, and compares VLA, WAM and agent routes.
RoboColiseum [13] reports a gap between task understanding and reliable execution. RLE-Bench [10]
and Embodied First Exam [14] instead evaluate agents that write and iterate on code to solve robot
control and robot learning engineering problems.
Most of these benchmarks entangle reasoning and execution within one task, so a failure cannot be attributed
to either, or measure them in separate evaluations, leaving open whether they hold together when one
realized outcome sets the conditions for the next decision. Mind2Act World addresses this gap by deriving
tasks from real-world requirements and organizing them into Mind, Act and Mind2Act views with graded
difficulty, so that the same demands are measured in isolation and in composition across architectures.
Table 1 compares Mind2Act World with 21 representative simulation benchmarks. Mind2Act World tasks
also have long horizons, with training demonstrations averaging 60.7 s overall and 99.6 s in the Mind2Act
view. Demonstrations in RoboTwin 2.0, RoboDojo, MIKASA-Robo-VLA and RoboCasa365 average 13 to
40 s, and only BEHAVIOR-1K, whose tasks are full household activities, has longer ones.
3 Mind2Act World Benchmark
Mind2Act World asks whether failures in manipulation tasks arise from incorrect reasoning, imprecise
execution, or a poor composition of the two. The benchmark comprises 30 simulation tasks on a bimanual
ARX X5 platform, organized into 3 views that place different demands on a system. Mind focuses on
history-dependent reasoning, Act emphasizes constrained physical execution, and Mind2Act focuses on
their composition. Each task has Easy, Medium, and Hard levels, and each level is evaluated in a Clean
and a Random scene setting, yielding 180 configurations. Fig. 2 presents an overview of our 30 tasks.
The following sections describe the design principles (Section 3.1), task views (Section 3.2), difficulty
levels (Section 3.3), training data (Section 3.4), and evaluation setting (Section 3.5).
3.1 Design Principles
Four principles guide the benchmark design: (i) build tasks from real-world requirements, (ii) evaluate
reasoning and execution capabilities separately and in combination, (iii) verify physical outcomes and
record task progress, and (iv) support evaluation across different model architectures.
(i) Tasks are built from real-world requirements. Embodied agents in industry, homes, and service
settings must follow instructions across multiple stages and produce the required physical effects at
the right time. We construct tasks in three steps. First, we identify a practical requirement, such as
remembering orders after they leave view, maintaining a target instrument reading, intercepting objects
on a conveyor, or responding to appliance feedback. We then reproduce this requirement in a tabletop
scene and define completion conditions based on physical outcomes. Table 2 groups the tasks into five

3.2 Task Design
Task design follows the three views introduced above. Mind tests decisions that depend on earlier
information (Section 3.2.1). Act tests whether a specified operation produces the required physical
effect within spatial and temporal constraints (Section 3.2.2). Mind2Act tests their composition across
dependent operations (Section 3.2.3). Within each view, tasks are grouped by capability. The following
subsections explain each category through representative tasks and describe the failures it can reveal.
Appendix Table 20 lists all tasks, their source workflows, categories, and difficulty variables. Appendix F
provides the full specifications.
3.2.1 Mind: Reasoning over Long-Horizon Dependencies
A decision may depend on information that is no longer visible, such as a part on an earlier tray, the effect
of an unlabeled control, or the portion of an order already served. These dependencies require a system to
retain evidence and update task state throughout an episode. Many end-to-end visuomotor policies use only
the current observation or a short history of recent frames [15, 16, 19, 20]. Recent memory-augmented
policies have begun to address this limitation [29, 30, 55, 56]. Mind tasks examine whether a system can
make correct decisions after relevant cues disappear, while requiring only routine execution.
Memory categories. The ten Mind tasks cover five memory categories, with two tasks per category. The
categories differ in how earlier information supports later decisions. Entity memory retains an object’s
identity or an associated value when its current appearance is insufficient. Feedback-rule learning uses
interaction to infer and retain a rule or hidden configuration. Examples include learning the effects of
unlabeled controls and recovering an arrangement from feedback that reports only the number of correct
positions. Process memory tracks completed operations and their order, such as fulfilled requests or the
original order of disassembled layers. Spatial memory retains locations when a reference frame moves
or a map is hidden. Integrated memory combines identity, location, and order, as in tracking a covered
workpiece or matching cards revealed two at a time. Difficulty increases the information to retain, such as
bindings, layers, hazard cells, card pairs, or exchanges, while keeping precision demands low.
Representative cases. We demonstrate representative cases in Fig. 3. In workpiece tracking and route
replay (M1), the robot remembers the target location and station route, tracks the hidden target through
cover exchanges, and retrieves it to visit the required stations in order. This tests integrated memory
of identity, location, and sequence. In steamer-stack order restoration (P2), the robot observes each
basket’s contents as it unstacks the baskets, then restores their original order and replaces the lid. On
Hard, distractor baskets have identical exteriors but different contents. The robot must retain the original

mmlab@SIGS
layer order and select the correct baskets, testing process memory.
What Mind diagnoses. Mind task records distinguish decision errors from execution errors, such as
choosing the wrong item versus failing to place the correct one. Because execution is routine, an error
on a Mind task usually comes from the task state a system maintains.
3.2.2 Act: Dynamic Response and Precise Physical Execution
Correct decisions must be translated into physical effects that meet spatial and temporal constraints.
Common examples include intercepting conveyor objects, passing through cyclic gates, maintaining
instrument readings, carrying loose loads, and folding fabric. Act tasks specify what to do and evaluate
how it is executed. Act provides a reference for execution demands that also occur in Mind2Act workflows,
such as conveyor pickup in meal assembly. This allows performance on separate operations to be
compared with performance in a dependent workflow.
Execution categories. Dynamic response concerns the timing of an action’s effect relative to a changing
scene. Examples include intercepting objects before they leave the reachable region, pressing a button
within a cue window, and passing through a cyclic gate before it closes. Dynamic stability adds a related
demand. The robot must control its own motion without disturbing a carried load, such as loose balls on a
tray. Timing follows the simulation clock, which pauses during inference. Precise physical execution
concerns spatial accuracy, contact, and stability. It includes setting instrument readings, maintaining tool
contact along a path, controlling stamp orientation, folding cloth that remains flat after release, and spacing
dominoes to complete a physical chain to a bell. Difficulty increases through shorter time windows,
additional obstacles or loads, tighter tolerances, and less controllable materials or shapes.
Representative cases. Cases are demonstrated in Fig. 3. In domino chain to a bell (PR6), the robot
places dominoes upright along a curved band and pushes the first to trigger a chain that rings a bell.
This requires precise spacing and orientation so that contact propagates through the chain. Difficulty
increases with more dominoes, greater height variation, and a larger band span. In tray carrying with
loose balls (DR4), the robot carries loose balls on a tray past obstacles while keeping each ball within
its marked circle. This tests dynamic stability, as the robot must control tray motion to preserve the
positions of the balls throughout transport.
What Act diagnoses. Act tasks assess endpoint accuracy, stability after release, contact continuity,
and timing. By limiting long-horizon reasoning demands, they provide a reference for interpreting
execution failures in Mind2Act workflows.
3.2.3 Mind2Act: Composition across Dependent Operations
Mind2Act evaluates how reasoning and execution interact across a workflow. Retained information, such
as an order, layout, or recipe sequence, determines the next operation and its execution requirements.
Each physical outcome then changes the task state on which later decisions depend. A system may
handle the separate demands yet struggle to combine them over time. Mind2Act therefore focuses
on outcome-conditioned continuation, that is, choosing subsequent actions from the state produced
by earlier operations. Each task combines a memory or reasoning demand with a physical execution
demand, as listed in Appendix Table 20.
Composition mechanisms. The tasks cover five composition mechanisms. In cumulative progress (CP01,
CP03), remaining demand or slot occupancy determines the next operation. Progress advances only after
successful delivery or stable plate placement. In state-dependent prerequisites and repair (CP02, CP08,
CP09), later operations require a valid physical state. The robot may need to stop a turntable before
covering a cake, rebuild a misplaced base to match a remembered blueprint, or redraw original strokes
removed during erasing. In feedback-driven adjustment (CP04, CP06), temperature or balance-beam

Representative cases. In rotating-cake decoration (CP02), the robot remembers a briefly shown macaron
layout and reproduces it on a rotating cake. It must stop the turntable and verify that the cake is stationary
before placing the cover. In temperature-controlled cooking (CP04), the robot adds ingredients in the

strokes. It must erase these strokes and redraw any original content removed during erasing, so that the
final board matches the remembered colors, shapes, and layout.
What Mind2Act diagnoses. Mind2Act examines whether a system uses retained information to guide
execution and updates that information from physical outcomes. Comparisons across views, supported
by stage and event records, help identify composition failures.
Difficulty inside the process. Task difficulty often arises during intermediate operations. Fig. 3 shows
four steps from each of eight Hard-level demonstrations, two Mind, two Act and four Mind2Act tasks.

the pot reaching 80 °C, CP07 uses 13 of 28 parts across a 36 cm river, and CP09 requires uncapping
pens with both hands. Failures in these intermediate operations can prevent later stages from succeeding,
so we report stage progress alongside final success (Section 4.3).
3.3 Difficulty Design
Aggregate scores can obscure the conditions under which performance declines. We therefore define
three difficulty levels for each task. The objective remains fixed, while memory load, reasoning demands,
precision, timing, or workflow dependencies increase. Easy tests the core requirement under the least
demanding conditions. Medium increases the information to retain, the required precision, or the number
of operations. Hard introduces greater demands through tighter constraints, longer dependent sequences,
or combinations of these factors. The levels define a progression within each task rather than a common
numerical scale across tasks. Comparing success and partial-progress scores across the three levels shows
how performance on the same task changes as its demands increase. Fig. 4 illustrates these variations.
Mind tasks increase the information to retain, such as unknown mappings, blocks, hidden hazards, or card
pairs, while requiring only routine execution. Act tasks vary physical demands, including load, path shape,
fold geometry, and domino count. Mind2Act tasks vary orders, river width, shapes, or balls to increase the
number and dependencies of operations. Appendix F specifies every task and difficulty level.

3.4.1 Data Collection and Quality Control
Each episode has a setup defined at three levels. The task determines the objective and rules, the difficulty
level determines the demands, and episode parameters specify variations such as object positions, orders, or
target readings. Task scripts define the operation sequence and end-effector targets. Inverse kinematics and
motion planning convert these targets into joint trajectories. Quality control checks physical completion
against simulator states and events. For example, CP01 requires the requested items to be released into the
correct tray and remain supported. Recording checks cover missing or corrupted frames, invalid state
or action values, timestamp alignment, and duplicates. Demonstrations must also satisfy task-specific
qualification conditions to provide clear examples for learning. A DR3 demonstration must retain at
least one second of withdrawal margin, and an M2 demonstration must use only information revealed by
actual card flips. These conditions are stricter than task success requirements and apply to demonstration
selection rather than policy scoring. Every retained demonstration passes the evaluator used at test time.
The collection therefore also shows that each task and level can be completed on this platform by a scripted
controller with access to simulator state. Demonstration and evaluation seeds are disjoint.
3.4.2 Data Statistics
Each episode records the task instruction, RGB images from a head camera and two wrist cameras at
640 × 480, and joint states and targets. Physics runs at 120 Hz, and the recorded data are sampled at
30 Hz. Across the thirty tasks, the dataset contains 50 episodes per task and difficulty level, resulting
in 4,500 episodes, 8.19 million frames per camera, and 75.8 hours of recordings, as detailed in
Appendix Table 8. Mind and Mind2Act episodes average 58.5 and 99.6 seconds, compared with 23.9
seconds for Act. These averages are approximately 2.5 and 4.2 times the Act average, respectively.
Hard Mind2Act episodes average 127.6 seconds.

3.5 Evaluation Setting
Policies are evaluated through closed-loop interaction with the environment. Each episode starts from a
predefined test instance, and the system selects actions using updated observations. Execution ends on
task completion, task-defined failure, system termination, or budget exhaustion. Each configuration is
defined by a task, a difficulty level, and a scene setting. An end-to-end policy runs all 50 instances of
each configuration, and an agent runs a shared subset of 5. Instance parameters are fixed in advance and
shared across systems within the same evaluation protocol to control variation between runs.
We report two metrics. Final success rate is the fraction of valid episodes that meet all completion
Research preprint
12
mmlab@SIGS
conditions. Task score ranges from 0 to 100 and measures partial progress under task-specific rules.
High task scores with low final success indicate that a system completes parts of a task but often fails to
meet all requirements. This distinction supports analysis of incomplete workflows in Mind2Act. Both
metrics are computed from simulator states and events. Invalid or missing runs are recorded separately.
Section 4 specifies the interfaces, timing, scoring, and aggregation rules.
3.5.1 Episode Variation and Randomization
Episodes share the same objective, rules, and difficulty level but vary task-specific initial states and
parameters. These include object layouts, initial and target states such as end-effector poses, and event
sequences such as piano-key order. Some tasks also vary continuous and hidden parameters. In CP10,
launcher position and aim are sampled continuously, while the task seed determines the order of red
and white balls. In PR5, the target angle is sampled from a set defined for each difficulty level. The
sheet displays a target arrow without revealing the numerical angle or tolerance. These variations test
whether a system can adapt its behavior rather than replay a memorized trajectory. Using shared test
instances supports controlled comparisons. Each task and difficulty level is evaluated in two scene settings,
which we treat as separate configurations, giving 180 configurations in total (30 tasks × 3 levels × 2
settings). The Clean setting fixes scene appearance. The Random setting uses the same task instances
and additionally varies clutter, table and floor materials, and lighting as depicted in Fig. 5. Because the
two settings share task instances, comparing them isolates the effect of visual and scene perturbation
from that of task demands. Appendix A.4 details how the two settings are generated.
4 Technical Implementation and Evaluation Protocol
Systems that act through different interfaces must face the same scenes, the same clock and the same
notion of success. Building on the task design in Section 3, this section describes how policies connect to
the tasks, how an episode is executed and timed, and how its outcome is scored and protected against
shortcuts. Appendix B collects the implementation details needed for reproduction.
4.1 Platform and Policy Interfaces
Comparing agents and end-to-end policies requires a platform that both can drive and that produces the same
physical effects for both. We implement Mind2Act World in Isaac Sim with two ARX X5 arms and parallel
grippers. A fixed head camera captures the scene layout, while two wrist cameras capture local gripper-
object interactions. RGB images are rendered at 640 × 480 for all cameras. Task scenes combine shared
robot and workspace assets with task-specific objects and mechanisms as depicted in Fig. 7. Their geometry,
motion, and contact properties are configured to reproduce the physical effects required by each task.
All systems follow a common information contract detailed in Appendix B. Policies receive the task
instruction, selected RGB views, and declared robot state. History access is limited to information
previously exposed within the same episode. Hidden simulator state is accessible only to the evaluator.
This includes unannounced parameters used for scoring, such as the numerical target angle and tolerance
in PR5, gate timing in DR3, and allowable ball drift in DR4.
The benchmark supports two action interfaces. End-to-end policies predict joint or end-effector actions.
These predictions are converted to joint targets, and a configured number of actions is executed from
each chunk before the next prediction. Agents are multimodal language models that issue structured
tool calls. The move_eef tool accepts an end-effector pose target and gripper command. The backend
handles inverse kinematics and motion planning. The wait_steps tool advances simulation while
maintaining the current arm and gripper targets. Agents can use memory_resolve to retrieve a previously
observed image by its observation identifier and add it to the model’s context. Retrieval does not advance
simulation. An agent can also call give_up to end the episode itself. Every evaluated agent uses
this one fixed harness of tools, planner and history access. The same two interfaces cover every task

4.2 Episode Protocol and Timing
Each episode starts from a fixed instance for its task, difficulty level and scene setting, and systems
evaluated under the same protocol use the same instance list. Reset restores the robot, objects, task
stage, evaluator, and random-number state. Policy memory is cleared between episodes and retained
across stages within an episode. Physics simulation advances at 120 Hz, while arm and gripper targets
are updated at 30 Hz. Simulation pauses during model inference. Task deadlines use simulation time,
while inference latency and total runtime use wall-clock time, the elapsed real time. A delayed model
response therefore consumes wall-clock time without reducing the simulated response window, so timed
tasks measure responses in simulation time rather than real-time performance. For end-to-end policies,
each task and difficulty level has an episode horizon set to 1.2 times the 90th percentile of demonstration
durations, rounded up to a control step. These simulation-time limits range from 16.27–420.50 s for
Mind, 6.27–156.47 s for Act, and 34.40–308.50 s for Mind2Act. Agents receive four times this physics
budget and at most 150 decisions per episode, since a tool call spans many control steps. Wall-clock
limits are safety stops, not task limits. An episode stopped by one is scored like any other unfinished
episode. Episodes end on completion, policy termination, or budget exhaustion. Simulator crashes and
incomplete records are not counted as task failures, and infrastructure aborts are rerun. Appendices A
and B specify episode horizons, model-call budgets, and scheduling.

A success rate alone says how often a task is finished but not how far a failed episode got, while a
progress score alone can reward work that never completes the task. We therefore report the final success

credits task-specific progress and equals 100 exactly when the episode succeeds. Partial credit never

tasks of a view, each pooled over its three levels, and the primary result averages the Clean and Random

information and physical execution depend on each other, so strength in either capability alone is not
enough. For this reason, the Overall score weights the views as 0.25 Mind + 0.25 Act + 0.50 Mind2Act,
so that Mind2Act counts as much as the two single views together. The Overall SR is the plain fraction
of successful episodes. A Mind2Act task is scored as a whole. Which side failed is assigned by the
episode-level diagnosis (Section 6). Partial-credit rules are task-specific, so scores are comparable across
systems on one task. For success rates we report 95% Wilson score intervals over episodes, which capture
sampling over the fixed instance lists rather than variation between tasks or repeated runs. As a descriptive
reference for composition, we also compare a system’s Mind2Act success rate with the product of its Mind
and Act success rates, the value expected if a Mind2Act workflow required one independent success of each
kind. The task score is defined in Appendix B.3, and per-task success predicates are listed in Appendix F.2.
4.4 Evaluation Integrity
Because an agent can declare that it has finished and a policy can repeat an action until something
happens, the evaluator has to judge outcomes. Completion is therefore assessed using environment-side
Research preprint
15
mmlab@SIGS
predicates. Commands and success declarations receive credit only when the required physical conditions
are met. Repeating an action earns credit only when it satisfies a new requirement. Task rules distinguish
irreversible failures, unscored actions, and valid exploration. For example, in DR4, a ball leaving its circle
causes failure even if it later rolls back. In F2, submitting an incomplete layout does not consume an
attempt. In M2, revealing a mismatched pair is valid exploration. Any action sequence that satisfies the
task rules is valid. A run is classified as a valid failure only when its record is sufficient to evaluate the final
success conditions. Unavailable observations and infrastructure interruptions are recorded separately.
5 Benchmark Results
Building on the protocol in Section 4, we evaluate nine systems that span the two main routes to embodied
manipulation: multimodal agents that act through tools and end-to-end policies trained on demonstrations.
Section 5.1 describes the systems and the evaluation procedure. Section 5.2 analyzes how well they reason,
act and compose the two, where and why they fall short across views, conditions and application domains,
and what the evaluation costs in time and money. Section 5.3 summarizes which system suits which task.
5.1 Experimental Setup
5.1.1 Systems
We evaluate two system families, each ranked on its own leaderboard. Agents are multimodal language
models that select motion tools from camera images and robot state, without any training on Mind2Act
World. We evaluate GPT-6 Astra [70], DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash [71] and Gemini-4-Argon [72]. End-to-end
policies map observations and the instruction to robot actions after fine-tuning on the shared demonstrations.
We evaluate the VLAs KinRT [73], 𝜋0.5 [20], G0.5 [22] and X-VLA [74], and the WAMs InternW0 [76]
and FastWAM [75]. Appendix C lists the evaluated systems.
5.1.2 Training and Adaptation
We fine-tune each end-to-end policy from its public checkpoint on the shared collection of 4,500
demonstrations, fifty per task and difficulty level (Section 3.4.2), with the initialization, global batch
size, and training steps of each system detailed in Appendix C. In contrast, we use the agents directly
as provided through their APIs, without fine-tuning.
5.1.3 Inference and Evaluation Instances
All evaluations run at 120 Hz physics and 30 Hz control/RGB observations, with simulation paused
during inference so that task deadlines are measured in simulation time and inference cost in wall-clock
time. Across the 180 configurations (30 tasks × 3 levels × 2 settings), the nine evaluated systems
account for 56,700 scored episodes.
At the start of an episode, each system receives the English task instruction, selected camera views,
and declared robot state. End-to-end policies generate action chunks that are converted to robot targets
at 30 Hz, whereas agents issue pose-level motion-tool requests and may retrieve earlier observations
from the same episode. For each task and difficulty level, we fix a shared list of 50 instances and
evaluate them in both Clean and Random settings (Table 3). End-to-end policies run on all 100 resulting
episodes per task and level (9,000 episodes per policy), while agents run on a shared subset of 5 Clean
and 5 Random episodes (900 episodes per agent).
Random instances preserve the underlying task instance while adding table clutter, one of 23 table materials,
and one of six light types (with RGB channels scaled in [0, 2] and intensity in [0.8, 1.2]). The range
is deliberately wide, so Random serves as an appearance stress test. End-to-end policies are allotted a

and Random, with a 2-hour wall-clock safety limit. Agents receive 4× this physics budget, up to 150
decisions per episode, and a 6-hour wall-clock safety limit. All three agents are evaluated at medium
reasoning effort. Appendix B details the remaining run settings.

M MIND2ACT
Do reasoning and execution work together?
Mind2Act workflows mostly end at their first steps, before reasoning and execution ever have to work
together. The Mind2Act view chains dependent operations into one workflow, in which remembered
information guides each action and each physical outcome decides what comes next. Table 4 and Fig. 8a
show that every system succeeds less often on this view than on Act. GPT-6 Astra drops from 52.7%
on Mind and 39.0% on Act to 12.0% on Mind2Act, while the other two agents complete no Mind2Act
episode. The end-to-end policies reach only 0.2 to 3.6%, close to their Mind rates and far below their
Act rates. For example, X-VLA, the strongest VLA on Act, drops from 19.6% to 0.2%. This shows that
strength on a single view does not carry over to workflows that need both, and that for the end-to-end
policies the missing memory, rather than execution, sets the limit.
The few Mind2Act successes come from workflows that a system can finish with steps it already performs
on the single views. GPT-6 Astra succeeds reliably only on conveyor meal assembly (CP01), in 60%
of its episodes, and the picks in CP01 resemble the conveyor pickup of DR1, on which it succeeds in
77%. CP01 alone supplies 18 of its 36 Mind2Act successes, whereas the agent completes no episode of
CP03, CP04, CP07, CP09 or CP10. The end-to-end policies, by contrast, succeed mostly at Easy, where

Partial scores further indicate that most Mind2Act episodes fail early rather than near completion. For

This suggests that Mind2Act failures are driven less by the coordination of reasoning and execution than
by single steps that reasoning or execution gets wrong. Reliable composition therefore first requires each
capability to hold up at every step of a workflow, which no current system achieves.
¶ APPEARANCE
Does it hold up when the scene looks different?
A change of appearance leaves the strongest agent’s memory largely intact, whereas the VLAs lose
most of theirs. The Clean and Random settings use the same task instances, and Random only adds clutter
and changes the table materials and the lighting, so comparing the two shows how much a change of
appearance alone affects each system. Because all demonstrations were recorded in Clean scenes, Random
scenes also look different from what the end-to-end policies saw in training. In overall score the change
matters little, since no system gains or loses more than about three points (Appendix Table 11).
The four fine-tuned VLAs, however, keep most of their Act successes and lose most of their Mind
successes. They still complete 86 to 94% as many Act episodes in Random scenes as in Clean scenes, but
only 28 to 41% as many Mind episodes (Fig. 8b). Their Mind successes fall from 97 to 35 for KinRT,
from 82 to 23 for 𝜋0.5, from 41 to 17 for G0.5 and from 24 to 7 for X-VLA. An Act task shows its
target in the current image, so the policy can still find it among new clutter and lighting. A Mind task
instead requires the policy to decide from earlier observations, and in Random scenes those observations
look unlike the demonstrations it was trained on. The VLAs therefore seem to remember how a scene
looked in training rather than what it contained.
GPT-6 Astra behaves the other way round, as its Mind success rate only moves from 54.7% to 50.7%
and its Mind2Act rate stays at 12.0%. It appears to remember what the scene contains, such as which
items it has seen and where they are, and new textures, lighting and clutter do not change that. Its Act
successes drop more, from 65 to 52 of 150, so for the agent a new appearance hurts execution more
than memory. The two world-action models differ from each other, as InternW0 completes 83 Mind
episodes in Random against 78 in Clean and keeps 93% of its Act successes, whereas FastWAM falls
from 198 to 67 successes overall. For the world-action models, coping with a new appearance therefore
depends on the individual model rather than on the model type.

7 LATENCY & COST
Would it keep up in real time, and what does each success cost?
Agent episodes take longer and cost more as the workflow grows, while end-to-end policies respond
within a fraction of a second. Simulation pauses while a model computes, so a timed task measures
whether the right action is chosen and executed, not whether it is chosen in time. The pause hides a
speed gap of one to nearly three orders of magnitude. As shown in Table 6, an agent call takes 11.0 to
35.6 s, whereas an end-to-end policy takes 77.1 to 488.1 ms, and model requests fill 67 to 86% of an
agent episode’s wall-clock time, against 2 to 29% for the end-to-end policies.
For an agent, this time goes to deliberation and grows with how much a task leaves to be decided. GPT-6
Astra spends 16.8 minutes on an Act episode and 19.1 on a Mind episode, but 30.7 on a Mind2Act
episode, where each physical outcome changes the next decision and its calls take longest (13.0 s against
9.2 s on Act), yet it completes only 12.0% of these workflows. A longer run does not by itself bring
more success, as DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash is the slowest agent (96.1 minutes per episode) but completes
only 6.6% of its episodes, against 34.6% for GPT-6 Astra. GPT-6 Astra, in turn, costs about twenty
times as much per episode as the other two agents (USD 25.13 against USD 1.12 to 1.24 at list price),
but because it succeeds far more often, its cost per success (USD 72.7) is only about four times that
of DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (USD 18.9).
End-to-end policies are much faster, and X-VLA, for example, predicts in 153 ms, about seventy times
faster than a GPT-6 Astra call. It finishes an Act episode in 6.5 rather than 16.8 minutes and reaches
19.6% on Act, the highest among end-to-end policies and half the agent’s 39.0%. Speed also varies
among the end-to-end policies, as InternW0 succeeds slightly more often than KinRT (8.7% against

How far is each application domain from deployment?
Domains built on pressing and placing reach at best about half of episodes, and kitchens and
assembly remain far from deployment. Each task is built from a need in one of five application domains
(Table 2), so the per-task results can also be read by domain (Mean rows of Table 5). GPT-6 Astra
succeeds in 53.3% of the companion and edutainment episodes and in 46.0% and 42.6% of those in

buttons, setting a slider or placing an item. It falls to 17.9% in home and food service and to 4.4% in
assembly, where at least half of the tasks are Mind2Act workflows.
The end-to-end policies rank the domains differently, as they do best in manufacturing and logistics (KinRT
20.9%), where conveyor pickup and gate passage repeat one practiced motion, and stay below 12% in every
other domain. Four of the five domains each hold two tasks that no system completes, namely kitchen
loading and cooking (CP03, CP04), workpiece tracking and tray carrying (M1, DR4), oriented stamping
and whiteboard restoration (PR5, CP09), and the domino chain and bridge building (PR6, CP07).
The best domain value, 53.3%, still means failing about every second attempt, so no domain is yet close
to deployment even in simulation. Agents come closest in edutainment and manufacturing, end-to-end
policies come closest in repetitive factory motions, and neither comes close in kitchens or assembly.
5.3 Which System Suits Which Task
The leaderboards and the analysis of Section 5.2 show how the systems rank and where each of
them succeeds and fails. Together they show that agents and end-to-end policies currently handle
different kinds of task demand.
Agents for reasoning-heavy steps. A strong agent is suited to the steps of a task that turn on what was
seen before and on what to do next. GPT-6 Astra remembers and reasons well (52.7% on Mind), keeps
that memory when the scene changes, and solves tasks whose target can be read and reached in a few
deliberate moves. Its limits lie in contact and timing. It completes none of the five contact-rich tasks,
and its timed successes are measured with a simulator that waits for it. The other two agents use the
same tools yet differ widely on Mind, so this profile is specific to the model.
End-to-end policies for execution-heavy steps. End-to-end policies are suited to steps that one practiced
motion can cover. The four evaluated VLAs retain 86–94% of their Act successes under scene randomization,
and each has its own strength: G0.5 on timed reactions, X-VLA on resistance adjustment and cloth folding,
and KinRT and 𝜋0.5 on memory, where the VLAs show little. They retain almost no task state, so any
step that depends on earlier information is better left to another system.
World-action models differ widely. InternW0 is the only end-to-end policy whose memory survives
scene changes, and it nearly matches the agent on the blueprint task. It still follows its learned sequence
without checking preconditions, as in cooking. FastWAM drops in both views under scene randomization,
so this advantage is not inherent to world-action models.
Some demands are met by no system yet. No evaluated system succeeds on M1, and GPT-6 Astra
completes no episode of P2. No system sustains contact under force (DR4, PR5 and PR6 are never
completed), and none completes Mind2Act workflows reliably. GPT-6 Astra reaches 12.0% on Mind2Act,
and no end-to-end policy exceeds 3.6%. Neither family yet covers both reasoning and execution.

6 Diagnostic Analysis
Success rates measure task completion, and task scores measure partial progress. But they cannot identify
where or why a system fails within an episode, which is what model developers need to know. In
this section, we examine where tasks fail, which errors recur across systems, and how these findings
inform improvement priorities. Episode-level diagnosis and model profile cards (Section 6.1) connect
failure patterns to task progress, budget use, and stopping decisions (Section 6.2), and the failure codes
point to directions for improvement (Section 6.3). Inference latency, runtime and API cost are analyzed
alongside success rates in Section 5.2.
6.1 Diagnosis Harness and Model Profile Cards
Diagnosis harness design. We classify failures by the first unrecoverable error identified in the episode,
using 21 failure codes (Appendix E.1). Mind errors concern perception and task reasoning, Act errors
concern physical execution, and composition errors concern using physical outcomes to guide subsequent
actions. Three episode-level codes capture timeouts, stalls, and cases with insufficient evidence. The label
follows the observed error, regardless of the task’s view. A Mind failure involves an error in perception or
task reasoning. Examples include selecting the wrong object based on a misremembered order (M-MEMORY)
and incorrectly predicting how the scene will change (M-PREDICT). An Act failure occurs when the system
selects an appropriate action but executes it incorrectly. Examples include losing a grasp (A-GRASP) and
executing the correct action too early or too late (A-TIMING). A composition failure occurs when the system
proceeds without verifying a required outcome or fails to update its next action based on feedback. For
example, the system may continue pressing the same button after the light cue changes (C-NO-UPDATE).
The harness combines three layers of review as in Fig. 9a. First, deterministic rules use scorer outputs
and simulator events to isolate the failure or bound the step at which it occurred. For cases left open by
rules, an LLM judge inspects the video and action or tool-call history without overturning rule-based
verdicts, flagging ambiguous cases as uncertain. Finally, human reviewers settle the cases the judge marks
as uncertain. Fig. 9 shows the workflow on an illustrative example, and Appendix E.4 reports the results
of this procedure for every failed episode of three representative systems.

6.2 Failure Patterns of the Evaluated Systems
We read the measured cards of G0.5, InternW0 and GPT-6 Astra (Appendix E.4), together with
the run logs of the agents.
The overall pattern. Physical execution is the largest diagnosed failure group in all three systems,
accounting for 41.4% of G0.5’s failures, 37.2% of InternW0’s, and 55.3% of GPT-6 Astra’s. GPT-6 Astra’s
failures are particularly concentrated in execution, whereas G0.5 and InternW0 also show substantial
perception and reasoning failures, at 27.7% and 32.8%, respectively; 6–11% of each system’s failures
occur while closing the loop. These proportions refer to the first unrecoverable error identified in
each failed episode. The rule layer alone would overstate Mind-side errors for the end-to-end policies
(Appendix Table 17), because scorer records reveal a misremembered item more readily than video
reveals a motor fault. A card therefore combines the layers.
The dominant execution errors differ across systems. Failed or lost grasps are the most frequent errors
for G0.5 and GPT-6 Astra, with 1,324 and 120 cases, while fine-control errors lead InternW0’s profile
with 1,132 cases. The two end-to-end policies also record 924 and 1,011 memory failures. These profiles
identify distinct priorities: grasp reliability for G0.5 and GPT-6 Astra, fine control for InternW0, and
task-state retention for both end-to-end policies.
Differences across tasks. The dominant failure also varies by task, even within the same system. For
G0.5, 202 of 300 bridge-building episodes (CP07) fail to grasp a construction piece (Fig. 13), making
object acquisition the main obstacle to further progress. In light-cued pressing (DR2), however, 90 of 190
failures involve a misread light (M-PERCEIVE) and 88 involve a response that is not updated after the light
changes (C-NO-UPDATE). Only eight concern the press itself or its timing. In incremental meal orders (P1),
memory errors account for 210 of G0.5’s 300 failed episodes and 245 of InternW0’s 298 failed episodes.
Where progress stops. No evaluated system completes whiteboard restoration (CP09) or bridge building
(CP07), and failures on such workflows often occur before their reasoning stages are reached. On
CP09, 299 of 300 G0.5 episodes stop before board removal. GPT-6 Astra fails during the lift in all
30 tray-carrying (DR4) episodes (Fig. 15). Zero success on these tasks is therefore consistent with
early physical bottlenecks rather than failures in downstream planning or repair. Most episodes fail in
Mind or Act before closed-loop composition is tested.
Analysis of budget use. Under the same decision budget, the agent logs show distinct termination patterns
(Appendix Table 18). Gemini-4-Argon voluntarily stops in 829 of 900 episodes and uses approximately
33 decisions per episode. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash uses 111.8 decisions on average, voluntarily stops in 385
episodes, and exhausts the decision and physics budgets in 286 and 94 episodes, respectively. GPT-6
Astra averages 79.6 decisions per episode. It voluntarily stops in 460 episodes, and only one of them
succeeds, a gate-passage episode (DR3) whose task was already complete when it stopped. The other
459 account for 77.9% of its 589 failed episodes, and in 264 of them the agent has used no more than
100 of the 150 allowed decisions. Among these 459 stops, the first unrecoverable error is attributed
to execution in 289 cases, perception or reasoning in 127, and composition in 28. The remaining 15
cases fall outside these three capability groups.
These results distinguish resource limits from the errors that prevent task completion. DeepSeek frequently
exhausts its budget. GPT-6 Astra shows a different pattern: many unsuccessful episodes end with
substantial decision budget remaining, and execution errors dominate its voluntary stops, often after its
motion planner rejects the requested poses. For episodes affected by execution errors, additional decisions
are useful only if they enable effective retries or corrective actions (Table 7).

Mind failures: task state across stages. Misremembered information is the most frequent Mind-side
code of both measured end-to-end policies, with 924 failures of G0.5 and 1,011 of InternW0, and the VLAs
lose 59–72% of their Mind successes when the scene is randomized (Section 5.2). These failures point to
maintaining task-relevant identities, order, and progress across operations, and to recognizing an item
under a new appearance. Closing this gap calls for data that stress what the failures expose, with varied
scene appearance and longer gaps between the moment information is shown and the moment it is needed.
Act failures: grasp, contact and timing. Failed or lost grasps account for 1,324 failures of G0.5 and
fine-control errors for 1,132 of InternW0. On bridge building (CP07), 202 of 300 G0.5 episodes never
grasp a piece. G0.5’s frequent grasp failures motivate training and control changes that improve contact
acquisition and object retention. InternW0’s fine-control errors motivate closer examination of action
resolution and feedback during manipulation. Responses must also fall within the short windows of timed
tasks such as the light-cued button (DR2) and the cyclic gates (DR3). On DR3, most failures of the two
end-to-end policies come from the withdrawal after delivery rather than from the gate timing. The arm is
still inside the gate corridor when the gate closes, or touches the gate while it closes. Demonstrations
can be weighted toward these segments, with more variation in object pose, contact and timing, instead
of being spread evenly over whole episodes.
Composition failures: checking and repairing outcomes. InternW0 has 761 precondition failures, 271
of them on cooking (CP04), where it switches on the heat before all ingredients are in the pot. Each step is
executed, but its outcome is not checked before the next one starts. Such failures call for checking whether
the required physical state has been achieved before proceeding, and repairing a step that failed. The
scripted demonstrations show only steps that succeed, so a policy never sees a failed step followed by
its repair. Recovery demonstrations can begin at frequently observed failures, such as a slipped grasp
or an incomplete placement, and show how to restore progress.

Because an end-to-end policy learns its behavior from demonstrations, the cards show where new data for
it should be built. On whiteboard restoration (CP09), 299 of 300 G0.5 episodes end before the board is
lifted off its stand, so the later stages of the task are never reached. Adding full-length demonstrations
would spend most of their steps on later stages, which the policy almost never reaches. Demonstrations
constructed around the breakpoint instead, starting from the states in which episodes stop and from
perturbed states near them, concentrate the training data where the policy fails.
Agents: the harness. For agents, the same codes also point to the harness. GPT-6 Astra breaks
down mostly during execution and often stops after its motion planner rejects the requested poses
(Section 6.2), so finer motion tools and a retry step before stopping come first. Tool feedback should
help distinguish an ineffective request from a completed operation and support an appropriate retry or
alternative action. The harness can also return the outcome of each step and require a check before
the next one, which addresses composition failures. It can also keep a record of earlier failures, so
that an agent recognizes a breakpoint it has met before.
7 Limitations and Future Directions
7.1 Limitations
Limited causal attribution. Each view is a set of tasks, not an intervention on one system. A Mind
task still requires routine manipulation, an Act task still requires the robot to perceive its cue, and the
Mind2Act workflows are longer than the single-view tasks and differ from them in content (Section 5.2).
A low Mind2Act result therefore cannot yet be separated from the effect of a longer horizon or of an
early execution error. The diagnosis harness attributes failures within each episode, where rules settle
22 to 38% of the failures of the three diagnosed systems and the LLM judge, with human review of
uncertain cases, attributes the rest.
Paused clock and a fixed agent harness. Simulation pauses during inference, so timed tasks test whether
an action is chosen and timed correctly in simulation time, not in real time (Section 5.2). The agents
also receive an inverse-kinematics and motion-planning service and four times the physics budget of the
end-to-end policies, and they are not trained on the benchmark. All three agents use the same fixed harness,
so the results do not show what a model would achieve with its own tools, controllers or memory. We
therefore rank agents and end-to-end policies on separate leaderboards and treat the lead of the strongest
agent as a result under the paused clock rather than as real-time ability.
Limited system coverage. The results cover three agents, four VLAs and two WAMs, and they include
no hybrid or memory-augmented system. This is enough to compare the two families but too few for a
broader analysis, for example of how model size, training data or architecture shapes the results on Mind,
Act and Mind2Act. Each agent is also evaluated on only ten episodes per task and level, so its results by
difficulty level are coarse. Some episodes were run again after infrastructure failures (Appendix B).
Limited embodiments and settings. All tasks are tabletop manipulation tasks in simulation on one
bimanual platform with parallel grippers, and each task and level uses one fixed English instruction. Other
embodiments and settings, such as dexterous hands, mobile manipulation, navigation and humanoid
robots, are not yet covered. The Random setting changes appearance over a wide range and is not meant
to model a deployment scene. Each capability category contains between one and six tasks, which is
too few to support claims about a single category. How the results in simulation relate to results on
the physical robot has not yet been measured.

Composition and outcome feedback. The current views show where a system fails, and the next step is
to show why. On the Mind2Act tasks, an oracle for Mind will supply the task state that would otherwise
have to be remembered or inferred, and an oracle for Act will let a system call scripted skills for the
physical operations. The gain from each oracle shows how much that capability contributes, and the gap
that remains with both oracles shows the cost of composition. Each Mind2Act workflow will also be paired
with the Mind and Act tasks that share its demands, and progress will be reported for each stage as the share
of episodes that pass it among those that reach it, which removes the effect of horizon length. Matched pairs
of runs that start from the same task state and differ only in whether the system receives feedback on a grasp,
a placement or a contact will then measure how much the outcome of an action guides the next decision.
Broader and fairer comparison. The leaderboards will cover more systems and settings, so that the
comparison between the two families no longer rests on one fixed setup. The task suite will also grow
beyond tabletop bimanual manipulation to dexterous hands, mobile manipulation, navigation and humanoid
robots, so that the three views can be tested on other embodiments. Human teleoperation and a scripted
expert acting through each interface will give every task a reachable upper bound, and a version of the
protocol that adds inference time to the simulation clock will show how much of the agents’ result on
timed tasks remains in real time. A sandbox setting with its own leaderboard will let an agent bring its
own harness, which runs in an isolated container with access only to the cameras, the robot state and
joint-level control. The evaluated systems will extend to memory-augmented end-to-end policies, which
test whether the weak memory of the VLAs comes from their architecture, and to hybrid systems that
pair a language-model planner with a learned executor, which test whether combining the memory of
the best agent with the practiced motions of the VLAs closes part of the gap. Repeated runs of the same
instances will show how much results vary, together with the time and cost of each evaluation, and the
difficulty levels of tasks on which the levels currently make no difference will be recalibrated.
From diagnosis to better models. The diagnosis will move from explaining failures to guiding
improvement. It will cover every evaluated system rather than three representative ones, and its rules and
prompts will be released. The current harness reports only the first unrecoverable error of each episode,
which shows where an episode breaks but not what happens after it. The diagnosis will therefore trace the
Research preprint
29
mmlab@SIGS
whole course of a failed episode and record multiple breakpoints, including how an early error leads to
later ones and whether the system notices a mistake and recovers from it. Episodes will also start from
verified intermediate states of the scripted demonstrations, so that each stage of a workflow is measured
even when an earlier stage fails. A developer can then change a model in response to its profile card and
evaluate the new model on the same instances to check whether the change helped.
Transfer to real robots. The last direction tests whether the findings hold outside simulation. Mind2Act
World will add real-robot experiments in which its tasks are mapped to physical counterparts, starting with
Mind2Act tasks such as CP02 and CP04 and extending to a wider set of tasks, so that the Mind, Act and
Mind2Act results can be verified on physical hardware. The same tasks will also be paired with similar
tasks in other simulators, which shows how much of the failure analysis carries across platforms.
8 Conclusion
We introduce Mind2Act World, a benchmark that measures what a robot retains and decides, how well it
executes, and whether the two hold together when each physical outcome sets the next decision. Thirty
tasks in three views, each with three difficulty levels and paired Clean and Random scenes, yield 180
configurations, all scored from physical outcomes by the same evaluator for agents and end-to-end policies.
Our evaluation of three agents, four VLAs, and two WAMs over 56,700 episodes yields three main
findings. First, the strongest agent leads every view without task-specific training, operating under a
paused clock and with a motion planner, while the four fine-tuned VLAs have similar Overall success
rates and differ mainly in the tasks they handle best. Second, single-view ability does not carry over to
Mind2Act tasks: the best system falls from 52.7% on Mind and 39.0% on Act to 12.0% on Mind2Act,
and no end-to-end policy exceeds 3.6%. Third, the VLAs are limited even before composition, by
memory that mostly disappears when the scene changes and by the first physical contact of the longer
workflows. Eight of the thirty tasks are completed by no system.
Wang Yangming held that knowing and acting are one. In current embodied systems, they remain
separate abilities, unevenly developed and not yet joined. Mind2Act World makes the distance between
them measurable. The interventions, references and additional systems described in Section 7 are
our next steps toward explaining it.
