Abstract
Video foresight reasoning requires vision-language models
to anticipate future events or next actions from currently
observed videos. Existing methods fit future captions, ra-
tionales, or full visual states, but face two limitations. First,
they rely on annotations or external large models for su-
pervision, limiting scalability.
We address this with an
on-policy self-distillation framework that turns past–future
video pairs into a natural self-supervised signal without ex-
tra annotations or LLM supervision. Second, they treat the
whole future clip as equally valuable, although most of it
is irrelevant context information. We address this by defin-
ing a foresight score, from which we obtain a contrastive
privilege advantage that drives the model to learn the most
valuable future information. Since the score is intractable,
we optimize a tractable lower-bound surrogate that interpo-
lates this advantage, computed between the true and a ran-
dom reference future, with a teacher–student distillation ad-
vantage; together, they amplify decision-relevant content in-
formation and suppress irrelevant context information. We
call the resulting framework Future-OPSD. On open-ended
prediction and closed-ended planning, Future-OPSD im-
proves base models by up to 14.2 and 15.9 points without
compromising general video understanding. Code will be
available at https://jingchensun.github.io/future-opsd/.
1. Introduction
Video foresight reasoning aims to infer plausible future
events or states from an observed video by reasoning over
its visual dynamics, causal relations, and agent intentions.
This ability has been studied through diverse task formu-
lations, including activity forecasting and action anticipa-
tion [10, 17, 19, 22, 29, 62], trajectory forecasting [2, 23,
43], future frame and representation prediction [14, 39, 54],
and video-language future event prediction [31, 59]. With
the advent of vision-language models (VLMs), recent work

Recent VLM-based methods have begun to explicitly
train foresight reasoning.
Merlin [60] injects foresight
through trajectory-based pre-training and instruction tun-
ing. Next-Event Prediction [55] learns from captions of fu-
ture segments and text-only LLM rationales, and further ap-
plies reinforcement learning with multiple-choice answers.
Another line grounds reasoning in future visual states:
TwiFF [35] reasons with explicit future frames, FUTURE-
L1 [27] aligns latent reasoning with future-frame embed-
dings before answer-based reinforcement learning with an
LLM judge, and IVT [65] adds next-embedding prediction
to fine-tuning on annotated event descriptions.
Despite this progress, these methods share two limita-
tions.
First, their supervision relies on ground-truth an-

as LLM rationale generators and judges [27, 48, 55, 65],
which limits scalability. Second, they treat the future clip
as a uniformly valuable target and fit its full textual or vi-
sual content [27, 35, 55, 65], yet future information is not
equally useful for a given question. As shown in Fig. 1, to
predict what to do next, only rinse the knife is event-relevant
content information, while most of the scene (e.g., tap, dish
rack, sink) is irrelevant context information.
To address the first limitation, we adopt an on-policy
self-distillation framework to turn naturally occurring past–
future video pairs into a self-supervised learning signal. We
split each video into a past clip and its future clip (the paired
future).
The student sees only the past and generates a
reasoning-and-answer response, while a frozen copy of the
initial student that additionally sees the paired future acts
as the teacher and scores each token of that response. As
supervision comes from the video’s own future, this frame-
work needs no annotations or external large models and thus
scales well.
To address the second limitation, we define a foresight
score to teach the model to identify the most valuable fu-
ture information. It measures how well a student response
matches the true future. Directly optimizing this score is
intractable, as it averages over all possible futures.
We
therefore derive a tractable surrogate objective that prov-
ably lower-bounds the foresight score. The surrogate inter-
polates between a contrastive privilege advantage, which
compares the teacher’s scores of the same response under
the paired true future and an unrelated reference future, and
a teacher–student distillation advantage toward the paired-
future teacher. The joint interpolated objective suppresses
shared context information and amplifies content informa-
tion specific to the paired future (Fig. 2). We refer to the
framework combining these two designs as Future-OPSD.
We evaluate Future-OPSD on Qwen3-VL and Qwen3.5
across two video foresight tasks: open-ended prediction and
closed-ended planning. Future-OPSD improves the fore-
sight reasoning of the base models by up to 14.2 and 15.9
points, respectively, and consistently outperforms Naive
OPSD, which does not optimize the foresight score (Tabs. 1
and 2). The gains transfer across tasks and largely preserve
general video understanding. Stronger teachers and more
data bring further gains. Token-level analyses and discus-
sions show that the interpolated objective suppresses con-
text information and amplifies content information.
Our contributions are:
• We propose Future-OPSD, which is, to our knowledge,
the first on-policy self-distillation framework for video
foresight reasoning.
It turns naturally occurring past–
future video pairs into a self-supervised signal, requiring
no extra annotations.
• We introduce a foresight score and derive its tractable
lower bound as a token-level training objective, which en-
ables the model to automatically identify and learn the
most valuable future information.
• Experiments on Qwen3-VL and Qwen3.5 show gains of
up to 14.2 and 15.9 points on open-ended prediction and
closed-ended planning, with strong cross-task transfer, no
compromise in general video understanding, and gains
that grow with stronger teachers and more data.
2. Related Work
Video Foresight Reasoning. Early studies on video fore-
sight focus on action anticipation with recurrent and trans-
former models [18–20, 38, 54], future event prediction in
natural language [31], and LLM-based long-term action
planning [28, 62].
With the rise of VLMs, benchmarks
such as FutureBench, TwiFF-Bench, EgoPlan-Bench, and
SEED-Bench-R1 evaluate future-event reasoning and next-
action planning [7, 8, 35, 55], while CausalVQA, Future-
Omni, and RIVER extend evaluation to physical, audio-
visual, and streaming settings [6, 15, 46]. Existing train-
ing methods fall into three lines: (i) supervised learning
of textual future targets and rationales [55, 60]; (ii) model-
ing explicit or latent future visual states, or predictive video
representations [3, 27, 35, 49, 50, 65]; and (iii) reinforce-
ment learning with answer-, event-, or generation-based re-
wards [9, 48, 55]. These methods rely on annotations or
external large models for supervision, or fit the whole fu-
ture clip uniformly. In contrast, Future-OPSD does not fit
the future: it uses future clips as privileged evidence to score
the student’s own responses, without annotations or an extra
large LLM as a judge to provide supervision signals.
On-Policy Self-Distillation. Learning using privileged in-
formation trains a student with information available only
during training [37, 52].
On-policy distillation has a
teacher score student-generated trajectories to reduce expo-
sure bias [1], and OPSD uses the same model, conditioned
on privileged context, as its own teacher [63]. Multimodal
variants use temporal, high-resolution, or localized visual
evidence for video understanding [32, 53, 56–58, 64], and
related ideas extend to world-action models and video gen-
erators [13, 34]. Recent analyses show that privileged con-
text can shift the teacher in task-irrelevant ways, termed
privilege illusions [47, 61]; RLCSD contrasts textual hints
within outcome-reward RL [40]. In contrast, Future-OPSD
is, to our knowledge, the first OPSD framework for video
foresight reasoning, and differs from prior contrastive distil-
lation in two ways. (1) Data: it contrasts the true future clip
of the same video with a future clip from an unrelated video,
rather than textual hints [40]. (2) Objective: grounded in a
foresight score and its KL-regularized lower bound (Theo-
rem 2), it interpolates contrastive and distillation advantages
rather than relying on contrast alone.

3.1. Problem Setup and Notation
For paired data (X, Z) ∼p, X denotes past frames and a
question, and Z the paired future, i.e., the subsequent clip
recorded in the same video. The student πθ generates a
reasoning-and-answer response Y from X alone; the frozen
teacher π0 scores it given (X, Z). Thus, Y ∼πθ(· | X) and
pθ(y | x, z) = πθ(y | x). This gives Y ⊥⊥Z | X and the
Markov chain Z →X →Y . We sample a reference future
˜z ∼pZ from another video, independently of (X, Z, Y ),
where pZ is the future marginal. Training requires no an-
swer or rationale labels. Inference uses only the student.
We assume finite past, future, and response spaces and pos-
itive teacher probabilities for all allowed responses. Full
assumptions and proofs appear in Appendix A.

4.1. Main Results (RQ1)
Models and datasets.
We use two open VLM families,
Qwen3-VL [4] and Qwen3.5 [42], with Qwen3-VL-8B-
Instruct and Qwen3.5-9B as the students in RQ1. Both use
reason-then-answer prompting, with native thinking dis-
abled for Qwen3.5-9B. We consider two video foresight
tasks:
(a) Open-ended prediction:
we train on Future-
L1 [27] (50K clips, one open-ended question per clip) and
evaluate in-domain on TwiFF-Bench [35] (1,078 questions).
(b) Closed-ended planning: we train on EgoPlan-IT [8]
(43,294 four-way next-action questions on 30.5K progress
clips from 610 EPIC-KITCHENS-100 videos [11]) and
evaluate in-domain on SEED-Bench-R1 [7] (L1–L3).
Training and evaluation.
We fine-tune all student param-
eters for 100 steps (256 queries, one rollout each), with
learning rate 10−6, temperature 1.0, c = 5, λ = 0.75, and
no separate KL loss (Sec. 3.4). Teachers remain frozen at
initialization and receive up to three future frames; refer-
ence futures use the same format from other videos. Fol-

4.2. Teacher and Data Scaling (RQ2)
Using stronger VLM teachers further improves video
foresight reasoning. We train on EgoPlan-IT in two set-
tings: (i) a Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct student with a frozen
Qwen3-VL-4B teacher, and (ii) a Qwen3-VL-4B student
with a frozen Qwen3-VL-8B teacher. As all else is fixed,
the gain of Future-OPD over Future-OPSD comes from the
stronger teacher. It improves in-domain planning in both
settings and transfers to open-ended prediction (Tab. 3).
Future-OPSD improves video foresight reasoning
with more training data, and a stronger teacher further
amplifies the gains. We train Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct on
increasing amounts of EgoPlan-IT data, up to 50K prompts,
with either a frozen self-teacher or a frozen Qwen3-VL-4B-
Instruct teacher. Figure 3 shows that Future-OPSD scales
better than Naive OPSD and overtakes SFT and GRPO
at larger scales without answer labels, while the stronger
teacher enables more sustained gains.
4.3. How Does Future-OPSD Work? (RQ3)
Future-OPSD amplifies supervision on content informa-
tion and suppresses supervision on context information.
In Fig. 4, Naive OPSD reinforces generic descriptions such
as “observation” while penalizing the decision-relevant to-
ken “greaseproof.” Future-OPSD redirects supervision to-
ward evidence for the immediate next action. After train-
ing, it correctly predicts picking up the paper (A), whereas
Naive OPSD jumps to placing dough on it (B). This case il-
lustrates how future contrast helps distinguish the next step
from the overall goal.

4.5. Which Design Matters? (DS2)
Images outperform text. On Future-L1, z = text com-
prises descriptions generated by Qwen3.5-27B from the
7
frames used as z = image. Both methods achieve higher
scores with images (Tab. 5).
A possible explanation is
that captions compress the future before teacher scoring,
whereas frames let the teacher select event evidence condi-
tioned on the prediction question. The Qwen3-VL-8B im-
age advantage is consistent with retaining cues omitted by
question-agnostic captions, although the score comparison
alone does not establish this mechanism.
Our search identifies λ = 0.75 as the best overall set-
ting. We sweep λ ∈{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} for both stu-
dents on EgoPlan-IT and Future-L1 (Fig. 5). Combining
contrastive and distillation signals improves performance,
while pure contrast (λ = 1) collapses training on Future-
L1. This is consistent with Sec. 3.4: at λ = 1, the KL
anchor to the frozen future-conditioned teacher disappears,
allowing policy drift and a larger surrogate gap without im-
proved foresight.

5. Conclusion
Existing methods for video foresight reasoning depend on
annotations or external large models, and learn from the en-
tire future clip even though not all of it is equally important.
To address both limitations, we proposed Future-OPSD. It
adopts on-policy self-distillation to turn naturally occur-
ring past–future video pairs into a self-supervised signal,
and optimizes a tractable lower bound of a foresight score
that interpolates a contrastive privilege advantage with a
teacher–student distillation advantage, helping the student
focus on the most valuable future information.
Experi-
ments on Qwen3-VL and Qwen3.5 show that Future-OPSD
improves video foresight reasoning without compromis-
ing general video understanding, and scales with stronger
teachers and more data. Token-level analyses confirm that it
amplifies content information and suppresses context infor-
mation. Although our conclusions are limited to the datasets
and models studied here, we believe our findings provide
useful guidance for improving VLM foresight reasoning.
