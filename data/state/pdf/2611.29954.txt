Yggdrasil: Tree-Routed Recurrent Experts for State Tracking and Fast CPU
Inference
Cosmo Santoni 1
Abstract
Parallel-trainable recurrences such as Mamba, lin-
ear attention, and DeltaNet offer constant-cost
streaming inference, but they suffer from two lim-
itations: 1) they struggle to track discrete state,
and 2) they read every parameter at every step,
tying model capacity directly to CPU latency.
We introduce Yggdrasil, a recurrent architecture
that decouples capacity from per-step compute
by looking up each layer’s transition in a large,
tree-routed bank of recurrent experts. Each router
addresses its bank using the current token and
the state of the layer below. We prove that this
cross-layer context is necessary—a single token-
indexed layer of non-expanding transitions with
finitely many reachable states cannot perform con-
ditional resets—while avoiding feedback from the
layer’s own past preserves parallel-scan training.
Because each step traverses one tree path and
reads a single expert per layer, active weights stay
within a few hundred kilobytes in CPU L2 cache
while the full bank resides in RAM. Among the
parallel-trainable models we evaluate, Yggdrasil
is the only one to solve all five state-tracking tasks.
At matched active parameter counts, it achieves
the lowest test bits on enwik8 (1.540 bpb) and the
human genome hg38 (1.567 bpb), while stepping
3.0× and 3.3× faster on a single CPU core (6.7 µs
and 5.8 µs) than the fastest streaming baseline,
and delivering the highest GPU throughput of all
models at batch 1024. On the Genomic Bench-
marks, it is the strongest causal model at matched
active parameters (86.0% mean accuracy).

Many sequence tasks must score symbols one at a time as
they arrive, including base-by-base DNA modelling, byte-
level compression, and on-device inference (Hutter, 2006;
Zhang et al., 2025; Gerganov & llama.cpp contributors,

2023; Gemma Team, 2025). Because these workloads can-
not batch over time, their cost is governed by a single step
at batch size one, ideally running at constant cost per posi-
tion on one CPU core (Williams et al., 2009). Base-level
genomic models like HyenaDNA model DNA causally with-
out a tokeniser (Nguyen et al., 2023), but recompute their
context window at every base unless distilled into a recur-
rence (Massaroli et al., 2023). Parallel-trainable recurrences
such as Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2024; Dao & Gu, 2024), linear
attention (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Schlag et al., 2021),
and the DeltaNet family (Yang et al., 2024; 2025; Siems
et al., 2025) do provide constant-cost streaming steps. Yet
they hit two fundamental walls. First, they struggle to track
discrete state, and none of the parallel-trainable baselines
solves all five state-tracking tasks we evaluate (S5, A5, par-
ity, cycle navigation, and modular arithmetic with operator
precedence). The strongest, DeltaProduct-4, solves four (Ta-
ble 1), whereas LSTMs and GRUs solve all five at the cost
of sequential training (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997;
Cho et al., 2014; Delétang et al., 2023). Second, they are
dense, reading every parameter at every step so that any
added capacity directly increases per-token latency.
We introduce Yggdrasil, a recurrent architecture that breaks
both walls with a single mechanism, looking up each layer’s
transition in a large bank of recurrent experts rather than
computing it (Figure 1). Each routed layer stores 2D experts,
and a depth-D binary tree selects one in D comparisons to
supply the transition, input, and readout for that step. The
tree computes its address from the current token and the
state of the layer below. Because this address never reads
the layer’s own past, each layer remains an affine recurrence
and trains as an associative parallel scan (Blelloch, 1990;
Martin & Cundy, 2018).
This design directly satisfies the three requirements for ex-
act state tracking. First, transitions must be non-diagonal,
since diagonal recurrences cannot track non-solvable groups
like S5 and A5 (Merrill et al., 2024; Shakerinava et al.,
2026); Theorem 14 establishes the finite-state form of this
limit for stacks of diagonal layers. Second, transitions need
negative or complex eigenvalues, as eigenvalues restricted
to [0, 1] cannot track parity (Grazzi et al., 2025). Third,
conditional resets require transition selection that depends
on more than the current token, and we prove that a sin-
gle token-indexed layer of non-expanding transitions with

The same lookup makes Yggdrasil hardware-aware. Like
Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2024), it is built around the memory
hierarchy, but for CPU inference rather than GPU training
(Section 3.3). Because each step reads only one expert
per layer, its active weights fit inside the cache of a single
CPU core while the full bank resides in RAM. Mixtures
of experts and memory layers also decouple storage from
compute (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Lample
et al., 2019; Berges et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2026), but
only in layers that look up outputs. Yggdrasil moves sparse
lookup into the recurrence itself; replacing its recurrent
experts with output-only experts loses 0.244 bits per byte
at equal storage (Section H). Prior work on input-switched
affine networks, second-order RNNs, and PD-SSM also
selects transitions per step (Foerster et al., 2017; Giles et al.,
1992; Omlin & Giles, 1996; Terzi´c et al., 2025). Yggdrasil is
the first to look up recurrent transitions in a large tree-routed
bank using lower-layer context, preserving both parallel-
scan training and a cache-sized CPU step.
Contributions.
1. Tree-routed recurrent experts. Yggdrasil looks up
each routed layer’s transition in a bank of 2D experts,
requiring O(D) routing work and cache-sized weight
reads per step while preserving parallel-scan training
(Section 3).
2. State-tracking and sparse-access theory. We prove
that a single token-indexed layer of non-expanding
transitions with finitely many reachable states cannot
perform conditional resets. We give exact construc-
tions tracking S5 and A5 in 8-bit integer arithmetic
and every finite automaton, plus a counting separation
between sparse and dense models in weights read per
step (Section 4).
3. Accuracy.
Yggdrasil is the only parallel-trainable
model in Table 1 to solve all five state-tracking tasks.
It achieves the lowest test bits on hg38 and enwik8 at
every matched active size (Tables 7 and 8), with its
smallest models outperforming every dense baseline
at roughly 10× the active parameter count. At 470k
active parameters (307.3M stored), it averages 86.0%
across the eight Genomic Benchmarks tasks (five splits
each) under the Caduceus protocol (Grešová et al.,
2023; Schiff et al., 2024), making it the best causal
model at matched active parameters.
4. Speed. Yggdrasil is the fastest model on one CPU core,
with its largest models stepping 3.3× and 3.0× faster
than the fastest streaming baseline, and achieves the
highest GPU throughput at batch 1024 across all sizes
on both datasets (Table 17). At equal stored parame-
ters, its CPU step is 162×–13,566× faster than dense
models, and at least 3,289× faster at 730M (Section 5).
2. Background and related work
Linear recurrences.
An affine recurrence updates a state
ht ∈Rn as ht = ¯Atht−1+¯ut. When the coefficients do not
depend on ht−1, affine composition is associative and all
states can be computed in parallel over time (Blelloch, 1990;
Martin & Cundy, 2018). The transition is time-invariant
in S4, S4D, S5, and the LRU (Gu et al., 2022a;b; Smith
et al., 2023; Orvieto et al., 2023), and input-dependent in
the Mamba family (Gu & Dao, 2024; Dao & Gu, 2024;
Lahoti et al., 2026). Linear attention corresponds to a scalar
or identity transition with a rank-one write (Katharopoulos
et al., 2020; Schlag et al., 2021). To move beyond diagonal
transitions, recent work uses delta rules, Householder prod-
ucts, one-hot column transitions, structured linear CDEs,
and factored recurrent maps (Schlag et al., 2021; Yang et al.,
2024; 2025; Siems et al., 2025; Terzi´c et al., 2025; Walker
et al., 2025; Sutskever et al., 2011), while unitary RNNs
constrain the dynamics to preserve norm (Arjovsky et al.,
2016; Lezcano-Casado & Martínez-Rubio, 2019). All of
these models—like RWKV-7 (Peng et al., 2025)—read ev-
ery parameter at every step. By contrast, GRUs, LSTMs,
and the sLSTM of xLSTM gate on the state itself and must
train sequentially, whereas xLSTM’s mLSTM gates only on
the input and trains in parallel (Cho et al., 2014; Hochreiter
& Schmidhuber, 1997; Beck et al., 2024).
State tracking.
A model tracks a task if the correct label
is the unique logit maximiser at every labelled step of every
valid input sequence. Its margin is the smallest gap between
the correct logit and any other logit; in our constructions,
this margin is positive and independent of sequence length.
Group tasks require maintaining a running product, and they
expose the limits of diagonal recurrences, since positive di-
agonal transitions cannot track parity and general diagonal
transitions cannot track non-solvable groups (Sarrof et al.,
2024; Merrill et al., 2024; Grazzi et al., 2025; Shakerinava
et al., 2026). Non-diagonal recurrences overcome this bar-
rier (Grazzi et al., 2025; Siems et al., 2025; Terzi´c et al.,
2025). However, group tasks only permute states, whereas
general finite automata also reset them. In a permutation–
reset automaton, each input either permutes the states or

Sparse parameter access.
Mixtures of experts, hash lay-
ers, product-key memories, memory layers, and Engram
decouple stored capacity from active computation (Shazeer
et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Roller et al., 2021; Lam-
ple et al., 2019; Berges et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2026).
Token-indexed tables provide another form of sparse access
(Sadhukhan et al., 2026; Gemma Team, 2025; Zhou et al.,
2026; Tseng & De Sa, 2026), while hierarchical experts, soft
decision trees, and fast feedforward networks use trees for
conditional computation (Jordan & Jacobs, 1994; Frosst &
Hinton, 2017; Belcak & Wattenhofer, 2023a; Cho & Bengio,
2014). All of these methods retrieve a value that enters the
current step’s output; Yggdrasil instead retrieves the tran-
sition of its recurrence, a difference we compare at equal
storage in Section H. Following mixture-of-experts conven-
tion, we distinguish stored parameters (the full model, de-
termining memory footprint) from active parameters (those
read per token, determining step latency and compute). Yg-
gdrasil is built around this split, keeping active parameters
in one CPU core’s cache while the stored bank resides in
RAM.

3.2. Parallel evaluation and training
Because layer l’s router and additive input depend only on
the token and layer l −1, they can be evaluated at every
position in parallel once layer l −1 is computed. Only the
affine recurrence in Equation (1) runs over time. Affine
composition obeys

3.3. Hardware-aware design
Yggdrasil ties per-step cost to the memory hierarchy rather
than total parameter count. For fixed width, block size, input
ranks, and output size, a routed layer stores Θ(2D) parame-
ters but performs only O(D) work per token, because each
step reads D router nodes and one expert, while the transi-
tion, lower-layer input, and readout cost O(nb), O(nklow),
and O(nR + CR) regardless of bank size (Table 11). Dou-
bling the expert bank adds just one node comparison per
routed layer, trading Θ(2D) storage for O(D) compute.
On a CPU core, this trade keeps the active working set in
cache. Our medium and large models read roughly 100 and
280 KB of 8-bit weights per token, which fits comfortably
inside a single core’s 2 MB L2 cache, while the full bank
of 2D experts stays in RAM (Section J). Across all sizes, a
step touches between 30 and 281 KB (under 0.2% of stored
weights), running 2.6× to 5× faster than the same step
with cold caches (Section J). On a GPU, the step executes
as a single fused kernel over precomputed expert banks,
achieving the highest throughput of all evaluated models at
batch size 1024 (Section K).

4.1. Exact permutation tracking
Let each token specify a permutation matrix Πx on five
coordinates, and set v = (−2, −1, 0, 1, 2)⊤. A token-layer
expert with transition Πx and input Πxv −v produces, start-
ing from h0 = 0,
ht = Πxtht−1 + Πxtv −v
= Πxt · · · Πx1v −v.
Because the entries of v are distinct, ht uniquely identifies
the composed permutation. A linear readout then assigns the
largest logit to the corresponding group element with a pos-
itive margin independent of sequence length (Theorem 9).
This construction tracks both S5 and its subgroup A5 exactly.
Moreover, because its states, inputs, materialised transitions,
and readout coefficients all fit in 8-bit integers, 32-bit accu-
mulation evaluates the updates and logits without error at
any length.
The same construction extends to any finite group with a
faithful orthogonal representation of dimension m ≤b,
tracked by a single block with readout rank R ≥m, which
covers parity and cycle navigation as special cases. Parity
requires the eigenvalue −1, which an expert achieves via
either a negative decay or a π-rotation of a two-dimensional
block (−I). Non-solvable groups, however, require off-
diagonal transitions, because no stack of diagonal affine
layers with finitely many reachable states per layer can
track A5, even if each layer selects its coefficients from the
token and all states below it (Theorem 14). This establishes
a finite-state counterpart to bounded-precision limits on
recurrent models (Merrill et al., 2024; Grazzi et al., 2025;
Shakerinava et al., 2026).
4.2. Contextual writes and finite automata
A token-indexed affine map can reset its state on a desig-
nated symbol by setting Ax = 0. Writing the first symbol
and preserving it across all later steps is harder, because
the same symbol must trigger a write at t = 1 and leave
the state untouched afterwards. A single token layer with
non-expanding transitions and finitely many reachable states
cannot do this, whereas adding one routed layer can.
Theorem 1 (Contextual memory). A single token-only affine
layer whose transitions are non-expanding in the Euclidean
norm, ∥Ax∥2 ≤1, and which reaches finitely many states
cannot track the first symbol of an arbitrary binary input,
even with an arbitrary readout depending on (ht, xt). A
token layer and one routed layer with block size b ≥2 and
router depth D = 2 can track both the first-symbol task and
even pairs, whose label is [xt̸ = x1].
The positive construction uses two token-layer coordinates
to separate the first step from all later steps. Reading this
context, the routed layer writes the current symbol at t = 1
and applies the identity thereafter, without ever depending
on its own past state. The impossibility result is a spe-
cial case of Theorem 11, and the construction appears in
Theorem 12. The same obstruction governs modular arith-
metic with operator precedence, which a token layer and
one routed layer of depth 7 track modulo 5.
Permutations and contextual resets suffice for any finite
automaton. In its Krohn–Rhodes cascade (Section 2), every
component either permutes its states or resets to a fixed state,
selected by the token and the states of lower levels.
Theorem 2 (Finite automata). Every finite automaton can
be tracked by Yggdrasil with sufficiently large state width,
block size, readout rank and router depth. If the automaton
admits a permutation–reset cascade with ℓlevels, ℓ+ 1
routed layers suffice.
The proof assigns one routed layer to each cascade level and

4.3. A memory–computation separation
Sparse access lets a model query a large transition table
without reading all of it at every step. We formalise this
advantage using a family of hidden-counter tasks. Each
token carries an action at ∈{1, . . . , K} and a counter
increment, and the label is a running product in a fixed finite
group G whose factor is looked up in a table φ indexed
by the action and the counter modulo M, yielding |G|KM
distinct tasks (Theorem 15).
When M is the product of the first I primes, a token layer
tracks the counter via its residues and a single routed layer
retrieves the corresponding table entry. State width, block
size, router depth, and per-token work all grow only polyno-
mially in log M and log K (Theorem 17). By contrast, any
dense architecture with ω bits per weight that reads all of its
weights at every step requires

5. Experiments
We evaluate Yggdrasil across four axes: length generalisa-
tion on state tracking, accuracy and latency on DNA and
text at matched active parameter counts, transfer to DNA
classification on the Genomic Benchmarks, and scaling plus
ablations.
5.1. Protocol
Tasks. Our state-tracking suite covers the word problems
of S5 and A5 (Merrill et al., 2024; Grazzi et al., 2025;
Siems et al., 2025), parity, cycle navigation, and modular
arithmetic with operator precedence (Delétang et al., 2023),
trained on lengths 3–40 and tested on lengths 40–256. Next-
token prediction uses base-level hg38 and byte-level enwik8
(Section E).
Models and sizes. We compare against official public imple-
mentations of GRU, LSTM, Mamba-2, Mamba-2 + PKM,
a Llama-style Transformer, Gated DeltaNet, DeltaProduct
(with 2 or 4 Householder factors), PD-SSM, and HyenaDNA
(Cho et al., 2014; Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997; Dao
& Gu, 2024; Lample et al., 2019; Touvron et al., 2023;
Vaswani et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2025; Siems et al., 2025;
Terzi´c et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2023) (Section F). Text
models target 35k, 105k, and 330k active parameters; DNA
models target 27k, 90k, and 290k. Every model sits within
10% of its target using its native architecture, with Yggdrasil
accessing at most 0.22% of its stored parameters per token.
On state tracking, hyperparameter search also selects model
size.
Training and selection. For every model and task, we
run eight Bayesian-search trials on one seed, selecting by
validation bits on text and DNA and by out-of-distribution
validation accuracy on state tracking. We then train the
chosen configuration across three seeds and report the seed
with the best validation score (Section D.1). We follow
the length split of Walker et al. (2025) but report accuracy
on held-out test sequences rather than the best validation
accuracy reached during training.
Metrics. We score text and DNA by test bits per token
of the float model over the full test split. State tracking is
scored by final-answer accuracy averaged over test lengths,
with ≥0.90 marking a solved task (Delétang et al., 2023).
Latency. All CPU timings measure one batch-1 step com-
puting the full output distribution on a single pinned core.
Yggdrasil runs in our 8-bit integer kernel (Jacob et al., 2018),
and each baseline runs in its fastest public runtime (includ-
ing 8-bit quantised runtimes) that matches its float accuracy
(Section J).
5.2. State tracking
Yggdrasil is the only parallel-trainable model in Table 1 to
solve all five state-tracking tasks. Among the other paral-
lel baselines, DeltaProduct-4 solves four, DeltaProduct-2
solves three, Gated DeltaNet and PD-SSM solve two, and
Transformer and Mamba-2 solve none. Only PD-SSM and
Yggdrasil among the parallel models solve cycle naviga-
tion. GRU and LSTM also solve all five tasks, but require
sequential training.

5.4. Genomic Benchmarks
We evaluate transfer to DNA classification on the eight
tasks of the Genomic Benchmarks (Grešová et al., 2023)
under the Caduceus protocol (Schiff et al., 2024): mouse
enhancers (Mouse), coding versus intergenomic sequences
(Coding), human versus worm (Hum./worm), Cohn and
Ensembl human enhancers (Cohn, Ensembl), regulatory
elements (Reg.), open chromatin regions (OCR), and non-
TATA promoters (Non-TATA). Because each task classifies
a short sequence, a causal streaming model can score it as
it arrives. Pretrained on hg38 and fine-tuned per task at
roughly 470k active parameters (307.3M stored; Section M),
Yggdrasil averages 86.0%, outperforming both HyenaDNA
(85.3%) and Mamba (84.6%) as the strongest causal model
at matched active parameters (Table 2).

Speed at equal stored parameters. Matching models from

widening to 3,289×–13,566× at 730M (Table 15). Dense

Ablations. Removing block rotations sends S5 and A5 to
chance, and either sharing transitions or routing on the token
alone sends modular arithmetic to chance. On enwik8, re-
moving the routed-layer recurrence to store outputs instead
costs 0.244 bits per byte at equal storage and token-only
routing costs 0.153, whereas sharing a single transition per
routed layer stays within 0.003 bits per byte while losing
conditional resets on modular arithmetic (Section H).
6. Conclusion
Yggdrasil replaces computed recurrent transitions with con-
textual lookups into a tree-routed bank of experts. Address-
ing each bank from the token and the state of the layer below
yields contextual transitions while preserving parallel-scan
training. The resulting architecture tracks permutations,
conditional memory, and arbitrary finite automata; solves
all five evaluated state-tracking benchmarks in parallel; and
keeps active weights in CPU L2 cache while the expert bank
sits in RAM, improving both accuracy and CPU latency at
matched active parameter counts.
Limitations.
Yggdrasil shifts capacity from per-token
compute into stored parameters. Its largest models occupy
681.3 MB on enwik8 and 146.5 MB on hg38, which fits eas-
ily in laptop RAM but is much larger than the 1.5 MB foot-
print of the matched dense baselines. Our main comparisons
therefore hold active parameters fixed. Dense models with
comparable storage, such as a 41M-parameter Transformer-
XL (1.06 bpc on enwik8; Dai et al., 2019), achieve lower
bits at the cost of reading every parameter on every step.
Finally, our experiments cover up to 470k active parameters
and contexts of 256 bytes or 1024 bases; scaling to larger
active sizes, longer contexts, subword vocabularies, and
attention hybrids remains future work.

A. Further related work
The linear RNN of Fan et al. (2024) computes a block-diagonal transition from each input and extrapolates on regular
languages such as even pairs and modular arithmetic, without a bank of stored experts or a tree that addresses one. Linear
transformers fail parity, which their recurrent and self-referential extensions solve (Irie et al., 2023). Depth recovers part of
what diagonal transitions lose, since chains of diagonal selective layers approach the expressivity of dense ones as the chain
grows (Muca Cirone et al., 2024), and state-space models with non-negative gates recognise exactly the star-free languages
at finite precision (Sarrof et al., 2024). Grazzi et al. (2025) show that linear recurrences whose transitions are products of
generalised Householder matrices, each with eigenvalues in [−1, 1], can recognise any regular language, and Yggdrasil
reaches every finite automaton with one selected transition per layer, drawn from a routed bank and addressed by the layer
below.

B.12. Diagonal stacks
Proposition 14 (Diagonal stacks). Let every layer of a stack have diagonal transitions (real or complex), with transition
and input chosen from the token and the states of the previous layers, and let each layer reach finitely many states. If the
tokens generate A5, the stack does not track the running product in A5.

realise only solvable groups (Krohn & Rhodes, 1965). The proof shows that a stack of diagonal layers with finitely many
reachable states is such a cascade.

D.2. Hierarchical residual experts
Hierarchical residual experts follow hierarchical shrinkage in decision trees (Agarwal et al., 2022) and the fast feedforward
layers of UltraFastBERT, in which every node on the path contributes to the output (Belcak & Wattenhofer, 2023b). They
avoid the shrinking batch of hard trees, in which an independent expert would learn only from the few tokens that reach it
(Belcak & Wattenhofer, 2023a). They leave the function class unchanged, since a sum of skew-symmetric generators is
skew-symmetric and the decays are clipped after the sum.
E. Tasks and settings
Text and DNA. enwik8 is split into 90M, 5M and 5M bytes with V = 256 (Hutter, 2006). hg38 is read one nucleotide at a
time with V = 5, the four bases and the unknown base, on the interval split of Basenji and HyenaDNA (Kelley et al., 2018;
Nguyen et al., 2023) (Section L).
State tracking. A word problem is a sequence of group elements labelled at each position by their running product, and on
S5 and A5 the final answer is the final group element. Training draws fresh sequences at every step with random lengths
from 3 to 40 and averages the loss over every labelled position, as in the word problems of Liu et al. (2023) and Merrill et al.
(2024). Validation and test sets hold 4096 and 8192 sequences with lengths drawn uniformly from 40 to 256. Search trials
are validated every 2k steps. The validation set selects the trial and the checkpoint, as in Walker et al. (2025), and the test set
gives the score. The search also sets the size of every model (Tables 3 and 5).
State-tracking results. The learned models generalise beyond the training lengths on the tasks that the constructions of
Section 4 support. A token-layer block suffices for the group tasks, and contextual routing supports modular arithmetic with
precedence (Table 1). Mamba-2 remains near chance on both S5 and A5. DeltaProduct-4 reaches 0.935 on S5, consistent
with the finding that sufficiently long Householder products can learn this task (Siems et al., 2025).
F. Baselines
Implementations.
GRU and LSTM are the PyTorch modules with a token embedding and a linear output layer. Mamba-2 is
the mamba_ssm language model with expansion 2, convolution width 4 and, on text and DNA, head size P = min(64, d/4)
rounded down to a multiple of 8 (Dao & Gu, 2024). The Transformer is the transformers (Wolf et al., 2020) Llama
model (Touvron et al., 2023) with RMSNorm pre-normalisation, rotary position embeddings, a SwiGLU MLP of width 8d/3,
no biases, 2 to 4 heads and an untied output layer. Gated DeltaNet and DeltaProduct are the fla models (Yang & Zhang,
2024) with their default value expansion, output gate, short convolution and MLP ratio (Yang et al., 2025; Siems et al.,
2025). Gated DeltaNet and DeltaProduct use negative eigenvalues on the state tasks (Grazzi et al., 2025), and DeltaProduct
also on text and DNA, where it adds a forget gate. HyenaDNA is the model code of its authors with V = 5, trained from
scratch (Nguyen et al., 2023). PD-SSM is the released model code of its authors with their default dictionary size of 8,
trained from scratch (Terzi´c et al., 2025). Mamba-2 + PKM is the Mamba-2 language model with one product-key memory
layer (Lample et al., 2019), whose keys and values bring its stored parameters to those of Yggdrasil at each size, and its
number of heads and selected keys is searched with its other settings.
Active parameters.
Active parameters are the total minus the rows of an untied input table that the current token does
not read; every baseline therefore counts its whole output layer as active. For Yggdrasil they are one expert per layer, the

H. Ablations
The ablations change one part of Yggdrasil at a time and keep the token layer. Passing the output gives the next layer the
readout o(l)
t
= Prh(l)
t
of the layer below, of dimension 16, in place of its state of dimension 128, both in the router features
and in the layer input. The residual stream gives the next layer the sum of the projected outputs of all layers below. Routing
on the output keeps the state in the layer input and gives the router the output of the layer below. The shared transition keeps
one transition per routed layer, while the experts keep their own inputs and readouts. The diagonal variant removes the
rotations in every layer, and the non-negative variant clips the decays to [0, 1] in every layer while keeping the rotations,
whose complex eigenvalues lie outside [0, 1].
Storing outputs sets Ar = 0 in every routed-layer expert, so the state of a routed layer is the current expert input and carries
no recurrence. Its experts then store outputs, as in memory layers and Engram (Lample et al., 2019; Cheng et al., 2026).
Its input-map rank is raised from 32 to 49 to match the stored bytes of the full model. Every variant has its own search
of 8 trials on each task on which it runs, with the protocol of the full model (Table 4). On enwik8 the shared transition is
matched to the full model in active parameters. Every cell is the validation-best of 3 seeds.
Removing the rotations leaves S5 and A5 at 0.016, against chance levels of 0.008 and 0.017 (Table 9). Clipping the decays
to [0, 1] alone leaves the rotations to supply eigenvalues outside [0, 1], and S5 still reaches 0.901 and parity 0.999. Doing
both, so that every eigenvalue lies in [0, 1], sends parity, S5 and A5 to chance, as Theorem 14 and Grazzi et al. (2025)
predict. A shared transition or token-only routing leaves modular arithmetic at 0.290 and 0.260, near its chance level of 0.2.
What flows between layers does not decide state tracking, since all three such variants solve the four group tasks and keep
modular arithmetic far above chance.
On text and DNA the routed-layer recurrence and contextual routing carry most of the gain (Table 10). Removing the
routed-layer recurrence to store outputs costs 0.244 bits per byte on enwik8 and token-only routing 0.153, whereas sharing a
single transition per routed layer stays within 0.003 bits per byte on enwik8 and 0.004 on hg38. Expert-specific transitions
are therefore required for conditional resets on modular arithmetic (0.290 vs. 0.981) while contextual routing and recurrence
carry most of the language-modelling gain. The state passed between layers must be wide. Passing the 16-dimensional
output costs 0.040 bits on enwik8 and 0.016 on hg38, and a wide residual stream stays within 0.005 of passing the state.

J. CPU timing
Protocol.
Every model runs on one core of an Intel Xeon Gold 6426Y (Sapphire Rapids, 2 MB of L2 per core, 37.5 MB of
shared L3 per socket), pinned to one thread with the whole node reserved. A timed step consumes one token and computes
the full distribution over all V classes at batch 1. Each measurement streams real test tokens with the state carried, skips the
first 10,000 tokens and reports the median, over 5 rounds of 2000 steps, of the mean time per token in each round. The
Transformer keeps a sliding key–value cache over its training window T, and HyenaDNA is timed as in Section L. The
comparison at equal stored parameters uses random weights from the initialisation of each model. Speedups are computed
from unrounded times.
Runtimes.
Yggdrasil runs in our 8-bit integer kernel (Jacob et al., 2018). A step reads the token-layer expert of the token,
traverses each tree router on ν(h(l−1)
t
), reads one expert per routed layer and computes the logits, with 8-bit states, routing
weights and readouts, fixed-point block transitions and AVX-512 VNNI integer dot products. The kernel stores the token
input of each expert either as the precomputed rows Tr[x] (table mode) or as the 8-bit factor Ur from which it computes
Tr[x] at every step (factored mode). Both modes compute the same integer function. Each baseline is timed in the fastest
runtime of Table 12 that passes the accuracy check. Compiled runtimes are driven by a C++ loop, and ONNX Runtime
(ONNX Runtime developers, 2021) binds every input and output once to preallocated buffers so that a step is a single call.
At equal stored parameters Yggdrasil runs in factored mode on enwik8 and in table mode on hg38 (Table 16).

K. GPU timing
Every model runs on one NVIDIA A100-PCIE-40GB with torch 2.10.0 and CUDA 12.8, each baseline in its fastest public
GPU path. These are the fused cuDNN kernels for GRU and LSTM, mamba_ssm for Mamba-2, the Hugging Face Llama
decode with FlashAttention and a key–value cache for the Transformer, the fla kernels for Gated DeltaNet and DeltaProduct
and the Hugging Face model for HyenaDNA, streamed as in Section L. Yggdrasil runs one fused Triton 3.6.0 kernel that
updates all layers, followed by the output layer, with the expert banks precomputed. Every model runs under CUDA graphs
with one step per replay, whose outputs match eager execution to within 2 × 10−6, and computes the full output distribution
in fp32. Each measurement is the median of 5 rounds of 512 steps after 64 warm-up steps.

L. Genomics
Split.
We use the hg38 intervals of the Basenji BED file (Kelley et al., 2018), which the HyenaDNA code loads for its
training, validation and test sets (Nguyen et al., 2023). No test interval overlaps a training or validation interval. HyenaDNA
is trained from scratch at the three sizes with the protocol of Section D.1.
HyenaDNA timing.
The official HyenaDNA code has no recurrent decode. Its CPU time is that of offline scoring, in
which one forward pass over a window of 1024 bases is divided by the window length. This is a lower bound on its time in
streaming. A streaming step recomputes the window for every new base and takes 1,605, 5,003 and 7,297 µs on one core at
the three sizes, 876 to 997 times its offline time. Its GPU time is that of streaming.
M. Genomic Benchmarks
We follow the protocol of Caduceus (Schiff et al., 2024) on the eight tasks of the Genomic Benchmarks (Grešová et al.,
2023). Yggdrasil is first pretrained on the hg38 training split by next-base prediction for 10,000 steps at batch 1024 with
windows of 1024 bases, about 10.5B bases, with reverse-complement augmentation. The model has state size 336, 4 routed
layers (5 layers in total), router depth D = 10 and input rank 84, and its learning rate of 6.23 · 10−3 comes from the
hyperparameter search. It stores 307.3M parameters and reads about 470k active parameters per base, and it reaches 1.575
test bits per base on hg38. Every fine-tune starts from this checkpoint. Each task is run on 5 cross-validation splits with
seeds 1 to 5 and a 90/10 split of the training set into training and validation, with early stopping on validation accuracy.
Yggdrasil trains for 10 epochs at batch 256. For each task we take the learning rate in {10−3, 2 · 10−3} and the choice of
reverse-complement augmentation with the best mean validation accuracy over the 5 splits, and report its mean test accuracy.
