Abstract
Geospatial foundation models can reuse information learned from large Earth-observation archives,
but their value for climate resilience depends on whether that information transfers to unfamiliar events,
regions and observing conditions. This review connects advances in deep learning with the evidence
needed for hazard monitoring and early warning. A descriptive audit of 137 full-text hazard-application
studies is interpreted alongside a separate corpus of 37 model and benchmark records. Ninety-eight
application studies reported an out-of-distribution experiment, whereas headline evaluations included
held-out events in 31 studies, regions in 25 and sensors in only two. None of the ten studies coded for
geospatial foundation-model fine-tuning used sensor holdout as a headline test. Uncertainty-related
methods were recorded in 16 studies, including two with explicitly calibrated probabilistic output.
These overlapping counts describe evaluation practice within the coded corpus, not a ranking of
model families. We distinguish Earth-observation representation learning from atmospheric forecasting
and examine how labels, observability, forecast lead time and decision requirements constrain their
contribution across flood, wildfire, drought, heat, cryosphere and cascading-hazard applications. We
then develop a decision-backward evaluation framework: define the action and information available
when it must be taken, reproduce the relevant shift, retain a matched task-specific baseline, and report
misses, false alarms, reliability and end-to-end latency. The framework turns transferability from a
broad model claim into a testable requirement for climate adaptation and risk management.
1. Introduction
A flood map delivered after inundation and a warning
issued before peak discharge can both be accurate and still
serve very different purposes. The first supports inspection
and recovery; the second must arrive early enough to change
an action. Their usefulness depends not only on a model’s
predictions, but also on what was observable, when informa-
tion became available and which mistakes matter to the user.
This distinction is central to applying geospatial artificial
intelligence (GeoAI) to climate resilience. A model that
recognizes familiar landscapes is not necessarily a model
that remains reliable during an unfamiliar extreme.
Deep learning has expanded the use of satellite imagery,
environmental time series and physical-model output for
Earth-system analysis. Geospatial foundation models extend
this development by pretraining representations on large,
heterogeneous archives and adapting them to downstream
tasks. In parallel, machine-learning weather models predict
the evolution of atmospheric fields. These developments
create opportunities for climate monitoring and adaptation,
but their evaluation must connect computational skill with
an environmental process and a practical decision [1–3].
Transfer is particularly important for hazards because the
next event is not a random new image from an unchanged
distribution. It may occur in a different basin, biome or
built environment; coincide with persistent cloud; or require
a sensor and processing chain that were absent during
development. Reference labels also vary: surface water, newly
inundated land, active fire, burned area and structural damage

are different targets. Generalization therefore has several
dimensions, and a high score under one split cannot establish
performance under all of them. Spatially and temporally
structured validation provides the methodological foundation
for making these distinctions [4, 5].
Existing reviews have explained AI applications to ex-
tremes, the progression of satellite hazard-monitoring meth-
ods and the opportunities for integrated early-warning sys-
tems. Benchmark studies have also shown why apparently
comparable foundation-model scores can depend on eval-
uation settings. Our contribution is complementary: we
examine the evaluation designs used in hazard applications
and connect them to the decisions that a deployment must
support. Table 1 positions this contribution without treating
architectural novelty, broader pretraining or a particular
model family as evidence of operational readiness [6–8].
The review addresses three questions. Which shifts
are actually tested in the coded hazard literature? Which
constraints can representation learning plausibly relieve,
and which require better observations, process information
or decision design? What controlled experiments would
establish a useful contribution from pretraining? We answer
these questions through a fixed descriptive audit, a synthesis
of relevant model and application studies, and a decision-
backward protocol. The emphasis is climate adaptation and
risk management: improvements must be judged against the
information and resources available to the people expected
to act on them.

2. Review approach and interpretation
2.1. Evidence sources and corpus boundaries
The application corpus was assembled through 35 Ope-
nAlex title-and-abstract queries covering publications from
2015 to 2026 (search date 1 November 2026), with up to 200
relevance-ranked records retrieved per query under the anony-
mous daily API budget. The query set spanned foundation-
model and self-supervised remote-sensing terms together
with flood, wildfire, drought, heat, cryosphere, compound-
hazard, damage-mapping, forecasting and evaluation families.
Exact query strings, reported hit counts and retrieved counts
are retained in the project search log and are available from
the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Retrieval
yielded 6,803 raw records, 6,092 unique DOI/title keys after
deduplication, and 5,867 records after near-duplicate title-
twin removal. Automated title prescreening and human title–
abstract screening retained 141 primary candidates spanning
flood, wildfire, drought, heat, cryosphere, damage assessment
and compound or cascading hazards. Evaluation-design
coding used the 137 records with usable full text. This is
a structured critical review with a descriptive evidence audit,
rather than a pooled meta-analysis of predictive accuracy or
a PRISMA systematic review.
A separate collection of 37 model and benchmark records
provides architectural context: 18 Earth-observation founda-
tion models, nine weather or climate models, five evaluation
benchmarks and five benchmark datasets. These records are
not added to the denominator of 137 application studies. Lit-
erature consulted to update the narrative and verify references
through 22 November 2026 informs the synthesis but does not
retrospectively alter the audit counts. This separation keeps
the descriptive results stable while allowing discussion of
recent developments.
2.2. Coding and units of analysis
Application studies were coded from usable full text
under a fixed coding protocol. Each record received codes
for hazard, task, modality, model family, pretraining, study
geography (locations, never affiliations), headline valida-
tion design, additional out-of-distribution tests, baselines,
uncertainty-related methods, explainability, and code or
weight availability. The unit of analysis is the study record,
not the image chip, model run or country mention. A
record can carry several hazard, model and validation tags.
Coders worked only from the study text: when a validation
description was ambiguous, the more conservative code
was assigned; abstract-only evidence was not used to infer
evaluation designs. Study-level coding tables for the 137
usable application records (from 138 verified) and the parallel
37-record foundation-model and benchmark extraction are
available from the corresponding author upon reasonable
request.
The headline validation code describes the test supporting
a study’s principal reported evaluation. An additional out-
of-distribution experiment can occur elsewhere in the same
paper. Consequently, the indicator ’any out-of-distribution
test’ and the counts of specific headline holdouts answer
different questions. Code availability denotes an explicit
availability statement or link in the coded paper, not a
continuing guarantee that a repository remains accessible.
An uncertainty-related code records a reported method; it
does not by itself establish calibration, coverage or suitability
for a decision.
Table 2 defines the main contrasts used in the synthe-
sis. For sensor change, the distinction between an unseen
sensor, a missing modality and adaptation to a new input
configuration is especially important. These settings may all
be operationally relevant, but they do not establish the same
form of transfer. Similarly, downstream test separation does
not establish that a location or acquisition period was absent
from a foundation model’s pretraining archive.
2.3. Descriptive synthesis
Unless a different denominator is stated, percentages
are calculated as 100 times the number of coded studies
divided by 137. Model-group comparisons use ten foundation-
model fine-tuning studies and 127 other studies. Hazard-
specific figures use the corresponding hazard subset. Cross-
tabulations count records carrying both tags; sums across
hazards or model families are therefore not counts of distinct
papers. No significance test is used to claim that one model
group has a better evaluation culture than another, and no
cross-paper accuracy average is calculated.

3. From observations to decisions
3.1. Separate the product from the action
Figure 1 contrasts two flood pathways. In post-event map-
ping, an optical or synthetic aperture radar (SAR) acquisition
is converted into a surface-water or inundation product and
checked against independent events or regions. The result can
prioritize field inspection. In pre-peak warning, the inputs
must be those available at the forecast issue time: rainfall,
upstream flow, meteorology and other admissible antecedent
information. A warning is useful only if it meets the required
lead time and has acceptable reliability. A retrospective map,
even a highly accurate one, cannot by itself demonstrate this
forecasting capability.
The task definition determines what counts as a valid
label. Water observed at satellite overpass is not necessarily
maximum flood extent, and permanent water is not newly
flooded land. Acquisition timing, cloud, canopy, topography
and mixed pixels can change the relationship between a
label and the event of interest. Reviews of flood mapping
and cloud-aware optical mapping systems show why sensor
characteristics and reference construction must remain part
of the evaluation, rather than being treated as preprocessing
details [6, 9].
3.2. Match the observation to the process
The coded studies most frequently used multispectral
optical imagery (70), digital elevation models or static covari-
ates (54), SAR (46), reanalysis or meteorological information
(34), in situ observations (29), coarse-resolution optical data
(29), very-high-resolution optical imagery (28), and thermal
infrared data (21). These are overlapping modality tags. Their
diversity reflects different measurement problems: an active-
fire signal, a canopy-temperature estimate and a snow-water-
equivalent estimate are not alternative labels for the same
phenomenon.
For an operational comparison, all models should receive
a clearly defined information set. A model using more
bands, later observations or additional local labels has an
informational advantage that should not be attributed entirely
to its architecture. In H2O-Net’s PlanetScope evaluation, the
RGB model achieved 60.92% mean intersection over union
(mIoU), compared with 42.63% for an RGB U-Net. The H2O-
Net result increased to 74.52% with near-infrared added. The
matched RGB comparison tests methods; the additional-band
comparison changes the information available to the method
[10]. This distinction is simple, but essential when interpret-
ing claims of multimodal or foundation-model superiority.
4. Foundation models: capabilities and
evidence boundaries
4.1. Earth-observation representation learning
Figure 2 summarizes two pretraining mechanisms, not a
ranking of their hazard performance. SatMAE learns from
masked temporal and multispectral satellite observations. Its
representation is trained to recover missing information from
the visible context. CROMA combines paired optical and
radar encoders, cross-modal contrastive learning and masked
reconstruction through multimodal fusion. These objectives
encourage useful representations, but downstream flood or
wildfire performance still depends on the adaptation data and
evaluation setting [11, 12].

The distinction matters when the operational sensor
configuration changes. Within-modality pretraining may
support a downstream task whose observations resemble the
training inputs. Cross-modal alignment creates a reason to
investigate transfer when one modality is unavailable; it is not
a measurement of that transfer. CROMA’s separate encoders
and fusion machinery are compressed into the schematic
for readability. A test of one missing input, a test on a new
satellite and a test of adaptation after acquiring target-sensor
labels should therefore be reported separately.
Recent models broaden the range of inputs and adap-
tation strategies. DOFA conditions processing on spec-
tral information to accommodate heterogeneous sensors;
AnySat addresses varying modalities, resolutions and scales;
TerraMind combines multimodal learning with generative
capabilities; and Prithvi-EO-2.0 extends multitemporal Earth-
observation pretraining [13–16]. These are different design
choices with different testable advantages. For a hazard user,
the relevant question is whether a particular choice improves
the specified task under matched labels, input availability and
computational resources. Generated or inferred modalities
must not be treated as independent measurements of the
environment.

4.2. Atmospheric forecasting is a parallel pathway
Pangu-Weather and GraphCast illustrate advances in
medium-range atmospheric prediction, while GenCast pro-
duces probabilistic forecasts, NeuralGCM combines learned
components with atmospheric dynamics, and Aurora adapts
a foundation-model approach to several Earth-system fore-
casting tasks [17–21]. Their outputs and verification settings
differ from those of an encoder adapted to segment flood
water or burned terrain. Neither lineage is the final stage of
the other.
Connecting the lineages could support impact-oriented
warning: atmospheric forecasts inform hydrological, fire or
exposure models, and Earth observations update the state
of the land surface. However, each connection adds an
uncertainty and timing requirement. A useful coupled test

4.3. A benchmark score is conditional evidence
GEO-Bench and PANGAEA organize diverse Earth-
observation tasks and expose the importance of consistent
adaptation settings and strong supervised baselines [22, 23].
A benchmark result is conditional on its labels, split, decoder,
tuning budget and metric. Differences among those choices
can change model ordering. Corley and colleagues’ audit of
foundation-model reporting reinforces the need to examine
the underlying comparison rather than borrowing a state-of-
the-art label from another paper [8].
For hazard applications, repeated use of a familiar event
can also narrow the effective diversity of the evidence. Many
papers evaluated on one held-out flood do not amount to
tests on many independent floods. Dataset-level separation,
event-level replication and pretraining provenance should
be reported together. The scientific value of a new model is
clearest when its claimed advantage survives a controlled
comparison and remains visible across several relevant shifts,
rather than depending on one favorable partition.
5. Uncertainty, explanations and operational
reliability
5.1. Uncertainty reporting is not the same as
calibration
Figure 3 shows the uncertainty-related coding in the
application corpus. Sixteen studies (11.7%) had a recorded
method: five used uncalibrated probabilistic outputs, four
ensembles, two explicitly calibrated probabilities, and five
other uncertainty-related approaches. The remaining 121
had no method recorded under this coding scheme. These
categories describe reporting practices; they are not a score
of the quality of every uncertainty estimate. In particular,
an output between zero and one is not automatically a
trustworthy probability.
Calibration asks whether events assigned a given prob-
ability occur at a corresponding frequency. Modern neural
networks can require explicit calibration, and uncertainty
behavior can deteriorate under distribution shift [24, 25].
For a flood map, reliability should therefore be checked
on held-out events or regions at the thresholds used for
action. For a warning, probability verification should be
conditioned on lead time and event severity. An overall
calibration statistic may conceal poor behavior for rare but
consequential outcomes.
The required uncertainty summary depends on the prod-
uct. Segmentation may need spatial error summaries and
event-level confidence intervals; a peak-flow forecast may
need prediction intervals, threshold exceedance probabili-
ties and coverage by lead time. Ensemble spread can be
informative, but agreement among models does not establish

5.2. Explanations should answer a defined question
Explainability methods were recorded in 27 studies
(19.7%). A visually plausible saliency map or feature ranking
can help diagnose a model, but it does not demonstrate that
the explanation faithfully represents the learned relationship.
Saliency sanity checks show why visual appeal alone is
insufficient [26]. Explanations should be linked to a question:
whether a model depends on an implausible feature, whether
behavior changes across environments, or whether an input
failure can be identified before use.
For impact studies, predictive explanation must also be
separated from causal attribution. A relationship between
vegetation and structure loss, for example, may be useful
for risk screening without proving that changing vegetation
alone would produce the predicted change in loss. Spatial
validation and an explicit observational interpretation are
important when translating explainable GeoAI into planning
advice. The Palisades fire study provides a recent example
of this distinction between model-based associations and
intervention claims [27].
5.3. Reproducibility and latency are part of the
result
Explicit code availability was recorded in 52 studies
(38.0%). Table 4 brings this result together with the other
trust-related indicators. Reproducibility requires more than
a model name: preprocessing versions, band definitions,
normalization, label rules, split identifiers, adaptation settings
and decision thresholds determine the experiment. FAIR prin-
ciples provide a useful basis for organizing these resources
so that other users can locate and reuse them [28].
End-to-end latency is equally important. Inference time
measures only one part of the path from an acquisition or
forecast to a delivered product. Data access, quality control,
atmospheric correction, registration, tiling, postprocessing
and review can dominate the operational delay. A comparison
intended for deployment should report the complete delay
and the hardware and input conditions under which it was
measured. Efficiency is a genuine advantage when it preserves
the decision-relevant performance needed by a resource-
constrained user; it is not a substitute for that performance.
6. What the application audit establishes
6.1. Model families and hazard coverage
The audit contains 56 flood, 51 wildfire, 16 drought, 14
damage, 11 cryosphere, nine heat, five compound-hazard
and five other hazard labels. Because studies can receive
several labels, these numbers describe coverage rather than
exclusive categories. Figure 4 shows the corresponding
model-family cross-tabulation. Encoder-decoder segmen-
tation was recorded in 71 distinct studies, convolutional
networks in 38, transformers or vision transformers in 31,
and classical machine-learning comparators in 29. Named
geospatial foundation-model fine-tuning occurred in ten.

6.2. Where the evidence is located
Figure 5 maps the locations recorded in the 110 geo-
graphically assignable studies. The United States accounts
for the largest number of country mentions, with other
territories represented unevenly. This distribution should
be read alongside the prominence of flood and wildfire
studies and the reuse of multinational benchmark datasets. A
country can be represented through training data, test data or
both; a filled polygon does not establish that the model has
been independently evaluated across all environments in that
country.
The practical implication is to evaluate geographic ap-
plicability before extrapolating. Environmental similarity,
sensor characteristics and label availability can differ substan-
tially within the same country and across borders. An area-
of-applicability analysis can help identify conditions unlike
the training data, but it complements rather than replaces
an independent test [29]. Reporting the actual evaluation
locations and their environmental characteristics is therefore
more informative than presenting a global map of data
coverage as evidence of global readiness.
6.3. Which shifts receive headline tests?
Ninety-eight studies (71.5%) reported at least one out-of-
distribution experiment. The specific headline codes were
random split in 48 studies (35.0%), held-out event in 31
(22.6%), held-out time in 27 (19.7%), held-out region in 25
(18.2%), spatial block in seven (5.1%), and held-out sensor in
two (1.5%). Figure 6 shows their distribution across hazards.
These codes overlap, so they must not be read as a partition
of the corpus or as successive levels attained by mutually
exclusive groups.
Random splitting is the largest single displayed category,
not a majority of the corpus. It can be appropriate for
a question about interpolation within a well-defined data
distribution. Its limitation arises when an interpolation result
is used to support a claim about a new event, place or sensor.
Conversely, merely reporting an extra transfer experiment
does not establish adequate performance for a specified
deployment. The audit records whether a test was present; the
strength of its result still depends on the held-out conditions,
reference quality and comparison design [4].
The rarity of headline sensor tests is the most concen-
trated gap in the displayed validation designs. It motivates
targeted experiments under sensor change, especially where
operational continuity depends on radar during cloud or on
substituting one constellation for another. It does not show
that all models fail under such changes, nor that only two
papers in the wider literature have ever considered sensor
differences. The inference concerns the principal test designs
in this coded sample.

7. Different hazards require different tests
Table 5 links the hazard coverage to observations, targets
and limiting factors. These factors are working hypotheses
about a hazard-task pair, not permanent properties of an
entire hazard. The same wildfire can generate an image-
segmentation problem, a spread forecast and a post-fire
hydrological assessment, each with different information
requirements.
7.1. Flood: mapping water and anticipating
discharge
Flood applications provide the clearest contrast between
representation learning and forecasting. Sen1Floods11 has
enabled shared flood-mapping experiments, while Kuro Siwo
expands multi-temporal SAR observations and manually
annotated events [30, 31]. These datasets support repro-
ducible comparisons, but the scope of a result remains tied to
the labels and event selection. Cloud-aware optical systems
further show that operational mapping involves acquisition
availability and end-to-end processing, not segmentation
alone [9].
Prithvi-based studies illustrate why the baseline must
receive comparable attention. Early work assessed a geospa-
tial foundation model on flood inundation mapping, while
U-Prithvi combined a pretrained encoder with a U-Net
architecture [32, 33]. In the latter study’s strengthened-
baseline comparison, Bolivia mean IoU was 87.77% for
U-Net and 87.70% for U-Prithvi. This near equality does
not invalidate pretraining; it shows that the conclusion
changes when the task-specific comparator is strengthened. A
model’s advantage should therefore be attributed to the tested
configuration, not to the foundation-model label in isolation.
A recent compact-model preprint similarly compared
pretraining, random initialization, frozen features, U-Net and
computational cost under a common water-mapping task.
Its results support an accuracy-efficiency trade-off rather
than universal superiority of a pretrained encoder [34]. For
warning, the relevant evidence is different: forecasting studies
such as global prediction in ungauged watersheds evaluate
future streamflow rather than a satellite-observed water mask
[35]. A mapping benchmark cannot substitute for basin and
event holdouts at useful warning lead times.
7.2. Wildfire: distinguish detection, aftermath and
spread
Active-fire detection identifies an ongoing thermal or
spectral signal; burned-area mapping identifies a changed
surface; severity and structure-loss analyses characterize
consequences. Fire-spread and danger forecasting concern

future conditions. Conflating these targets can make a method
appear to solve a wider operational problem than it actually
addresses. Models for danger forecasting that combine dy-
namic environmental information with geographic context
illustrate why weather and location must be treated jointly
rather than replaced by a larger optical encoder (Shams Eddin
et al., 2023).
Event-separated datasets are valuable, but the label-
generation process remains part of the task. The Sentinel-
2 active-fire benchmark covering 25 California wildfires
assigns each wildfire to a single partition and provides explicit
metadata and rule-derived masks [36]. Such a split prevents
the same wildfire from appearing in training and testing; it
does not turn a spectral rule into an independent observation
of every fire boundary. Evaluation against separately reviewed
or independently observed targets is needed when the claim
extends beyond reproducing the labeling rule.
For structure-loss screening, pre-event inputs, spatial sep-
aration and the distinction between association and causation
are especially important [27]. A decision about treatment
or land-use change needs more evidence than a model
explanation, even when predictive discrimination is useful.
Operational wildfire comparisons should report misses in
difficult settings, such as small fires or complex interfaces, as
well as aggregate segmentation or classification scores.
The Kincade Fire vineyard analysis provides a related
landscape-scale example: multisource observations can char-
acterize wildfire impacts and exposure at field scale, but a
spatial association between land cover and burn severity is
not by itself a transferable estimate of a management inter-
vention’s effect [34]. This distinction matters when remote-
sensing evidence is used to support adaptation planning
across agricultural and urban landscapes.
7.3. Drought: the reference defines the forecast
Drought is expressed through several related but distinct
targets, including soil moisture, vegetation condition and
categorical drought assessments. DroughtCast forecasts the
United States Drought Monitor, whereas vegetation-health
and soil-moisture forecasting studies predict different envi-
ronmental quantities [37–39]. A model can be skillful relative
to one reference without reproducing another. Reported
accuracy therefore depends partly on the target definition,
spatial support, aggregation and forecast horizon.
Temporal holdouts are necessary for an operational
forecasting claim, but they should be supplemented by tests
of rare severity classes and geographic transfer when those
uses are intended. Inputs should be screened for availability
at issue time, including the release timing of reanalysis and
monitoring products. A useful experiment compares the effect
of changing the reference target with the effect of changing the
model, while retaining a common split and evaluation scale.
This tests the label-definition hypothesis without assuming
that target choice always dominates architecture.
7.4. Heat: from atmospheric skill to exposure
Heat applications link atmospheric forecasts, near-surface
air temperature, land-surface temperature and human expo-
sure. These variables are related but not interchangeable.
Satellite thermal observations provide surface information;
ground observations and atmospheric models are needed
to interpret the conditions experienced by people. Next-day

Tests on recent high-impact heat events show that strong
average weather-forecast performance does not ensure equally
strong performance in an extreme case [41]. Such results
identify a limitation of the evaluated systems and lead times;
they do not, on their own, establish a fundamental physi-

stratify errors by intensity, lead time and relevant population
or urban setting, while retaining an appropriate forecasting
baseline. The nine heat labels in the audit identify a small
evidence subset, not a measure of the societal importance of
heat risk.
7.5. Cryosphere: sparse references and changing
observation conditions
Snow-water-equivalent estimation, glacier-front delin-
eation and glacial-lake mapping have different reference and
observation requirements. Regional transfer of a long short-
term memory model for snow-water equivalent addresses
a different question from adapting an image encoder to
delineate lakes or glacier fronts [42, 43]. Independent regions,
acquisition conditions and reference sources should therefore
be identified explicitly, rather than summarized by a generic
cryosphere accuracy claim.
SSL4SAR reports a 293 m mean calving-front distance
error on the CaFFe dataset. On a separate multi-annotator
subset, its ensemble result was 75 m, compared with 38 m
disagreement among human annotations [42]. The subset
comparison indicates remaining model error relative to
expert agreement; the different evaluation populations must
not be merged into one model-versus-human comparison.
Annotation variability is a relevant reference property, not
proof of an unavoidable error floor.
7.6. Damage and cascading impacts
Damage mapping often confronts changes in imaging
conditions, building form and disaster context. Work on
transferring a global building-mapping model to heteroge-
neous post-disaster imagery demonstrates that the extraction
problem itself can shift before any downstream damage
classification is attempted [44]. A held-out disaster should
therefore preserve the operational target and make clear
whether local labels, pre-event images or sensor-specific
adaptation were used.
Cascading hazards add a temporal connection between
processes. Burn severity can influence subsequent debris-
flow hazard, and fire-related canopy change can alter snow
persistence. Studies connecting pre-fire conditions, burn
severity and debris-flow exposure, and recent multisource
work on wildfire severity and snow persistence, illustrate
these linked pathways [45, 46]. A credible cascade test must
follow the sequence of events and propagate uncertainty
across stages. High accuracy for the first map does not
establish the skill of the downstream impact forecast. The
five explicitly compound-hazard studies in the audit motivate
shared event-chain benchmarks rather than conclusions about
the frequency of cascading risk itself.
8. Choosing the test from the decision
backward
8.1. Specify the claim before selecting the split
The proposed protocol begins with an action, not an
architecture. State who will use the output, what decision
it informs, when that decision is made and which information
is available by then. Specify the target, spatial support,
relevant event severity, acceptable delay, and the costs or
operational consequences of misses and false alarms. The
evaluation should then reproduce the change that could
invalidate that decision: a new event, region, season, sensor
or input-availability condition.

8.2. Experiment 1: maintain mapping under sensor
change
Consider an agency that must continue flood mapping
when cloud prevents an optical acquisition. A controlled
experiment would define the same target event and reference
labels, match the adaptation-label and tuning budgets, and
compare the available sensor configurations. A task-specific
baseline should be retained alongside the pretrained model.
Observation time, spatial resolution and the information
carried by each sensor should be aligned where possible and
otherwise reported as part of the operational comparison.
Two questions should be separated. A controlled sensor
comparison asks how performance changes when the observ-
ing configuration changes under closely matched conditions.
An operational substitution test asks which available product
provides the best decision support when perfect input parity is
impossible. Neither should be described as zero-shot sensor
transfer if target-sensor labels or model updates are used.
Pretraining overlap and any target-domain adaptation must
be disclosed.
Thresholds should be selected before final testing. Report
missed inundation, false alarms, event-level performance
and uncertainty, and stratify failures by conditions relevant
to response, such as cloud, forest, dense development and
narrow water features. Report the delay from data availability
to the delivered map. A gain in average overlap is useful only
when it does not obscure a failure mode that prevents the
intended inspection or response decision.

issue time and evaluate storms or water years that are excluded
from model fitting, tuning and calibration. Historical data
should be reconstructed as they would have been available
operationally, rather than supplemented with later observa-
tions or retrospectively revised products. The baseline should

Evaluate peak magnitude, arrival time, threshold ex-

8.4. Evaluate whether pretraining changes the
decision
A minimal attribution experiment compares the pre-
trained architecture with a randomly initialized counterpart,
a strong task-specific model, and the operational or classical
baseline relevant to the action. Match input bands, spatial and
temporal support, labels and tuning budgets; document differ-
ences that cannot be matched. Vary the available downstream
labels to test label efficiency rather than inferring it from
one training set. Repeated seeds quantify training variability,
whereas repeated held-out events quantify a different source
of variability; both should be identified rather than pooled
indiscriminately.
The practical output is a trade-off among decision-
relevant errors, lead time, cost and coverage. A compact
model may be preferable when it supports deployment on
limited hardware, while a larger model may be justified where
a reproducible gain changes the action. The result should
establish the condition under which the model helps, not
simply declare a winner across settings that were never jointly
evaluated.
9. Discussion and research priorities
9.1. From model capability to evidence of useful
transfer
The audit identifies an imbalance between the breadth
of potential deployment conditions and the specificity of the
tests reported as headline evaluations. This is not an argument
against foundation models. It is an argument for experiments
that can identify their contribution. A reusable encoder is
valuable when it improves a defined task, reduces the labels
or resources required, or extends reliable coverage under a
meaningful shift. Those outcomes should be demonstrated
directly.
Four research priorities follow. First, build event-diverse
and sensor-explicit benchmarks that distinguish observed
water, inundation, active fire, burned area and impact targets.
Second, report pretraining provenance alongside downstream
holdouts so that representation reuse can be separated from
evaluation independence. Third, evaluate calibration and
failure modes under the same shifts as accuracy. Fourth, test
coupled weather and Earth-observation systems at the issue
times and lead times required by an actual warning decision.
These priorities complement broader calls for integrated early
warning and transparent benchmarking [3, 8].
9.2. Test the limiting-factor hypotheses
The framework proposes that representation learning
is most useful when the observations contain the needed
signal and the principal difficulty is learning a transferable
representation from limited downstream labels. This predicts

an advantage that should be measured across label budgets
and independently held-out environments. When visibility is
the dominant limitation, adding an informative observation
may matter more than enlarging an encoder. When future
process evolution is the challenge, forcing information and
forecast lead time may dominate a mapping architecture.
When a decision depends on calibrated probabilities, a higher
overlap score alone is insufficient.
These propositions require intervention-style compar-
isons, not categorical declarations that one constraint always
controls a hazard. Change the sensor while holding the event
and reference as stable as possible; change the lead time
while preserving the forecast task; or change the reference
index while preserving the model and partition. The resulting
performance differences can reveal which limitation is most
important in that setting. Several constraints can bind simul-
taneously, and an improvement in one may expose another.
9.3. Transfer lessons from agricultural and urban
monitoring
Adjacent environmental applications help clarify these
evaluation choices without being counted as additional hazard
studies. The Sentinel-2 crop-yield review highlights the need
to interpret imagery alongside crop development and the
intended prediction scale [47]. Drone-based and satellite time-
series studies of branched broomrape illustrate a different
target-identification problem: an observed crop-stress signal
must be connected to the reference condition and observation
timing [48, 49]. These studies concern biotic stress, not direct
validation of drought or climate-extreme prediction. Their
relevance is methodological: visually similar stress responses
should not be assigned the same cause without supporting
evidence.
Spatial mapping studies provide another useful distinc-
tion. Farmland extent and visible-boundary mapping from
NAIP imagery concern observable land-cover geometry, not a
legal cadastral boundary [50]. Optical urban-canopy mapping
and resilience screening connect a remotely sensed feature
to a planning context, but do not turn canopy detection
into a validated heat-exposure forecast [51]. These examples
reinforce the need to define the output before selecting the
model, preserve scene or geographic independence when that
is the deployment question, and separate a mapped proxy
from the decision variable it is intended to inform.
9.4. Scope of the evidence and implications for use
The descriptive percentages characterize the accessible,
coded corpus. Full-text availability, the search source and
the distribution of hazards influence its composition. The
figures do not estimate the worldwide prevalence of a practice
or provide causal comparisons among model families. The
small fine-tuning and sensor-holdout subsets also make
broad group-level conclusions inappropriate. A transparent
interpretation is to use the audit to identify specific missing
tests and to use the application literature to design those tests.
The same principle applies to climate relevance. A flood
or fire map can support adaptation without demonstrating
climate-change attribution. A short-range weather forecast
is not a climate projection. An association with present-day
damage is not evidence of a mitigation benefit. Keeping these
distinctions explicit makes the review useful to researchers
and practitioners alike: it identifies what an output supports,
what additional evidence an application requires, and where

10. Conclusions
Geospatial foundation models expand the ways Earth-
observation information can be reused, but climate resilience
requires evidence about transfer under the conditions in which
decisions are made. The 137-study audit shows meaningful
use of out-of-distribution experiments alongside uneven cov-
erage of specific headline shifts. Held-out events and regions
were recorded in 31 and 25 studies, respectively; sensor
holdout appeared in two, with none among the ten foundation-
model fine-tuning studies. Uncertainty-related reporting was
also limited, and only two studies were explicitly coded for
calibrated probabilities.
These findings define an evaluation agenda, not a verdict
that pretrained models are ineffective. Earth-observation
representation learning and atmospheric forecasting offer
complementary capabilities, and their integration should be
tested through information availability, uncertainty propaga-
tion and decision timing. Across hazards, label definitions,
observability, forecast horizon and operational requirements
determine what improvement is possible and how it should
be measured.
The central recommendation is to choose the test from
the decision backward. Define the action, reproduce the
relevant shift, compare models under matched information
and adaptation budgets, and report the errors, reliability and
latency that govern practical use. Progress is demonstrated
when a model improves a decision under those conditions,
rather than when a familiar benchmark score is presented as
evidence of readiness for an unfamiliar world.
