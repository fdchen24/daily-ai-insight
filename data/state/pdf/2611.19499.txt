Do Probing and Steering Favor the Same Directions
Within and Across Language Models?

Abstract
A direction in a language model’s hidden state can be used to probe whether a
concept is present or to steer the model toward it. Although probe directions
are often reused for steering, the direction that best detects a concept need not
be the one that best changes it. We study this distinction in three instruction-
tuned models from different families. We construct a one-parameter family of
directions ranging from a covariance-adjusted discriminant to the mean difference,
and evaluate probing and additive steering, which adds the direction to the hidden
state, along the family at a fixed side effect. For truthfulness and sentiment, in
every model, the best probe is not the best steering direction: steering consistently
favors directions closer to the mean difference. This separation holds across 59 of
60 layer–model–concept combinations in a layer sweep, with matched probe and
steering texts in all six model–concept combinations, and under a rotation-based
steering operator. The exact steering optimum is less stable: for sentiment, an
intermediate direction produces a 27–56% stronger steering effect than the mean
difference, but this gain shrinks or disappears with matched texts and depends on
the layer and the operator.
The two roles also transfer differently across models. A probe transferred by a
linear map fitted on neutral text retains 81–91% of the target model’s above-chance
probing performance, while the transferred mean difference retains less of its
steering effect in 11 of 12 model pairs. This is a consistent tendency, although our
predefined criteria classify only three pairs, and nine remain inconclusive. Finally,
as an application on PopQA, steering toward “unknown” increases abstention by
18–44 percentage points on uncertain errors, versus 2–5 on confident errors, while
rarely correcting errors in cases where a correct answer is reachable.
1
Introduction
A language model’s hidden state can be used, without changing the model’s weights, to probe for a
concept or to steer the model toward it. A direction can predict a concept well because it exploits
variation correlated with the concept, without being the direction along which the model is most
effectively changed. Steering methods nevertheless often reuse a probe direction, for example,
the reading vector of representation engineering [Zou et al., 2023]. Park et al. [2024] relate the
representations used for probing and for steering through a causal inner product, which raises an
empirical question: does the direction that probes a concept best also steer it best in the ordinary
geometry of the hidden state? Earlier comparisons put a trained probe against the mean difference,
two fixed directions, and find that the mean difference can steer better [Li et al., 2023, Marks and
Tegmark, 2024] and that the two can detect equally well but intervene differently [Wu et al., 2025].

In contrast to these two-endpoint comparisons, which
show which of the two directions works better for each
operation at a chosen steering strength, we ask where be-
tween them each operation works best, at a fixed side
effect on unrelated text, so that no direction gains only by
perturbing the model more. We therefore construct a con-
tinuous family of directions that extends from a covariance-
adjusted discriminant to the mean difference, the direction
commonly used for steering, and we measure probing and
steering along the whole family at a fixed side effect, in
three model families, and carry each role across models
under its own transfer law (Figure 1).
The distinction matters most when representations are
compared across models. Recent work reports conver-
gence between independently trained models [Huh et al.,
2024], but whether two models agree on a concept may
depend on whether the concept is read or changed. A lin-
ear map between two models carries a steering direction
forward, whereas a probe has to be carried by the transpose of the reverse map (Section 2.2). Each
role can therefore be transferred under its own law, which turns representational alignment into a
functional question: do models agree more on how a concept is read than on how it is changed?
Our two main results concern the split between the roles. First, along a continuous family of directions
and at a fixed side effect, the direction that probes a concept best is not the one that steers it best. This
holds in all six combinations of model and concept at the main layer, also with matched probe and
steering texts, and, in separate analyses, at 59 of 60 combinations of layer, model and concept for
truthfulness and sentiment in a layer sweep and in the four cases where a rotation-based operator
reaches the same side effect (Appendices M, F and G). Second, between models of three families,
each role carried under its own law, probes keep most of their performance while the mean difference
keeps less of its steering effect in most pairs. A secondary result is that where steering works best,
and whether an intermediate direction beats the mean difference, depends on the concept, the layer,
the operator, and the texts.
As an application, using an error taxonomy adapted from Orgad et al. [2025], we show that steering
toward “unknown” turns uncertain errors into abstentions far more readily than confident ones, while
the probe and steering directions for this concept are less clearly separated than for truthfulness and
sentiment, and gating by the probe protects correct answers but passes our test only in Qwen.
2
Method: Probing, Steering, and Transfer
Two research questions outline this study. We ask whether probing and steering favor the same
directions, within a model and across models (RQ1), and whether this distinction helps to separate
wrong answers by whether a correct answer is reachable and to steer the model toward abstention
(RQ2). Section 2.1 defines the family of directions and how probing, steering, and their side effect
are measured on it; Section 2.2 describes how directions are carried between models; Sections 2.3
and 2.4 present the hypotheses that the experiments evaluate.
2.1
Background and Definitions
Probe score.
We treat each concept as binary and use texts labeled for its presence (y = 1) or
absence (y = 0), for example, true TruthfulQA answers and positive Yelp reviews labeled y = 1. Let
h ∈Rd, with d the hidden size, be the hidden state at the last token of the text after a fixed layer ℓ
(Section 3.1). This is the residual-stream vector at that token, the sum of the token’s input embedding
and the outputs of all attention and feed-forward blocks up to and including layer ℓ.
We define a probe as a direction w in the space of h, and its probe score as w⊤h. Appendix L shows
that reading h at the last token separates the labels better than max pooling over the tokens, the
reading used by Wu et al. [2025, Sec. 3.2], for both concepts and all three models. Below we define

Mean difference.
Let µ1 = E[h | y = 1] and µ0 = E[h | y = 0] be the mean representations of
the two classes. The mean difference ∆µ = µ1 −µ0 points from µ0 to µ1 and ignores how much
the representations vary within each class; its normalized form, ∆µ/∥∆µ∥, is the λ →∞end of the
family defined below.
Up to a scale factor, ∆µ is the steering vector of contrastive activation addition (CAA) [Rimsky et al.,
2024], so our comparisons with ∆µ are also comparisons with CAA; it is also the refusal direction
[Arditi et al., 2024], a strong AxBench baseline [Wu et al., 2025], and the target of Spherical Steering
[You et al., 2026]. Related methods use a single prompt pair [Turner et al., 2023] or a principal
component of differences [Zou et al., 2023].
Within-class covariance.
We measure how much the representations vary within each class by the
pooled within-class covariance ΣW = (1 −π) Σ0 + π Σ1, where Σ0 and Σ1 are the covariances of h
within the classes y = 0 and y = 1, and π = Pr(y = 1) is the class prior. Directions in which ΣW is
large are directions along which the examples of one class spread widely.
Fisher discriminant.
A probe’s score should separate the two classes, which a direction of large
mean separation does poorly if the representations also vary strongly along it within each class. For
the other end of the family we therefore want the direction that separates the classes best and, like ∆µ,
has a closed form. For nonsingular ΣW , we choose the Fisher discriminant wLDA ∝Σ−1
W ∆µ, which
adjusts the mean difference by the within-class covariance and maximizes the ratio of between-class
to within-class variance of the probe score w⊤h.
Under a shared-covariance Gaussian model, wLDA is the direction of the Bayes-optimal classifier, so
no other score separates the classes with a higher area under the ROC curve (Appendix B). Logistic-
regression probes share this population direction for such classes; with n < d and separable classes,
however, a trained logistic probe tends toward the maximum-margin direction as its penalty vanishes,
so our results concern the closed-form family rather than trained probes.

dimensions of h, but it is estimated from fewer examples and is therefore singular, with no inverse.

2.2
Probing and Steering Across Models
Let hA(x) ∈RdA and hB(x) ∈RdB be the hidden states of models A and B on the same input
x, defined as in Section 2.1. A direction of A cannot be used in B directly, because the two states
lie in spaces with different dimensions and bases. We therefore connect the two spaces with an
affine map hB(x) ≈MA→B hA(x) + bA→B, fitted on neutral text without concept labels, where
MA→B ∈RdB×dA and bA→B ∈RdB are estimated by ridge regression of hB on hA (Appendix C).
The reverse map, MB→A and bB→A, is fitted in the same way with the two models exchanged; it is
not the inverse of MA→B, which does not exist when dA̸ = dB.
A steering direction is a displacement of the hidden state, and the offset cancels when a displacement
is mapped, so we transfer it forward, δB = MA→B δ, and use δB/∥δB∥as the unit direction w in the
intervention T of model B, so that the strength a keeps its meaning. A probe is a linear functional,
which turns a hidden state into a score, and writing the state of A through the reverse map shows that
a probe is transferred by the transpose of the reverse map, wA→B = M ⊤
B→AwA (Appendix C). We
call these the steering law and the probe law. They are those of Chen et al. [2025, Sec. 4, App. B.2],
who train the maps jointly so that they invert each other; we fit each map separately in closed form,
and what we add is the comparison of how well each role transfers under its own law.
Because the maps never see the concept texts, a transferred direction can work in the target only to the
extent that both models encode the concept along directions that the map puts into correspondence;
the transfer thus tests what a direction does in the target, not whether the two representations are
identical. To check that the choice of law matters, we also use an orthogonal Procrustes map as a
control, for which the probe law and the steering law give the same vector (Appendix N.2, Table 16).
2.3
RQ1: Probing versus Steering
We test RQ1 with three hypotheses: H1a and H1b compare probing and steering within each model,
and H1c compares them across models. Each hypothesis names what we measure and the rule, fixed
before the experiments, by which it holds or fails (Appendix H).
Within models.
Within each model, we measure R(λ) and S(λ) on the same held-out questions
and reviews, which the probe reads as labeled texts and steering uses as prompts (Section 3.2). We
call the plateau of a curve the set of directions whose value lies within one standard error of its best
value. H1a asks whether the steering plateau lies at or beyond the probing plateau toward ∆µ; the
claim is universal only if it holds in all three models.

2.4
RQ2: Answer Reachability and Abstention
We study wrong answers through the same split between probing and steering, and separate them by
the model’s own answers to the same question. For each PopQA question (Section 3.1), with “I don’t
know” allowed, the model gives one greedy answer and five sampled answers, each judged correct,
wrong, or an abstention. C0 is the set of questions whose greedy answer is correct. C1 is the set of
questions whose greedy answer is wrong although at least one sample is correct, that is, errors for
which a correct answer is reachable under our decoding; we call them knowledge-available errors for
short. C2, the uncertain errors, and C3, the confident errors, are the sets of questions with no correct
answer among the six whose wrong greedy answer is repeated in at most two or in at least four of the
five samples; Cm holds the remaining such errors, repeated in exactly three samples, and AK and AU
the greedy abstentions with and without a correct answer among the six (Table 5 in Appendix I).
The probe of RQ2 is a member of the family of Section 2.1, fitted to separate questions with and
without a correct answer among the six and read at the last prompt token, before any answer is
generated. These labels describe whether a correct answer is reachable under the decoding we test,
not whether the model knows the fact or recognizes its own error.
We test RQ2 with three hypotheses. H2a (probing) compares the probe with an output-only baseline,
i.e., the entropy of the first answer token, Hent(q) = −P
v p(v | q) log p(v | q). It asks whether the
hidden state separates (i) knowledge-available errors from errors without a correct answer (C1 vs.
C2 ∪Cm ∪C3, and, with abstentions included, C1 ∪AK vs. C2 ∪Cm ∪C3 ∪AU) and (ii) uncertain
from confident errors (C2 vs. C3). Each contrast is judged against Hent with paired bootstrap
intervals; Appendix I gives the rules.
H2b (steering) moves every question along ∆µ toward “unknown” at κ0. (i) Abstention should rise
more on C2 than on C3, (ii) fewer than 10% of C1 answers should become correct, and (iii) the probe
end of the family should produce less abstention than ∆µ at the same K. H2b(i) holds if the bootstrap
95% interval of the difference between the abstention gains on C2 and C3 lies above zero, H2b(ii) is
judged by the point estimate, and H2b(iii) holds if the interval of the difference in abstention over all
questions between the probe end and ∆µ lies below zero.
H2c (probe, then steer) steers only the questions the probe flags; it should keep at least 80% of the
abstention gain of H2b on C2 with at most half of its over-abstention on C0, judged by point estimates
at the strength of ∆µ (Appendix J varies the gate threshold). Every direction is compared with a

Experiments
3.1
Models and Datasets
We study LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3.5-9B (the post-trained checkpoint), and Gemma-3-12B-it,
with raw prompts and no chat template. We abbreviate these as LLaMA, Qwen, and Gemma, and in
tables as L, Q, and G; figure labels omit the -Instruct and -it suffixes. For each model, we use a layer
at about 0.44 of its depth, layer 14 of 32, 14 of 32, and 21 of 48, respectively (layers counted from
0). We chose this depth in advance. A sweep over ten depths in all three models and for all three
concepts (Appendix M) supports it: averaged over the concepts, the probing AUROC at this layer is
within 0.004 of the best layer in every model, and H1a holds at 59 of the 60 combinations of layer,
model, and concept for truthfulness and sentiment, the two concepts with a steering sweep.
For truthfulness, we use TruthfulQA [Lin et al., 2022], each question followed by the answers of its
multi-true list (MC2), labeled true or false (Appendix K); for sentiment, we use 2,000 Yelp reviews
(1,000 positive and 1,000 negative) [Zhang et al., 2015]. Both datasets are split 50/25/25 into an
estimation subset and two held-out sets, on which the directions are evaluated. For RQ2, we use 4,000
PopQA questions [Mallen et al., 2023]; the concept “unknown” is used only in this experiment, with
labels taken from each model’s own answers (Section 3.4). Neutral WikiText-2 sentences [Merity
et al., 2017] measure the side effect K and serve to fit the maps between models.

3.4
Answer-Reachability Experiment
Each answer is judged by matching it against PopQA’s gold answer and its aliases (Appendix K).
The six answers give the label of “unknown”, y = 1 if none of them is correct (“no correct answer”)
and y = 0 if at least one is (“some correct answer”). Unlike the labels of TruthfulQA and Yelp, these
labels depend on the model. Because the models abstain on most questions, the positive class is
dominated by abstentions: AU makes up 82%, 92% and 68% of it in LLaMA, Qwen and Gemma,
wrong answers the rest, so ∆µ mainly separates questions the model will abstain on from questions
it will answer correctly; a direction estimated from wrong answers alone was not run. The probe
is cross-fitted over two folds of the questions, with λ chosen inside each training fold. For every
contrast of H2a, we compare it with Hent and with a combination of both (Appendix I).
For steering, the directions of each fold are computed from the other fold; no question is steered with a
direction that has seen it. We compare five directions: ∆µ (mean of “no correct answer” minus mean
of “some correct answer”), the probe end wλ∗, the same ∆µ applied only to the questions the probe
flags (gated), a random unit direction, and the ∆µ of each other model carried over by the steering
law. Each is applied from the last prompt token onward at four strengths, a ∈{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4}
(ten times smaller for Gemma), and the greedy answer (at most 24 new tokens) is judged again.
We report each change in percentage points (pp), the percentage of the questions in a case that end
with a given outcome (an abstention, or a correct answer) after steering minus the same percentage in
an unsteered rerun. The budget κ0 is the K that the “unknown” ∆µ produces at a = 0.1 (0.01 for
Gemma); Appendix I gives its values and how each direction is read at it. Intervals come from a
bootstrap over questions within each case (2,000 resamples). We report 95% intervals throughout and
do not correct for multiple comparisons, except in the pooling ablation (Appendix L), which uses
Holm’s method.

Related Work
Probing and steering concepts.
A unified analysis of steering methods favors the mean of differ-
ences [Im and Li, 2025], and ridge-regularized probes have been used to obtain concept directions
for steering [Gao et al., 2026]. Closest to our setting, Cheng and Amo Alonso [2026] and Hedström
et al. [2025] steer along a probe’s own direction with a strength set per token in closed form, in
several model families; the first notes that linear decodability does not guarantee linear controllability,
which agrees with our finding that the best probe is not the best steering direction. These works vary
the strength along one direction; none of them, nor the comparisons cited in Section 1, varies the
9
direction itself along the family between the probe and the mean difference and measures probing and
steering at each point of it at a fixed side effect. Concept erasure removes several directions [Ravfogel
et al., 2020] or, in closed form, the subspace that linear classifiers use [Belrose et al., 2023]; we study
single directions.
Representation transfer.
Learned maps carry objects from one model to another: affine stitches
transfer sparse autoencoders, sparse probes and steering vectors between sizes of one family [Chen
et al., 2025], affine maps and autoencoders transfer steering vectors between sizes and across families
[Bello et al., 2025, Oozeer et al., 2025], and an affine map on final hidden states lets one model’s
states be decoded through another’s output head [Gorbett and Jana, 2026]. These works move one
kind of object at a time, mostly steering vectors, and judge it by the behavior it produces in the target;
where probes and steering vectors are both moved [Chen et al., 2025], the two are tested on different
pairs of models, so their transfer cannot be compared. We instead move the same family of directions
in both roles between the same three model families, in all six ordered pairs, each role under its
own law and both measured against the target’s own direction at a fixed side effect, with the wrong
law and an orthogonal map as controls; this shows that probes transfer more reliably than steering
directions and that each role transfers well only under its own law.
Knowledge awareness.
Whether a model knows an answer can be predicted from its own judgment
[Kadavath et al., 2022] or its internal states [Gottesman and Geva, 2024], and errors made despite
knowledge can be distinguished from errors caused by missing knowledge [Simhi et al., 2024, Orgad
et al., 2025]. A probe on the hidden state before generation can also stand in for sampling-based
semantic uncertainty [Han et al., 2024]. Other studies examine confident errors and association-
driven hallucinations [Simhi et al., 2025, Cheang et al., 2026], steer knowledge awareness or gate the
intervention [Ferrando et al., 2025, Kumaran et al., 2026, Hedström et al., 2025, Lee et al., 2025], or
train steering toward abstention into the weights of one layer [Yang et al., 2026]. We instead study
what steering toward “unknown” does to each type of error, and how its probe and steering direction
differ and transfer. As an application, this steering reduces hallucinations by turning wrong answers
into abstentions, mostly the uncertain ones, at a small cost in correct answers.
6
Conclusion
In each model we test, the direction of the family that probes a concept best does not steer it
best: probing peaks near λ = τ and steering further toward the mean difference, in all six main
combinations, also with matched texts, at 59 of 60 combinations for truthfulness and sentiment in a
layer sweep, and in the four cases where rotation reaches the same side effect. The two roles also
separate across models. Each carried under its own law, probes keep most of their performance, for
“unknown” as well, while the mean difference keeps less of its steering effect in most pairs; under
maps fitted on neutral text, the tested models agree more on how a concept is read than on how it is
changed, although part of the steering loss may come from the maps. Where steering works best is
less stable: whether an intermediate direction beats the mean difference depends on the concept, the
layer, the operator, and the texts (Appendices F, G and M). On PopQA, steering toward “unknown”
turns uncertain errors into abstentions far more readily than confident ones; the probe for “unknown”
steers almost as strongly as the mean difference in Qwen and Gemma, and gating by the probe
protects correct answers but passes our test only in Qwen.
These results come from three concepts and three models, with the main analyses at one layer per
model; the twelve ordered pairs of the cross-model experiments share models and are therefore
not independent, and all results concern single directions under additive and rotational steering.
Appendix A lists the further limitations. Probe directions should therefore not be reused for steering
without checking how well they steer.

A
Limitations
Scope of the evidence.
All results come from three concepts (truthfulness, sentiment, and “un-
known” on PopQA) and three instruction-tuned models (LLaMA-3.1-8B, Qwen3.5-9B, Gemma-3-
12B), and the main analyses use one layer per model. The layer sweep of Appendix M covers the
probing and steering of RQ1 only; the steering of RQ2, including the H2b(iii) and H2c verdicts,
was run at the paper’s layer alone, so we do not know how those verdicts change with depth. Other
concepts, base models, or larger models may behave differently.
The cross-model pairs are not independent.
Three models give six ordered pairs, and two
concepts give twelve. Every pair shares its source or its target with four other pairs, so a property of
one model appears in four of the twelve points at once; for example, the poor probing of Gemma’s
own mean difference affects every pair into or out of Gemma. Counts such as “11 of 12 pairs”
therefore describe a pattern in three models and should not be read as twelve separate confirmations.
The cross-model comparison also uses only the unmatched probe and steering texts of Section 3.2;
the matched-text control of Appendix F was run within models only.
Single directions and two operators.
We study one direction at a time, drawn from a single
one-parameter family, and two ways of applying it: addition and a gated rotation. We do not study
subspaces, nonlinear probes, sparse-autoencoder features, or steering operators learned from data, and
the conclusions may not carry over to them. The rotation operator is gated by the state’s cosine with
the direction, and its side-effect budget is set on neutral text where the gate opens less often than on
task text; its H1b verdicts therefore mix the effect of the rotation with that of the gate (Appendix G).
What is measured.
Steering is measured by the change of an answer margin (truthfulness, “un-
known”) or of word log-odds (sentiment), not by task accuracy, so a direction that moves the margin
need not change which answer the model gives. Probing is measured by AUROC. The two transfer ra-
tios ρprobe and ρsteer therefore compare different quantities, an AUROC above chance and a steering
effect, and their comparison is approximate. The side effect K is the next-token KL divergence on
200 neutral WikiText-2 sentences, not on the task prompts or over generated continuations, and the
budget κ0 is anchored to the mean difference at one strength; the post hoc check over a sixteen-fold
range of budgets (Appendix D) reads saved curves and has no intervals.
Maps and estimates.
The maps between models are fitted on neutral text and fit the concept texts
less well (R2 of 0.13–0.47 against 0.48–0.70 on held-out neutral text), so part of the loss of a carried
steering direction may come from the map rather than from the models, and no pair can be called
“neither shared”. ΣW is estimated once per model and concept from fewer examples than dimensions,
so the estimate is singular and the small-λ end of the family is the part of ∆µ outside the span of the
training states rather than the Fisher discriminant (Appendix B).
RQ2 labels.
The cases of RQ2 are defined from the greedy answer and five sampled answers, so
they capture whether a correct answer is reachable under this sampling, not whether the model knows
the fact; a question whose correct answer appears with probability p in one sample is missed by all
five with probability (1 −p)5, which biases the “no correct answer” class toward questions the model
could sometimes answer. Knowledge-available errors (C1) are rare, 71, 33, and 35 questions, so
the H2b(ii) verdicts rest on point estimates with wide intervals. Prompts are plain text without the
models’ chat templates, and Qwen’s thinking tokens are handled by a token budget (Appendix I).
Truthfulness data.
Truthfulness is tested on TruthfulQA alone, and its directions are computed
and evaluated on the same dataset, so a gain of an intermediate direction cannot be separated from a
gain of fitting the test distribution. Truthfulness probes trained on one dataset can generalize poorly
to others [Liu et al., 2024], and we did not test whether the directions of this paper transfer to other
truthfulness data.

B
The two ends of the family as divergence witnesses
Under a shared-covariance Gaussian model, the score of wLDA is a monotone function of the

Steering by rotation
Spherical Steering [You et al., 2026] rotates the hidden state toward a direction instead of adding
the direction to it. We repeat the RQ1 measurements with a gated rotation in this style: h is rotated
toward w along the great circle by gθ degrees, at most by the angle between them and with its norm
kept, where g = min{1, max{0, (c −β)/(1 −β)}} and c = −tanh(κ cos(h, w)). This c equals
their vMF gate score, the probability of alignment with −w minus that with w under a two-class
softmax; we fix β = −0.15 and κ = 20. Unlike their method, which rotates by a fraction of the
angle to w, we rotate by the angle gθ, so that the side effect can be matched to the additive budget κ0.
The angles θ run from 1 to 40 degrees (0.1 to 4 for Gemma), and every effect is read at the additive
budget κ0 of Section 3.2, so the two operators are compared at the same side effect.

RQ2 protocol
The 4,000 PopQA questions, the few-shot prompt (which allows “I don’t know”), and the answer
judge are described in Appendix K. Each model produces a greedy answer a0 and five samples
(temperature 0.7, top-p = 0.95, top-k as in each model’s generation configuration: none for LLaMA
and Qwen, 64 for Gemma; repetition penalty 1.0).
Each answer is judged correct, abstaining, or wrong using alias matching. Let r(q) count the non-
abstaining samples that repeat a0. We use “knowledge-available” operationally: at least one of the
six answers is correct. This shows that the model can reach the answer under some decoding, not that
it explicitly knows the fact; conversely, “no correct answer” means only that none of the six tested
answers was correct.
The probe measures whether a correct answer is reachable, not whether the model recognizes its
own answer as false. The cases follow the error taxonomy of Orgad et al. [2025], who resample 30
answers per question and separate, among others, consistently incorrect answers, answers spread
over many alternatives, and errors among whose resampled answers the correct one also appears;
our C3, C2 and C1 roughly correspond to these three types, defined here from six answers with
abstention allowed. Six answers are far fewer than their thirty, and the labels inherit a sampling bias:
a question whose correct answer has probability p under sampling is missed by all five samples with
probability (1 −p)5, 0.77 at p = 0.05 and 0.44 at p = 0.15, and is then counted among the questions
without a correct answer rather than in C1. C1 is therefore a conservative set, and C2 contains some
questions that more samples would have moved to C1; C0 is defined by the greedy answer alone
and is unaffected. This is why C1 is small (71, 33 and 35 questions) and its intervals wide, and why
the 10% limit of H2b(ii) rests on 7, 3 and 3 questions; the C2-versus-C3 contrasts, with 180–576
questions per class, are not affected in the same way.
They probe the generated answer tokens to predict the error type and to select a correct answer among
the samples. We instead probe before any answer is generated, compare the probe with an output-only
baseline, and ask what steering does to each type of error. In our data, the entropy of the first answer
token separates C2 from C3 better than the probe (Figure 11).
The two parts of H2a ask whether the representation tracks whether the answer is reachable at all and,
when it is not, whether it tracks the model’s commitment to its wrong answer. H2b(ii) expects few
corrections because steering toward “unknown” should produce abstention, not retrieval, and H2b(iii)
tests the split between probing and steering on “unknown” itself.
All changes are measured against an unsteered rerun, which agrees with the labelling run on 99.3–
100% of the questions. The budget κ0 is 0.0012, 0.0018 and 0.0018 nats for LLaMA, Qwen and
Gemma. We use ∆µ at its anchor strength (K = κ0), and the probe end, the random direction, and
the transferred ∆µ interpolated per question to K = κ0, linearly in

The gate of H2c flags a question when its probe score exceeds the 90th percentile of the C0 scores on
an inner split of the training fold. To see how the verdicts depend on this choice, we recompute post
hoc the gated outcomes for thresholds between the 75th and the 97.5th percentile of the C0 scores
on the whole training fold; the original threshold lies at its 91st to 96th percentile. No new answers
are generated. The gated run equals, question by question, the ∆µ-steered answer where a question
is flagged and the unsteered answer elsewhere (agreement 99.7–100%), so another threshold only
changes which of the two saved answers is used.

Datasets and prompts
All datasets are public (Table 9): TruthfulQA [Lin et al., 2022], Yelp polarity [Zhang et al., 2015],
WikiText-2 [Merity et al., 2017], and PopQA [Mallen et al., 2023]. Every model receives the same
texts, so examples line up across models although the tokenizers differ; activations are taken at the
last token of each text. All splits and samples use seed 0.
TruthfulQA.
TruthfulQA consists of 817 questions; the multiple-choice lists of its release contain
5,882 reference answers (2,585 true, 3,297 false). The dataset release provides two multiple-choice
lists per question. The multi-true list (MC2) contains all reference answers of the question, of which
one or more are true; it corresponds to the multiple-choice task of Lin et al. [2022, Sec. 3.2], while
the names MC1 and MC2 and the answer counts come from the dataset release. The single-true list
(MC1) contains the same false answers but only one true answer (4,114 answers, 2–13 per question).
For MC1, a model selects the answer to which it assigns the highest likelihood, and the score is the
share of questions for which this answer is the true one. For MC2, the score is the total probability
the model assigns to the true answers, normalized over all answers in the list. We use the MC2
answers as labeled probing examples and the MC1 list for the steering margin; we do not report

Reading position: last token versus pooling
A probe is obtained in two steps. First, estimation: from the hidden states of the training texts, ∆µ
and ΣW are computed and from them the direction w (Sections 2.1 and 3.2); this is done once,
and the result is a fixed vector. Second, scoring: for a held-out text, some of its hidden states are
multiplied by w and the products are combined into one number, the probe score, from which the
AUROC is computed. In all our main experiments both steps use the hidden state at the last token
of the text. AxBench [Wu et al., 2025, Sec. 3.2] instead scores every token of a text and keeps the
maximum. We compare three ways of turning a text into one probe score.
The first is the one used in all our main experiments: the direction w is estimated from the hidden
states at the last token of the texts, and a held-out text is scored by w⊤hT at its last token. The second
is max pooling, the scoring used by AxBench [Wu et al., 2025, Sec. 3.2]: every token of the text is
scored, w⊤ht for t = 1, . . . , T, and the largest score is kept;
In the second variant, the vector w is computed once from last-token states, as in the first variant
(ours), so the direction is the same in both variants. It is then, however, multiplied with the hidden
state of every token and the largest product is kept, so the same text can receive a different score
in the two variants. The hidden states at the intermediate tokens have their own class means and
within-class spread, and a direction estimated from them would differ from the one estimated at the
last token; no variant estimates ∆µ and ΣW from all token positions.
The third is mean pooling: the hidden states of the text are averaged into ¯h, ∆µ and ΣW are re-
estimated from these averaged states, and a held-out text is scored by w⊤¯h; here both the estimation
and the scoring change. In the second variant the direction estimated at the last token is applied to
every token, whose class means and within-class covariance can differ from those at the last token;
no variant estimates ∆µ and ΣW from all token positions.
Only the tokens that carry the concept are pooled: the answer for TruthfulQA and the whole review
for sentiment; special tokens are excluded. Each variant chooses its best direction of the family on
the first held-out split, and the choices are compared on the second split with a paired bootstrap that
resamples questions or reviews (1,000 resamples). Significance is corrected with Holm’s method

over all 18 comparisons we ran, including a third variant, directions computed from mean states but
scored by max pooling, which is omitted here and was never better than the last token.
Table 10 gives the held-out AUROC of the last-token reading on the second held-out split and, for

The loss under max pooling therefore shows that a direction fitted at the last token does not transfer
to scoring every token, not that token-level estimation would do worse; directions estimated across

Layer choice basis
The paper reads and steers every model at about 0.44 of its depth (Section 3.1). This appendix gives
the basis for that choice: how well each concept can be probed, and how strongly ∆µ steers it, at
other depths. To check this, we repeat the RQ1 measurements at ten relative depths, from 1/8 to 7/8
of the layers: layers 4, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 24 and 28 of 32 in LLaMA and Qwen, and 6, 12, 15,
18, 21, 24, 27, 30, 36 and 42 of 48 in Gemma. The data, splits, and steering prompts are those of
Section 3.2, and the probe and the steering intervention always use the same layer.
To keep the computing time of the sweep manageable, with ten depths for each model and concept
and six steering strengths per direction, the family has six members instead of fourteen, λ ∈
{10−3, 0.1, 1, 10, 100} τ and ∆µ. Values at the paper’s layer can therefore differ slightly from those
of Section 4.1 when the main run’s best direction lies at a grid point the sweep lacks: the best AUROC
does so only for sentiment in LLaMA (0.980 against 0.981) and Qwen (0.964 against 0.966), whose
best probe sits at 3τ, and the H1b ratios, whose λ∗is chosen on the coarser grid, by up to 0.08
(truthfulness in Gemma, 0.99 against 1.07).
Each layer has its own budget κ0, the K of ∆µ at a = 0.1 (0.01 for Gemma) at that layer. For RQ2,
we sweep probing only. The PopQA labels do not depend on the layer, so we read the prompts of all
4,000 questions at the ten layers and compute the cross-fitted probe of Section 3.4 at each of them,
with 95% bootstrap intervals (200 resamples). Steering toward “unknown” was not swept.
Table 11 gives, for each relative depth, the layer in LLaMA and Qwen and in Gemma and the held-out
AUROC of the best direction of the family, on all 4,000 questions for PopQA. From 3/8 of the depth
upward, the AUROC of each model and concept varies by at most 0.04 between layers. Averaged
over truthfulness, sentiment, and PopQA, the best layer is the paper’s layer 14 in LLaMA, layer 18
in Qwen, and layer 24 in Gemma; the layers we use come within 0.004 AUROC of that average
in Qwen (0.957 against 0.960) and within 0.002 in Gemma (0.960 against 0.962). On PopQA, the
AUROC stays within 0.01 of its maximum from 3/8 of the depth upward in LLaMA and Qwen, and
within 0.03 in Gemma, whose layer 21 lies 0.004 below its best layer, 24.
In all three models and for all three concepts, the 95% interval of the AUROC at the paper’s layer

For truthfulness and sentiment, the sweep stored no per-example scores, so these intervals use the

29
Three caveats temper this reading. First, ρprobe and ρsteer are ratios of different quantities, an AUROC
above chance and a steering effect, so comparing them is only approximate. In particular, AUROC
saturates: under the Gaussian model of Appendix B, AUROC = Φ(d′/
√
2) with d′ the standardized
separation, and at a target AUROC of 0.96–0.98 a probe that loses a third of its d′ still keeps about
85% of the AUROC above chance. Recomputed on the d′ scale from the same held-out AUROCs,
ρprobe falls to 0.65–0.79 and exceeds ρsteer in 9 of 12 pairs instead of 11: all six truthfulness pairs
hold, while for sentiment LLaMA→Qwen (0.76 against 0.93), LLaMA→Gemma (0.66 against 0.72)
and Gemma→Qwen (0.79 against 0.81) no longer do. For “unknown”, whose target probes lie further
from the ceiling (AUROC 0.92–0.94), the d′ ratio is 0.82–0.92 against a steering ratio of 0.30–0.79,
so all six pairs hold on either scale.
Second, the maps are fitted on neutral text and fit the concept texts less well (R2 of 0.13–0.47, against
0.48–0.70 on held-out neutral text; Table 16). Part of the loss in steering may therefore come from
the map rather than from the models, and for the same reason no pair can be called “neither shared”.
Third, ρprobe and ρsteer measure what a carried direction does in the target, how well it reads the
concept and how strongly it pushes the model, not how close it lies to the target’s own direction;
a carried direction can do the job while pointing away from the target’s own ∆µ. The measure is
therefore functional rather than geometric. In 10 of 12 pairs, the mapped ∆µ is closer to the target’s
own ∆µ than a mean difference computed with permuted labels (cosine 0.53–0.81, against a 95th
null percentile of 0.12–0.66). In the two sentiment pairs into Gemma, however, it is no closer to
Gemma’s own ∆µ than the permuted-label null (cosine −0.17 and −0.02) and still steers at 0.72 and
0.70 of its effect.
The disagreement is confined to the mean-difference end. Along the family, the carried source
direction MA→GwA,λ and Gemma’s own wG,λ have a positive cosine that rises from 0.20 at the
probe end to 0.50 at 32τ (from LLaMA) and 0.64 at 100τ (from Qwen), and turns negative only at
∆µ.

The two laws carry different things (Figure 10). The left panel plots the probing AUROC in the target
after the probe law against the source’s own ∆µ AUROC, and the middle panel plots the AUROC
after the steering law against the target’s own ∆µ AUROC; the right panel plots the steering effect at
the target’s κ0, relative to its own ∆µ, after the probe law (horizontal) and the steering law (vertical),
with 95% intervals. Dashed lines mark equality, and r is the Pearson correlation over the twelve pairs.
After the probe law, how well ∆µA probes in the target follows how well it probed in the source
(r = 0.98 across the twelve pairs): the probe law keeps what the direction reads. After the steering
law, it follows how well the target’s own ∆µ probes (r = 0.95): the steering law keeps where the
direction pushes, and in the new model that push looks like the target’s own mean difference. For
steering, the steering law wins in all twelve pairs. Gemma makes the difference visible. Its ∆µ
probes poorly in Gemma and, using the probe law, also in LLaMA and Qwen, where it does not steer
at all; using the steering law, it steers 0.62–0.81 as strongly as their own ∆µ. Both correlations are
driven largely by the contrast between Gemma and the other two models, whose AUROCs are similar.
A probe and a steering direction are different objects, and they have to be mapped differently.
