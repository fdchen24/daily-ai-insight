ABSTRACT
Latent generative models typically rely on three separate networks: an encoder
that tokenizes pixels, a generator that models the latent distribution, and a de-
coder that maps latents back to pixels. UNITE recently merged the first two into
a single generative encoder trained in one stage, but its decoder remains a sepa-
rate network. We ask whether this last network can be folded in too, so that one
diffusion transformer encodes, generates, and decodes. Simply letting the shared
backbone decode does not help. What makes it work is to cast all three modes
as position-aligned sequence maps over one fully shared backbone, distinguished
only by a mode token and the timestep. The resulting model, UNIFIED, is sim-
ple: one network trained from scratch in a single stage with only reconstruction
and flow-matching losses, without adversarial losses or pretrained encoders (e.g.,
DINO). On ImageNet-1K 256 × 256 at matched parameters, UNIFIED improves
gFID over UNITE at three sizes (∼60M, ∼115M, and ∼215M) and reaches 2.08
with 215M parameters, the lowest among compared models under 250M parame-
ters. On text-to-image generation, UNIFIED-B (218M) reaches a GenEval score
of 0.87 at 512 × 512, exceeding single-stage pixel diffusion models with around
one billion parameters. Full unification of latent diffusion is thus not merely fea-
sible but beneficial.

INTRODUCTION
Latent diffusion models (Rombach et al., 2022; Peebles & Xie, 2023; Ma et al., 2024) rely on
three trainable networks: an encoder that maps pixels to latents, a generator that models the latent
distribution, and a decoder that maps latents back to pixels. They are usually trained in stages:
the autoencoder is first trained for reconstruction, and then frozen while the generator is fit to its
latents. This recipe fixes the representation before the model that must sample from it is trained. It
also forces a choice of how much capacity each network receives, and the effect of that choice on
generation is hard to predict (Hansen-Estruch et al., 2025).
We ask whether this pipeline can collapse into a single diffusion transformer (DiT) whose every
block is shared by encoding, denoising, and decoding, trained in a single stage with only a re-
construction loss and a flow-matching loss, with no pretrained encoders such as DINO (Oquab
et al., 2024). Most single-stage training methods keep the tokenizer and generator as separate net-
works (Vahdat et al., 2021; Leng et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026b; Heek et al.,
2026), while the few that use a single network still reserve parts of it for individual modes: LDM-
is-AE (Zhang et al., 2026) splits one DiT by depth into encoding and decoding blocks, which turns
the allocation choice above into a choice of split depth, and DSD (Wang & Zhang, 2025) adds a
dedicated diffusion head and relies on DINO alignment.
UNITE (Duggal et al., 2026) is the closest step in this direction. It treats tokenization and generation
as the same latent-inference problem and solves both with one generative encoder, trained in a single
stage with only reconstruction and flow-matching losses, but it still decodes pixels with a separate
network (Figure 2b). A natural baseline is to let UNITE’s backbone decode as well. We call this
extension UNITE++ (Figure 2c): it shares every weight across the three modes and selects the
mode with a learned mode token. Like UNITE, it reads its outputs from readout tokens, which are
extra input tokens whose outputs are taken as the latent or as pixels. UNITE++ does not improve
upon UNITE: at ∼215M parameters it reaches a gFID of 2.24 vs. UNITE’s 2.23 and reconstructs

We therefore propose UNIFIED (Figure 2d), a single DiT that maps images to latents, latents to
latents, and latents to images as position-aligned sequence maps: a given sequence index refers to
the same image location whether it holds a pixel patch, a latent, or a noisy latent. The backbone is
fully shared, and each mode is selected by a learned mode token and lightweight input and output
heads. Relative to UNITE, UNIFIED changes only the sequence layout, the timesteps for encoding
and decoding, and the mode tokens, and replaces the standalone decoder with a lightweight pixel
output head. The training recipe is unchanged.
The remaining design choice is the timestep that conditions encoding and decoding. Both are noise-
less maps, so the natural choice is the clean endpoint (t = 1). This improves gFID over UNITE
(2.15 vs. 2.23 at ∼215M). At t = 1, however, three modes disagree on what to output: encoding
and generation both produce the clean latent, whereas decoding produces pixels. Sharing t = 1 thus
forces the last block to serve two output spaces, and the conflict is sharpest near the clean endpoint at
the end of the layers (Section 4.3). We therefore give decoding its own learned timestep embedding,
denoted tdec = −1 (a label outside [0, 1]), which leaves t = 1 to the two modes whose target is the
clean latent. This further lowers gFID to 2.08 and restores the alignment between the two passes
near the clean endpoint (Figure 3).
On ImageNet-1K 256×256 at matched parameter counts, UNIFIED improves gFID over UNITE at
all three sizes (∼60M, ∼115M, and ∼215M). Tiny UNIFIED nearly matches Small UNITE on gFID
with about half the parameters, suggesting that a standalone decoder uses capacity less efficiently
than the shared backbone. Against published models, UNIFIED-B reaches a gFID of 2.08 with
215M parameters, the lowest among compared models with at most 250M parameters, without a
pretrained autoencoder or vision foundation model (Section 4.2). On text-to-image generation at
256 × 256, UNIFIED-L (554M) outperforms both the pixel-space JiT (Li & He, 2026) (615M)
and DiTDH-XL (Zheng et al., 2026a) with a pretrained FLUX-VAE (959M) on GenEval, DPG-
Bench, and GenAI-Bench. At 512 × 512, UNIFIED-B (218M) reaches 0.87 on GenEval and 0.827
on DPG-Bench, exceeding single-stage pixel diffusion models with around one billion parameters
(Section 4.5).

Our contributions are:
• We propose UNIFIED, a single DiT that encodes, generates, and decodes with every back-
bone weight shared, trained from scratch in a single stage with only reconstruction and
flow-matching losses, without adversarial losses or pretrained encoders (e.g., DINO).
• We analyze how shared weights keep the three modes apart. We show that position align-
ment restores the reconstruction lost by naive full sharing, that the encoding timestep must
match what the encoder reads, and that a learned decoding timestep removes the conflict
between decoding and generation at the clean endpoint, leading to better performance.
• At matched parameter counts on ImageNet, UNIFIED improves over UNITE at three pa-
rameter scales and attains the lowest gFID among compared models with at most 250M
parameters. It also extends to text-to-image generation: at 512 × 512, UNIFIED-B (218M)
exceeds single-stage pixel diffusion models with around one billion parameters.

RELATED WORK
Autoencoders and latent diffusion. Latent generative models typically follow a two-stage recipe:
an autoencoder is trained for reconstruction and frozen, and a separate generator is then fit to its
latents. The tokenizer is usually a KL-regularized VAE (Kingma & Welling, 2014; Rezende et al.,
2014) for latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022; Podell et al., 2024) or a vector-quantized autoen-
coder (van den Oord et al., 2017; Razavi et al., 2019) for discrete generation, in both cases trained
with the perceptual (Zhang et al., 2018) and adversarial losses popularized by VQGAN (Esser et al.,
2021). Because the two stages are decoupled, progress has largely come one network at a time:
generators have moved to transformer backbones (Peebles & Xie, 2023; Zheng et al., 2024; Gao
et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026b) and flow-based objectives (Lipman et al., 2023;
Liu et al., 2023), while tokenizers have improved latent quantization (Mentzer et al., 2024; Yu et al.,
2024a) and compactness (Yu et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2025b). This decoupling has a cost, however.
The two stages optimize different objectives, which creates an optimization dilemma: a latent that
favors reconstruction can degrade generation. VA-VAE (Yao et al., 2025) addresses this dilemma by
aligning the VAE latent with a pretrained vision encoder (Caron et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2024).
Such encoders also serve as an alignment target for the generator (Yu et al., 2025), or even replace
the tokenizer’s encoder in the latent diffusion pipeline (Zheng et al., 2026a; Shi et al., 2026). We
instead revisit the recipe itself: a single backbone serves as encoder, decoder, and generator, and is
trained in a single stage without relying on a pretrained vision encoder.
End-to-end tokenization and generation. Closer to our setting, several methods train the tokenizer
and generator jointly (Leng et al., 2025; Duggal et al., 2026). The central difficulty is latent collapse:
once the generation loss can shape the latent, the generator can lower its loss by making the latent
trivial rather than modeling it (Leng et al., 2025; Wang & Zhang, 2025; Zheng et al., 2026b). Exist-
ing methods differ mainly in how they prevent this. Likelihood-based approaches keep a variational
bound whose KL or entropy terms hold the latent in place, and train a score-based (Vahdat et al.,
2021), normalizing-flow (Zhao et al., 2026), or diffusion (Zheng et al., 2026b; Heek et al., 2026)
prior jointly with the autoencoder. REPA-E (Leng et al., 2025) instead anchors the latent to an exter-
nal representation, backpropagating a representation-alignment loss, rather than the diffusion loss,
into the VAE. Other approaches stop the gradient through the denoising target and predict clean la-
tents (Duggal et al., 2026; Wang & Zhang, 2025; Zhang et al., 2026); this needs neither a variational
bound nor an external encoder, and we adopt it. Closest to our work, three methods built on this
recipe also share weights. UNITE (Duggal et al., 2026) merges the encoder and generator but keeps
a standalone decoder; LDM-is-AE (Zhang et al., 2026) splits one DiT by depth into encoding and
decoding blocks, which requires manually choosing the split depth and the feature-mixing schedule;
and DSD (Wang & Zhang, 2025) uses a single ViT for all three modes with lightweight blocks for
diffusion, but reintroduces an external alignment loss. UNIFIED instead shares every block of a
single DiT backbone across all three roles, with no role-specific blocks or alignment loss.

adjusted noise schedules, and loss weightings (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Hoogeboom et al., 2023;
Kingma & Gao, 2023; Hoogeboom et al., 2025), adaptive computation over a small set of latent
tokens (Jabri et al., 2023), or cascades across resolutions (Chen et al., 2025c). JiT (Li & He, 2026;

One transformer, many tasks. A single transformer can serve many tasks when the task is specified
through its input. The most common way is the input format: a text prefix or prompt (Raffel et al.,
2020; Chen et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2023; 2024a), the choice of input and target modalities (Mizrahi
et al., 2023), in-context example pairs (Wang et al., 2023), or a multimodal instruction (Xiao et al.,
2025). Unified multimodal models (Chameleon Team, 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025;
Wu et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025) extend this to understanding and generation in one backbone.
Another way is the corruption level of each input, set by per-modality or per-token timesteps (Bao
et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Le et al., 2025) or by masking ratios (Li et al., 2023; Hansen-Estruch
et al., 2024): clean inputs act as conditions and heavily corrupted ones are generated, so one network
can switch among conditional, joint, and marginal generation, or between representation learning
and generation. In this paper, we use a learned mode token and a mode-specific timestep to direct a
single DiT backbone between tokenization and generation.

METHOD
UNIFIED replaces the encoder, generator, and decoder of latent diffusion with a single diffusion
transformer Tθ that operates in three modes: encoding, generation, and decoding. Each training step
runs one pass per mode, and every pass is a position-aligned sequence map, in which index i refers
to the same image location whether it holds a pixel patch, a latent token, or a noised latent token.
The backbone weights are fully shared; the modes differ only in lightweight input and output heads,
in a learned mode token prepended to the sequence, and in the AdaLN conditioning, namely the
timestep and a class label that is null outside generation (Table 1).

Latent diffusion. The standard pipeline (Figure 2a) trains three networks: an encoder Eθ that maps
an image to its latent, a generator Gψ that recovers the clean latent from a noised one, and a decoder
Dϕ that maps latents back to pixels (Rombach et al., 2022; Peebles & Xie, 2023). The encoder and
decoder are trained first under a reconstruction objective and then frozen, so generative gradients
cannot shape the latent space that the generator must later model.
UNITE. UNITE (Duggal et al., 2026) observes that tokenization and generation are the same latent-
inference problem under different conditioning: tokenization infers the latent from a fully observed
image, and generation infers it from a noised latent. It therefore implements the encoding and
generation modes with one network, the generative encoder, trained in a single stage (Figure 2b).
Its encoding pass reads the image patches alongside K tokens drawn from N(0, I) and returns
their outputs as the latent, and its generation pass places zt at the same positions. UNITE calls these
tokens registers; we call them latent readouts, since unlike the registers of Darcet et al. (2024), whose
outputs are discarded, their outputs are the latent. We apply a stop-gradient to the encoder output z
in the generation loss, so the loss treats the latent as a constant. The same detached latent serves as
the clean target and, once noised, as the generator’s input. Because no gradient from the generation
loss reaches z, this loss cannot push the latent toward a trivial solution. The reconstruction and
generation losses still jointly update the shared weights. Writing each pipeline in terms of the three
functions it learns, gives

A naive full-sharing baseline: UNITE++. UNITE reduces three networks to two, but the decoder
Dϕ remains a standalone network. The interface in Equation 4 does not by itself determine how
the three passes are laid out. Its most direct realization keeps UNITE’s readout layout and lets the
generative encoder decode as well; we call this baseline UNITE++ (Figure 2c). Its encoding pass
is UNITE’s, reading the image patches alongside K latent readouts at tenc = 0; its decoding pass
reads the latent alongside N pixel readouts at tdec = 1 and returns pixels from them; and a learned
mode token selects the mode (Table 1). UNITE++ thus shares every backbone weight, the mode
tokens, and the training objective with UNIFIED, and differs only in the sequence layout and the
encoding and decoding timesteps. Section 3.3 motivates each of these two choices, and Section 4.3
isolates their effects.

3.3
SPECIFYING MODES IN SHARED WEIGHTS
With every backbone weight shared, the three passes differ only in what enters the backbone: the
values supplied by the input heads, the layout of the sequence, the mode token, and the conditioning
(t, c). The class label is null in both encoding and decoding, so the layout, the mode token, and the
timestep must keep the modes apart. We set each by a single principle.
Position alignment. In UNITE and UNITE++, the latent occupies its own group of readout tokens
(Table 1), so pixels and latents interact only through attention: encoding must route the content of
every image location into latent readouts with no fixed spatial correspondence, and in UNITE++
decoding must route it back out to the pixel readouts. The backbone thus learns this routing on top
of encoding and decoding themselves. UNIFIED instead aligns the sequences of all three modes:
the pixel patch, latent token, and noised latent token share one sequence index and one RoPE posi-
tion. This leaves attention to model context rather than to move content between pixels and latents.
Alignment comes at a cost. Readouts decouple the latent length K from the number of patches, as
in Perceiver-style readouts (Jaegle et al., 2022), while alignment ties K = N. In exchange, each
encoding or decoding pass has length N + 1 instead of 1 + K + N.
Encoding timestep. The timestep is the backbone’s explicit signal of how much of the content at
its output positions is observed: t = 0 marks pure noise and t = 1 a clean latent. We set it in every
mode to match that content. The generation pass reads zt and uses the sampled t. For the encoding
pass, what the output positions hold depends on the layout. In UNITE and UNITE++, they hold
N(0, I) latent readouts, the same input from which sampling starts, so the matched choice is the
noisy endpoint, tenc = 0: encoding is generation from noise, conditioned on the image through
attention. Under position alignment, the same positions hold the observed image patches, so the
matched choice becomes the clean endpoint, tenc = 1.
Decoding timestep. Decoding reads a clean latent, so the principle above would set tdec = 1. At
the clean endpoint, however, the modes disagree on what to output: encoding and generation return
the clean latent, whereas decoding returns pixels. The conflict is sharpest at the end of sampling.
There the generation input zt ≈z enters through the same head P in
z as the decoding input, and in
the unconditional branch of classifier-free guidance both passes also carry the null label, so their
AdaLN conditioning is identical and only the mode token, acting through attention, tells them apart.
The conflict surfaces in the last blocks near t = 1, where the two passes must diverge into different
output spaces. We resolve it by conditioning decoding on its own learned embedding in place of the
timestep embedding, denoted as tdec = −1 for timestep outside the flow interval.

4.1
FULL UNIFICATION IMPROVES GENERATION AT MATCHED SIZE
Table 2 compares three ways of spending a fixed parameter budget: separate networks, a shared
generative encoder with a standalone decoder (UNITE), and one fully shared backbone (UNIFIED).
Both UNIFIED variants improve gFID over UNITE at every size. The gain is largest when capacity
is scarce (0.22 and 0.33 at Tiny and Small vs. 0.15 at Base), and Tiny UNIFIED nearly matches
Small UNITE with about half the parameters (2.78 at 62M vs. 2.76 at 117M). This suggests that the
shared backbone is more parameter-efficient than separate networks.
4.2
COMPARISON WITH PIXEL-SPACE AND LATENT DIFFUSION MODELS
Table 4 compares UNIFIED with published class-conditional generators of at most 250M parameters
on ImageNet-1K 256×256. Appendix D describes all baselines and extends the comparison to larger
models (Table 10). In every parameter range, UNIFIED attains the lowest gFID without relying on
a pretrained autoencoder or vision foundation model, although it is trained for only 200 epochs,
against 600 for the pixel-space baselines. Even UNIFIED-T (62M, 2.78) improves on every pixel-
space baseline of 131–164M (2.96–3.66), including PixelREPA-B/16 and PixelU-B/16, which align
to a pretrained vision encoder. At 100–150M, UNIFIED-S (116M) reaches 2.43, against 2.96 for the
best pixel-space model, JiT-B/16 with register guidance (RG). At 150–250M, UNIFIED-B (215M)
reaches 2.08 and the highest IS in the table (314.4), improving on the end-to-end latent model DSD
(3.35) and on two-stage REPA-B (2.15), which uses both a pretrained autoencoder and a vision
foundation model. In the full comparison, every model with a lower gFID than UNIFIED-B uses at
least 545M parameters.

Position alignment. UNIFIED removes this detour by placing each pixel patch, latent, and noisy
latent at the same sequence index. With the same timesteps as UNITE++ (tenc = 0, tdec = 1)
and the same fully shared backbone, the layout is the only change: it improves rFID from 2.20 to
1.39, but its gFID is worse (2.31). We attribute this to a mismatch between the encoder’s input
and its timestep. The timestep tells the backbone how much of its input is observed: t = 0 means
pure noise. In UNITE++, the encoder reads N(0, I) readout tokens, so tenc = 0 describes its input
correctly. In UNIFIED, the encoder instead reads clean image patches, yet tenc = 0 still labels them
as pure noise, so the backbone receives the same conditioning for a fully observed image as for the
pure-noise input at the start of generation.
Clean endpoint for encoding. Encoding and decoding are both deterministic maps from fully ob-
served inputs, so both naturally belong at the clean endpoint. Changing only the encoding timestep
to tenc = 1, with the layout and tdec = 1 fixed, improves gFID from 2.31 to 2.15 and rFID from
1.39 to 0.97, now better than UNITE on both.
CKA analysis. Following UNITE, we compare what the shared backbone computes when encoding
and when generating with layer-wise CKA (Figure 3). Section 3.3 predicts that position alignment
lets the encoding pass track the generation pass from the early blocks, and the CKA is consistent
with this: both UNIFIED variants reach 0.48–0.72 in blocks 1–5 at every t, whereas UNITE and
UNITE++ stay at or below 0.15. Their encoders start from N(0, I) readout tokens, which carry
no image information until attention writes it in, so they align only in the last blocks (0.84–0.92 for
UNITE). The one exception is the last block near the clean endpoint. At t = 0.995, its CKA drops in
both models that decode at t = 1, whatever their layout (0.37 for UNIFIED with tenc = tdec = 1 and

this to the output conflict at the shared endpoint: encoding and generation output the clean latent,
whereas decoding outputs pixel features, and near t = 1 the generation input is nearly identical to
the decoding input, so the last block must serve both output spaces. We use CKA only to locate
where the two passes diverge, while the effect of each design choice on generation is measured by
the ablations in Table 3.

Implementation details.
We extend UNIFIED to text-to-image generation by replacing the class-
embedding conditioner with a text-encoder conditioner. We use Qwen3-0.6B (Yang et al., 2025)
as the text encoder and take its final-layer hidden states as the text conditioning tokens. Following

and 10% text-condition dropout. All other settings, including the learning rate schedule, optimizer,
EMA decay, gradient clipping, and sampler, follow the ImageNet setup. Optionally, we fine-tune
the pretrained model on BLIP3o-60k for 2K steps.

CONCLUSION
We presented UNIFIED, a single diffusion transformer that encodes, generates, and decodes with
fully shared weights. We systematically study how to unify these modes, including partial versus
full weight sharing, sequence layout, and the timestep conditioning of each mode, and find that it
works best when the three modes are cast as position-aligned sequence maps and decoding receives
its own learned timestep. At matched parameters on ImageNet-1K, UNIFIED improves generation
over UNITE at every size. This also extends to text-to-image generation, where it is competitive with
substantially larger models on GenEval, DPG-Bench, and GenAI-Bench, and at 512 × 512 exceeds
single-stage pixel diffusion models with around one billion parameters. Overall, full unification of
latent diffusion is not merely feasible but beneficial.
